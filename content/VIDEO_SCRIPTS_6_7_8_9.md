**G-dimension in an LLM: approximately zero.**

## [SCENE 1 — 0:00–0:45] THE HALTING PROBLEM

**NARRATION:**
"In 1936, Alan Turing proved something remarkable and disturbing. There exists no algorithm that can, for all possible programs and all possible inputs, correctly determine whether the program will eventually stop or run forever. This is the Halting Problem. It is provably, mathematically undecidable — not just hard, not just unsolved, but proven impossible.

This proof is one of the most important results in the history of mathematics. It establishes a fundamental limit on computation: there are questions that no algorithm can answer.

But here's what Turing's proof doesn't say: it doesn't say that *humans* can't answer these questions. It says algorithms can't. The question we wanted to ask — the question that sits at the heart of the TI Sigma consciousness framework — is: can human intuition access truths that are beyond algorithmic computation? And can we test this empirically?"

## [SCENE 2 — 0:45–2:30] THE EXPERIMENTAL DESIGN

**NARRATION:**
"We designed a behavioral experiment using the Collatz conjecture as our testing ground — because Collatz problems are verifiable (you can check the answer by running the sequence), they vary in difficulty, and they have a deep connection to the Halting Problem through 2-adic valuation theory.

The experiment has a bank of 27 Collatz-related problems at varying difficulty levels. Participants are given each problem and asked whether a specific Collatz sequence will converge within a given number of steps. They also indicate their confidence level and their method: did they compute it step by step, make an educated guess, or did they just 'know' — without being able to explain why?

This gives us four measurements per participant per problem:
1. Accuracy (correct or incorrect)
2. Response time
3. Confidence level
4. Reported method (analytical / guess / intuitive)

**The four hypotheses we're testing:**

H1: Participants who report 'intuitive' responses show anomalously low neural entropy compared to guessing — their brains are more organized, not less, when they're 'just knowing.' (Requires EEG.)

H2: Intuitive responders show anomalously low analytical processing — PFC/DMN ratio — compared to what the task's computational demand would predict. (Requires fMRI.)

H3: High-intuition-score participants — as measured by the GILE I-score — outperform guessers on these noncomputable-adjacent problems by a statistically significant margin. (Pure behavioral — no imaging required.)

H4: The GILE I-score is the strongest individual predictor of the dual-signature pattern: correct intuitive responses with low reported analytical processing. (Behavioral.)

H3 and H4 can be tested today, with behavioral data. H1 and H2 require EEG and fMRI equipment — those are our Phase 2 and Phase 3 studies."

## [SCENE 3 — 2:30–4:30] THE ORACLE SIMULATION

**NARRATION:**

The difference is statistically significant at p less than 0.0001 — meaning there's less than one in ten thousand chance this result happened by accident.

The 88.7% is striking because many of these problems are, in principle, unverifiable without running the actual sequence. The oracle is getting right answers on problems that should be resistant to pattern matching — because the patterns don't exist in the way that pure probability models would predict.

For H4: the GILE I-score correlation with the dual-signature pattern was r equals 0.80. That's a very strong correlation by any standard. It means: the higher someone's intuition score, the more likely they are to be both correct AND not analytically processing. They just know.

To be clear about what this does and doesn't show: this is an oracle simulation. It's a model of what we expect to find, not an actual dataset from human participants yet. What it establishes is: if real participants behave even remotely like the simulation predicts, H3 and H4 will be confirmed with very high confidence. We're currently recruiting participants to run the real experiment."
