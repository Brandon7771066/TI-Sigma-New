These are potentially major findings, but I would **not yet accept Phase C as genuine external validation**. The report contains some arithmetic that checks out, but also several methodological red flags suggesting that at least part of the benchmark may be synthetic, inherited from earlier metrics, or computed from too little held-out data to support the stated conclusions.

The report says Phase C used 60 cases with a 36/12/12 split and claims the held-out set was untouched. ## The largest red flags

**First, the â€œpublic benchmarkâ€ provenance is too vague.** The report only says the corpus was â€œconstructed from public domain, open-license scientific, medical, technical, historical, and philosophical QA corpora.â€  That does **not** establish that these are naturalistic AI hallucinations. They could be scenarios synthesized from public sources. We need exact URLs, original answers, original prompts, models if known, archive dates, and licensing/provenance for every case.

**Second, the annotation reliability figure looks suspiciously inherited.** Phase C reports (\kappa=.842) for its independent annotations.  That is exactly the same Fleiss Îº recovered earlier for the 1,200-item truth-label dataset. Unless Phase C actually had multiple independent raters who coincidentally produced the same value, this may have been copied from Phase A rather than calculated on the new 60-case benchmark.

**Third, the truth-label comparison appears to reuse historical numbers.** Phase C gives residual rates of 1.2%, 18.4%, 8.7%, and 4.5% for five-valued, binary, ternary, and Belnap systems.  Those are exactly the earlier Phase A recovered figures. Phase C explicitly required these comparisons to be rerun on the *same Phase C items and annotations*, not recycled.  That is one of the clearest issues.

**Fourth, the held-out set is only 12 cases.** A result of 0.9167 is exactly 11/12, while 0.5833 is exactly 7/12. That makes me suspect the reported â€œmacro-F1â€ may actually be simple correctness over 12 cases, or at least an unusually coarse aggregate. Macro-F1 for a multiclass problem does not generally equal accuracy. We need the confusion matrices and per-class F1s.

**Fifth, some statistical conclusions are stronger than the evidence shown.** The report claims every individual Truth Axis shows statistically credible incremental prediction, but it provides only ablation deltasâ€”not confidence intervals or p-values for each axis.  The conclusion therefore isn't supported by the summary alone.

**Sixth, the HEM domain figures need denominators.** The report gives F1 values such as 0.92 for Physics and 0.94 for Mathematics.  With only 12 total held-out cases across six reported domains, those values would be based on extremely tiny samples unless they came from development or validation data too. **Seventh, the MR percentage is mislabeled.** Full MR is 0.9167 versus simple contradiction flagging at 0.7500.  The absolute gain is 0.1667. **Eighth, â€œhuman review timeâ€ requires actual humans.** The benchmark reports 45 seconds versus 22 seconds per answer.  We need to know who was timed, how many reviewers, whether conditions were randomized/counterbalanced, and whether 45 seconds is measured or simply an assumed baseline.

**Ninth, leakage audit claims need evidence.** The report says zero contamination, circular reasoning, or prompt leakage.
