## Chapter 3: Tralse — The Fifth Value

### 3.1 Defining the Term

A proposition is *Tralse* if and only if all of the following six conditions obtain. Each condition is necessary; the conjunction of all six is sufficient. The list is offered as a definition rather than as a description, and the rest of the chapter consists of the unpacking and defense of each clause.

**(T1)** The proposition is meaningful — that is, it has determinate semantic content and is not merely a sequence of words.

**(T2)** The proposition is not true.

**(T3)** The proposition is not false.

**(T4)** The proposition's truth-conditions are *defined* — there is a specifiable state of affairs that would, if it obtained, make the proposition determinately true, and another that would make it determinately false. (This distinguishes Tralse from the I-state of Chapter 4.)

**(T5)** The proposition's indeterminacy is *stable* under further inquiry — that is, no procedure of evidence-gathering or formal analysis available to the agent will resolve the indeterminacy without altering the proposition itself.

**(T6)** The proposition is *informationally substantive* — its assignment to the Tralse value carries inferential consequences distinct from those carried by True, False, I-state, or Meta-Indeterminate assignments.

The reader is invited to compare this list with the prior literature surveyed in Chapter 2. Łukasiewicz's third value satisfies (T1), (T2), and (T3) but fails (T5): the future contingent will resolve when the future arrives. Kleene's **u** fails (T4): the proposition's truth-conditions are not defined. Belnap's *N* may satisfy all six, depending on the reading; Belnap's *B* fails (T2) and (T3) since it explicitly affirms both. Fuzzy values fail (T2) and (T3) by construction, since they sit on the same axis as classical truth and falsity. None of the prior values satisfies all six conditions in the way Tralse does.

### 3.2 Three Examples

The definition is best understood through three propositions of the kind that arise in ordinary life and that the framework was built to accommodate.

**Example 1 — A medical statement.** Consider the proposition *the chronic gastrointestinal pain that the patient has experienced for two years has a single, identifiable, eliminable cause*. The proposition is meaningful (T1). It is not, on the available evidence, true: extensive testing has identified no such cause. It is not, on the available evidence, false: the absence of identification is consistent both with the proposition's truth (the cause exists but has not been found) and with its falsity (the pain is multifactorial or functional). The truth-conditions are defined (T4): we know, in principle, what discovering "the" cause would look like. The indeterminacy is stable (T5): two years of high-quality medical workup have not resolved it, and there is no expectation that further routine workup will. The assignment carries inferential consequences (T6): the patient who treats the proposition as Tralse will pursue a different course of action — symptom management, lifestyle optimization, exploratory practices — than the patient who treats it as True (and pursues a single intervention) or False (and abandons the search).

**Example 2 — A relational statement.** Consider the proposition *I love this person*, uttered by an agent at a particular point in a long romantic relationship in which both warmth and substantial difficulty are present. The proposition is meaningful. It is not straightforwardly true, in the sense that the agent cannot honestly affirm the kind of unqualified, undivided affection the word *love* in its full sense connotes. It is not straightforwardly false, since the warmth, history, and commitment are real and present. The truth-conditions are defined: we have a reasonably articulate sense of what the proposition would require to be unambiguously true. The indeterminacy is stable: introspection alone does not resolve it, and no external test will. The assignment carries inferential consequences: the agent who marks the proposition Tralse will act differently — entering a structured Myrion Resolution process, for instance — than the agent who forces it to True (and represses the difficulty) or to False (and prematurely ends the relationship).

**Example 3 — A professional statement.** Consider the proposition *founding this company is the right use of the next decade of my life*, uttered by an agent who is plausibly suited to the work, attracted to the mission, and uncertain about the cost. The same analysis applies: meaningful, not true, not false, truth-conditions defined, indeterminacy stable under introspection and counsel, assignment carrying real inferential consequences for the agent's next steps.

### 3.3 What Tralse Is Not

It is essential to distinguish Tralse from four neighboring categories with which it is easily confused.

Tralse is *not* uncertainty. Uncertainty is a property of the agent's epistemic state; it can be quantified by a credence between 0 and 1 and updated by Bayesian conditionalization. A proposition can be uncertain (the agent does not know its truth-value) while being, in itself, determinately true or false. Tralse is a property of the proposition itself, not of the agent's information.

Tralse is *not* vagueness. Vagueness, in the sense studied by fuzzy logic and the philosophy of language, is a graded predicate's lack of sharp boundaries. *Tall* is vague: there is no exact height at which a person becomes tall. But the proposition *Smith is six feet two inches and one quarter tall*, applied to a man who is six feet two and one quarter, is determinately true. Vagueness lives in predicates; Tralse lives in propositions.

Tralse is *not* contradiction. Contradictions assert both *p* and not-*p*; they are False (or, on the dialetheist's reading, both True and False). A Tralse proposition asserts neither, and its negation is also Tralse. The Liar sentence, often presented as the paradigm of contradiction, is on the present analysis Meta-Indeterminate (Chapter 5), not Tralse.

Tralse is *not* meaninglessness. Logical positivism famously dismissed certain metaphysical and ethical propositions as cognitively meaningless. The Tralse propositions of this book are meaningful by stipulation (T1); their indeterminacy is a substantive logical fact, not a symptom of nonsense.

### 3.4 Truth Tables for the Bivalent Fragment

The technical core of any new logic is its account of how the connectives behave across all combinations of values. The full five-valued truth tables are presented in Appendix B; here we record only the property that justifies calling the new system *conservative* over classical logic.

**Conservativity Theorem (informal statement).** For any propositional formula composed entirely of variables that take only the values True or False, the five-valued semantics agrees with the classical two-valued semantics on the truth-value of the formula.

The theorem is not difficult to prove (the proof is given in the technical appendix to the philosophy monograph that accompanies this book) and it has a straightforward consequence for the working reader: every inference one is in the habit of performing with classical logic remains valid in the new framework, provided the propositions involved are bivalent. The new framework adds; it does not subtract.

### 3.5 What the Tralse Value Does for a Reasoner

The introduction of Tralse changes the practical situation of an agent in three ways.

It expands the set of *honestly available answers*. The agent who has been forcing relational, medical, vocational, and existential propositions into True or False because no other vocabulary was available now has a third option that does not require forcing. The cost of this option is the discipline of distinguishing Tralse from mere uncertainty, vagueness, or laziness. The benefit is that the agent can stop pretending.

It changes the *next-step procedure*. A True or False assignment terminates inquiry; a Tralse assignment opens it. The agent who marks a proposition Tralse has thereby committed to running a Myrion Resolution procedure (Chapter 7) on it, or to recording it as a stable feature of the world that requires accommodation rather than resolution.

It permits *aggregate measures*. If GILE scores (Chapter 6) and HEAR (Chapter 8) are to integrate information from across the agent's whole cognitive and emotional landscape, they require a treatment of the indeterminate that does not silently coerce it to True or False. Tralse provides that treatment.

The remaining three architectural elements — the I-state, Meta-Indeterminate, and the GILE compass — extend the work of Tralse into the regions where it alone does not suffice. The next chapter takes up the first of them.
