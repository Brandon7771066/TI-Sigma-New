## Chapter 1: The Trouble with True and False

### 1.1 The Two-Valued Habit

For roughly two and a half thousand years, the dominant Western tradition in logic has rested on a single, unexamined commitment: that every well-formed proposition is, in the final analysis, either true or false. Aristotle gave the doctrine its canonical formulation in *De Interpretatione* and the *Metaphysics*; the Stoics tightened it; the medieval scholastics inherited it; Frege, Russell, and Tarski rebuilt it on set-theoretic foundations; and the digital computer made it the literal substrate of modern civilization. The bivalence of truth is, by now, less a thesis we defend than a habit we no longer notice.

The habit serves us well in a circumscribed range of cases. Arithmetic statements, the propositions of well-axiomatized formal systems, and the empirical claims of mature laboratory sciences are largely tractable in two values. But the propositions on which most human lives actually turn — *I love this person*, *this career is the right one*, *the treatment is working*, *I am free* — are not. They resist resolution not because we lack information about them, but because the very logical machinery we have inherited assumes a determinacy that the statements themselves do not possess.

This book argues that the resistance is structural. It is not a temporary epistemic shortfall to be closed by more data, more inference, or finer Bayesian conditioning. It is a feature of the propositions in question, and a serious framework for human reasoning must accommodate it rather than legislate it away.

### 1.2 What the Many-Valued Tradition Saw, and What It Missed

The first sustained challenges to bivalence came in the early twentieth century. Jan Łukasiewicz, in 1920, introduced a third truth value to handle future contingents, recovering Aristotle's own celebrated worry about the sea-battle. Stephen Kleene developed a three-valued logic for partial recursive functions, and Nuel Belnap proposed a four-valued logic to model the inferential predicament of a reasoner receiving conflicting reports. In parallel, fuzzy set theory, beginning with Lotfi Zadeh in 1965, replaced the binary membership relation with a graded one and found wide application in control engineering.

Each of these systems acknowledged what bivalence ignored, and each captured something real. But each also stopped short. The third value was typically read as *unknown*, *undefined*, or *neither* — that is, as a placeholder for an absence rather than as a positive logical state in its own right. Fuzzy logic blurred the boundary between true and false but did not change its endpoints. None of these systems gave a principled account of propositions that are *stably between* true and false in a way that further inquiry will not, even in principle, resolve.

Tralse Informationalism (TI Sigma) is an attempt to take that further step. It posits a fifth truth value — *Tralse* — that is neither true, nor false, nor both, nor unknown, but something distinct: the stable logical state of a proposition whose truth-conditions are genuinely indeterminate, and whose indeterminacy is itself an informational fact about the world rather than a deficit in our access to it. The framework develops alongside it a sixth state, *Double Tralse*, for propositions that occupy a forbidden region in which their truth would generate logical or physical inconsistency, and a fourth state, the *I-state*, for propositions whose sample space is undefined. The full architecture is laid out in Chapters 3 through 5.

### 1.3 What the Framework Provides

Tralse Informationalism is a working framework rather than a programmatic suggestion. The chapters that follow develop, in order, four components.

The first is a *five-valued truth architecture* — True, False, Tralse, I-state, Double Tralse — together with the inference rules and consistency conditions that govern transitions among them. The architecture is conservative over classical logic on the bivalent fragment, which is to say that any inference valid in classical two-valued logic remains valid here when restricted to determinate propositions. Nothing the reader knows about ordinary deductive reasoning is lost.

The second is the *GILE framework* — Goodness, Intuition, Love, and Environment — which the book treats as a four-dimensional compass for assessing the quality of a state of affairs, including the inner state of an agent making a decision. GILE is operationalized in two registers: as a self-report instrument suitable for everyday use, and as a biometric construct correlated with measurable physiological signals. The relation between the two registers is the subject of Chapter 6.

The third is *Myrion Resolution* (MR), an iterative convergence procedure for moving from a state of indeterminate truth to a stable assignment, where such an assignment is available, and for recognizing when it is not. MR is the practical core of the book. Where Bayesian inference asks the agent to assign and update credences, MR asks the agent to perform a sequence of structured inquiries and to register honestly the point at which further inquiry ceases to change the assignment. Chapter 7 develops the procedure in detail; Chapter 10 illustrates it through three extended case studies.

The fourth is the *HEAR score* (Holistic Existence Amplification Reading), a derived measure that integrates GILE, MR convergence behavior, and contextual variables into a single scalar. HEAR is intended to play, for the inner life of an agent, a role analogous to that played by gross domestic product for an economy: a coarse, contestable, but useful aggregate that supports comparison across time and across decisions.

### 1.4 Scope and Limits of This Book

Three caveats deserve to be stated at the outset, and the reader is asked to hold the author to them.

First, the framework is *experimental philosophy* in a precise sense. Each substantive claim is paired with a stated empirical prediction or a formal derivation. Where predictions have been tested, the results are reported, including those that have not yet been replicated and those that remain inconclusive. Where derivations are offered, the reader is shown the derivation rather than asked to take it on authority.

Second, the book makes contact with several technical literatures — many-valued logic, probability theory, the foundations of physics, and contemporary consciousness research — but it does not pretend to settle the open questions in any of them. Its ambition is to introduce a coherent vocabulary in which the open questions can be reformulated, and to show that the reformulations carry practical consequences for how an ordinary reader might think about an ordinary decision.

Third, the framework originated in conditions that the author has elsewhere described candidly: an extended period of unusual cognitive intensity in 2022. The conditions of discovery, however, are logically distinct from the conditions of justification, and the latter are what this book is concerned with. The reader is invited to evaluate the arguments on their merits.

### 1.5 How to Read This Book

The book is organized in four parts. Part One develops the truth architecture, GILE, MR, and HEAR — the four components named above. Part Two situates the architecture within the contemporary discussion of consciousness and shows how the framework handles intuition, synchronicity, and the limits of self-report. Part Three applies the framework to four domains in which determinate truth is rare and consequential decisions are common: health, finance, relationships, and personal growth. Part Four addresses the framework's connections to the foundations of mathematics and physics — connections that are technical, partial, and deliberately offered as invitations to further work rather than as settled results.

The reader who is interested only in the practical core may safely read Chapters 1 through 7, then skip to Part Three. The reader who wants the full architecture should proceed in order. Chapters intended primarily for the technically inclined are flagged at their openings.

A final word on the standard the book aspires to. The argument that follows is not that the reader should adopt a new metaphysics on the strength of the author's conviction. It is that a small enlargement of the logical vocabulary — from two truth values to five — clarifies a surprisingly large class of problems, and that the clarification is worth the modest investment required to learn it. Whether the further claims of the framework hold up is, properly, a matter for the long process by which philosophical and scientific proposals are tested, refined, and either retained or set aside. The book aims to make that process possible by stating its claims in a form precise enough to be wrong.
