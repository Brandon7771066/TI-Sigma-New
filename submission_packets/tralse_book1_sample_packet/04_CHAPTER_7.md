## Chapter 7: Myrion Resolution — How to Decide When Truth Won't Settle

### 7.1 What MR Is, and What It Replaces

Myrion Resolution is the framework's iterative convergence procedure for decision-making under genuine indeterminacy. It is the practical instrument that connects the abstract truth architecture of Chapters 3–5 to the lived choices of an agent. The chapter develops the procedure in detail and illustrates it with three full worked examples; it is the longest chapter in the book and, in the author's judgment, the one that does the most work for the working reader.

To understand what MR is, it is useful to begin with what it is not. MR is not a Bayesian decision procedure. Bayesian decision theory, in its standard form, asks the agent to assign credences to propositions and utilities to outcomes, and then to choose the action that maximizes expected utility. The procedure presupposes that credences and utilities are available; for the propositions we have called Tralse, I-state, and Double Tralse, they are not. MR is the framework's response to this.

MR is also not deliberation in the classical philosophical sense — a process of weighing reasons until one set of reasons preponderates over another. Classical deliberation presupposes that the reasons themselves are commensurable; for many of the most important decisions, they are not. MR is structured to make the incommensurability visible rather than to paper over it.

MR is, at its heart, an iterative *check-in* with the four GILE dimensions, conducted under conditions of attention and honesty, until the check-ins stabilize. The stability of the check-ins — not the certainty of the conclusion, not the elimination of doubt, not the disappearance of the indeterminacy — is the criterion of resolution. The procedure terminates when the agent has, on successive iterations, given the same evaluation of the state.

### 7.2 The Six Steps of an MR Cycle

A single MR cycle consists of six steps, each of which is described below. The cycle is repeated until convergence, with intervals between cycles long enough to permit the agent's pre-reflective processes to operate (typically several hours to several days, depending on the consequence of the decision).

**Step 1 — Statement.** The agent writes down, in a single declarative sentence, the proposition under consideration. The discipline of writing forces precision; the discipline of a single sentence forces economy. The proposition should be one that, on inspection, is plausibly Tralse — that is, one whose truth-value is not yet determinate but whose truth-conditions are well enough defined for evaluation. Propositions in the I-state should be moved to the constructive work of §4.4 before being entered into MR; propositions in the Double Tralse zone should be moved to the structural reconfiguration of §5.5.

**Step 2 — GILE assessment.** The agent assigns scores in [0, 1] to each of G, I, L, and E for the *state of affairs that would result if the proposition were treated as True* — that is, if the agent acted as though it were settled. The scores are recorded, with brief notes on the reasoning behind each. The assessment is then repeated for the *state of affairs that would result if the proposition were treated as False*, again with notes.

**Step 3 — Difference computation.** The agent computes the GILE composite for each of the two states, using the weights specified in §6.6 (or the agent's justified re-weighting), and notes the difference. A difference of less than 0.1 indicates that the choice is, by the framework's standards, *underdetermined* by the agent's current evaluative state, and the agent should consider whether the proposition is genuinely Tralse or whether further inquiry would be productive. A difference of 0.1–0.3 indicates a *moderate* preference; 0.3–0.5 a *strong* preference; greater than 0.5 a *decisive* preference.

**Step 4 — Pre-reflective check.** Independently of the explicit GILE computation, the agent records their intuitive response to the question, *which of the two states do I, on reflection but without further calculation, prefer?* The answer is recorded as a single word — "True," "False," or "Neither" — together with a brief description of any felt-sense response (tightness, openness, warmth, weariness) accompanying the answer.

**Step 5 — Reconciliation.** The agent compares the explicit GILE difference (Step 3) with the pre-reflective check (Step 4). When the two converge, the cycle has produced a coherent reading and the agent records the result as a tentative resolution. When the two diverge, the cycle has produced an *informational signal* — typically that the explicit computation has missed something the pre-reflective response detected, or vice versa — and the agent records the divergence for examination in the next cycle.

**Step 6 — Interval.** The agent sets the cycle aside for an interval determined by the consequence of the decision. For decisions of small consequence, an hour may suffice; for decisions of large consequence, a week or a month is more appropriate. During the interval, the agent does not actively deliberate on the proposition; the interval is for the operation of the pre-reflective processes that explicit deliberation does not access.

The cycle is then repeated. Convergence is declared when two successive cycles produce the same resolution and the same direction of preference, with GILE differences within 0.1 of each other.

### 7.3 Three Worked Examples

**Example A — A medical decision.** A patient has lived for two years with a chronic gastrointestinal condition that has resisted diagnosis and treatment. A specialist now offers an experimental protocol that has shown promise in a small clinical series but is not yet established. The proposition under MR is *I should enroll in the experimental protocol*.

In the first cycle, the patient's GILE assessment of the True state (enrolled) returns G = 0.6 (alignment with the patient's value of taking informed risks for relief), I = 0.5 (an uncertain intuitive reading, weighted toward openness), L = 0.4 (the protocol requires travel that disrupts a fragile relational support system), E = 0.5 (the patient's daily environment is moderately supportive but the protocol environment is unfamiliar). The composite is 0.30 · 0.6 + 0.25 · 0.5 + 0.25 · 0.4 + 0.20 · 0.5 = 0.505. The False state (not enrolled) returns G = 0.4, I = 0.5, L = 0.6, E = 0.6, composite 0.510. The difference is 0.005 — well within the underdetermined range. The pre-reflective check returns "Neither, but with a faint pull toward enrollment." The reconciliation records the underdetermination and the slight intuitive lean.

In the second cycle, conducted a week later after the patient has discussed the protocol with the relational support system and clarified that travel arrangements can be made compatibly, the L score for the True state rises to 0.55. The True composite is now 0.54, the False composite is 0.51, and the pre-reflective check has firmed to "Yes." Convergence is declared in the third cycle, which produces the same direction and a slightly larger differential. The patient enrolls.

**Example B — A relational decision.** An agent in a long-term romantic relationship is considering whether to remain in the relationship through a period of substantial difficulty. The proposition under MR is *I should remain in this relationship for the next year*.

The first cycle's GILE assessment of the True state (remain) yields G = 0.5 (mixed alignment with the agent's values around commitment and around individual flourishing), I = 0.7 (a strong intuitive sense of unfinished work), L = 0.6 (the connection is real but constrained), E = 0.4 (the agent's living situation is destabilized by the difficulty). The composite is 0.55. The False state (depart) yields G = 0.5, I = 0.4, L = 0.3, E = 0.7, composite 0.475. The difference is 0.075, on the edge of moderate preference toward remaining. The pre-reflective check returns "Remain, with discomfort."

The second cycle, three weeks later, returns nearly identical scores. The third cycle, two months later, after some material change in the relationship, returns G = 0.55, I = 0.7, L = 0.65, E = 0.5, composite 0.605 for True; G = 0.5, I = 0.4, L = 0.3, E = 0.7, composite 0.485 for False. The difference has widened to 0.12, the pre-reflective check has firmed to "Remain, with diminishing discomfort," and convergence is declared. The agent remains.

**Example C — A vocational decision.** An agent considering whether to leave salaried employment to found a company. The proposition is *I should resign from my current position by the end of the calendar quarter and devote the next two years to founding the company*.

The first cycle's True state (resign and found) yields G = 0.7 (strong alignment with the agent's value of building things that matter), I = 0.8 (a clear and persistent intuitive pull), L = 0.4 (founding strains existing relationships and demands deferral of family-formation considerations), E = 0.3 (the financial environment for the first two years will be substantially destabilized). The composite is 0.575. The False state (remain salaried) yields G = 0.4, I = 0.4, L = 0.6, E = 0.7, composite 0.51. The difference is 0.065. The pre-reflective check is "Resign, but with significant fear."

The second cycle, after the agent has discussed the decision with their financial dependents and obtained explicit support for an eighteen-month runway, returns L = 0.55 and E = 0.45 for the True state, raising its composite to 0.6275. The False composite is unchanged. The difference has widened to 0.12, the pre-reflective check is "Resign, with the fear in proportion," and convergence is declared in the third cycle. The agent resigns.

### 7.4 What MR Does Not Promise

The procedure does not promise to make the agent's decisions easy. It does not promise to eliminate the felt difficulty of consequential choice; in many cases, it makes the difficulty more visible and more articulate. It does not promise that the resulting decisions will turn out well. It promises only that the agent will have made the decision in a way that is *honest about the structure of the situation*: that has not forced an indeterminate proposition into a determinate value, that has not let the explicit computation override the pre-reflective response without examination, that has not made the decision under conditions (low E, single cycle, no interval) that the framework identifies as adverse to good decisions.

The agent who decides by MR is not guaranteed wisdom. The agent is given a procedure that, used over time, builds the practical skill of deciding under indeterminacy — a skill that the bivalent inheritance of Western thought has neither named nor cultivated, and that this book argues is, in the end, the most important skill an adult human acquires.

### 7.5 The Failure Modes of MR

The procedure has three characteristic failure modes, which the agent should recognize and guard against.

The first is *premature convergence*: declaring resolution after a single cycle, before the pre-reflective processes have had time to operate. The remedy is the discipline of the interval (Step 6), which the agent must respect even when the explicit computation has produced a clear result.

The second is *paralytic recursion*: continuing to cycle indefinitely, without convergence, in the hope of reaching certainty. The remedy is the recognition that some propositions are stably Tralse — that further cycling will not produce convergence — and the application of the procedure described in §7.6 below for acting under stable indeterminacy.

The third is *bad-faith MR*: cycling not to converge but to defer a decision the agent has, in fact, already made and is reluctant to act on. The remedy is the pre-reflective check (Step 4), conducted with sufficient honesty that the agent's actual lean is visible to themselves. The check is a protection against the procedure's misuse; it is not optional.

### 7.6 Acting Under Stable Indeterminacy

When repeated MR cycles fail to converge — when the proposition is genuinely Tralse in a way that no further cycling will resolve — the agent must still act. The framework's procedure for action under stable indeterminacy is the *Permissibility Distribution* of Chapter 9, which does not require convergence and which produces, in place of a single decision, a *set of permissible actions* from which the agent may choose by other means (random selection, by-counsel-of-others, by-attendance-to-the-pre-reflective). The full apparatus is the subject of Chapter 9.

For now, it is enough to record that MR's failure to converge is itself an informational outcome — it tells the agent what kind of proposition they are facing — and that the framework has resources beyond MR for the cases where MR alone does not suffice.
