# MIM Revision: Consciousness as the Highest Form of Cognition — Meta-Metacognition, Vertical Agency, and the Free-Will Veto

**Brandon Emerick**
**February 2026**

---

## Abstract

The Meta-Information Model (MIM) of consciousness holds that consciousness is information about information -- meta-information processing that distinguishes aware systems from merely reactive ones. This paper revises and extends MIM by arguing that consciousness is not a separate category from cognition but rather the *highest form* of cognition itself. Where traditional cognitive science partitions mind into the modular triad of Affect, Behavior, and Cognition (the ABC model), we propose a vertical architecture in which these elements are layered rather than laterally separated. The revision introduces a four-level Vertical Agency Model: (1) subpersonal processes, (2) default free will at the agentic core, (3) meta-information monitoring via Meta-Relations, and (4) meta-executive consciousness operating as a free-will veto. The model resolves longstanding confusions in the free-will literature -- particularly the misinterpretation of Libet-style experiments -- by distinguishing between the *rarity* of meta-executive override and the *absence* of freedom at lower levels. The paper formalizes the distinction between boundary states and within-consciousness states, introduces the concept of Meta-Relations as vertically stratified evaluative states, maps the four-level model onto known neurocognitive structures, and integrates the entire framework with tralse logic from the Tralse-Informational (TI) epistemology. The central philosophical claim is that consciousness is not the origin of action but the arbiter of actions, and that free will exists at multiple vertical levels, with the highest level existing precisely so it can remain mostly silent.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 The Meta-Information Model and Its Original Formulation

The Meta-Information Model (MIM) was proposed as a framework for understanding consciousness as fundamentally informational. On MIM's original account, consciousness arises when a system processes not merely information about the external world but *information about its own information* -- meta-information. A thermostat processes temperature data; a conscious agent processes data about its own processing of temperature data. The distinction between first-order information and meta-information was taken to mark the boundary between non-conscious and conscious systems.

This formulation was productive. It explained why consciousness is associated with self-monitoring, introspection, and metacognition. It aligned with empirical findings in neuroscience showing that prefrontal and medial cortical regions -- areas associated with self-referential processing -- are reliably activated during conscious experience. And it connected naturally to the broader Tralse-Informational (TI) framework, in which information, not matter or energy, is ontologically fundamental.

### 1.2 The Limitation: Consciousness as a Separate Category

However, the original MIM harbored an unexamined assumption: that consciousness is categorically *different* from cognition. On the standard reading, cognition processes information, and consciousness is something additional -- a layer on top of, or qualitatively distinct from, ordinary cognitive processing. This assumption mirrors the broader tendency in philosophy of mind to treat consciousness as a "hard problem" (Chalmers, 1995) that cannot be resolved by describing cognitive mechanisms, no matter how completely.

The present paper challenges this assumption. The revision proposed here holds that consciousness is not a separate category from cognition but the *highest form* of cognition. Meta-metacognition -- the capacity to have feelings about one's own meta-evaluations, to monitor one's monitoring, to override one's overrides -- is what we experience as consciousness. The "hard problem" dissolves not because qualia are explained away but because the hierarchy of cognition, when extended vertically far enough, *becomes* qualitative experience. Feelings are not additions to cognition; they are what cognition feels like from the inside when it operates on itself at sufficiently high levels.

### 1.3 Overview of the Revision

This paper proceeds as follows. Section 2 presents the four-level Vertical Agency Model, which replaces the flat MIM hierarchy with a vertically structured account of agency. Section 3 analyzes the free-will veto -- the meta-executive capacity to cancel or redirect actions generated at lower levels -- and shows how it resolves confusions in the Libet-era free-will debate. Section 4 introduces the distinction between boundary states and within-consciousness states. Section 5 reframes psychology's ABC model as vertically layered rather than horizontally modular. Section 6 develops the concept of Meta-Relations. Section 7 maps the four-level model onto neurocognitive structures. Section 8 integrates the framework with TI and tralse logic. Section 9 addresses objections. Section 10 concludes.

## 2. The Four-Level Vertical Agency Model

### 2.1 From Flat to Vertical

The original MIM treated meta-information as a single additional layer above first-order information. This two-level picture -- information and meta-information -- was useful but insufficiently fine-grained. Human agency is not a simple stack of two layers. It is a hierarchy of at least four functionally distinct levels, each with its own processing logic, temporal dynamics, and relationship to action.

The Vertical Agency Model (VAM) proposed here distinguishes four levels of agentive processing.

### 2.2 Level 1: Subpersonal Processes

The lowest level comprises processes that operate below the threshold of personal-level awareness. These include motor preparation (the priming of muscle groups before voluntary action), affective impulses (the automatic surge of fear upon seeing a snake), perceptual salience (the involuntary capture of attention by a loud noise), and autonomic regulation (heartbeat, respiration, homeostasis).

Level 1 processes are fast, automatic, and evolutionarily ancient. They generate the raw material on which higher levels operate. Crucially, Level 1 processes are not "unfree" in the sense of being externally determined; they are simply *pre-agentive*. They are the substrate from which agency emerges, not a constraint upon it.

### 2.3 Level 2: Default Free Will (The Agentic Core)

Level 2 is the locus of what this paper terms *default free will*. This is the ordinary, moment-to-moment operation of human agency: the generation of desires, intentions, and choices through a process that is genuinely indeterminate but systematically biased by prior experience, personality, and context.

Key properties of Level 2 processing:

**(a) Indeterminate but biased.** Level 2 does not operate deterministically. Given the same inputs, the same agent may generate different candidate actions at different times. However, the space of candidates is not uniform -- it is shaped by habit, preference, and history. An agent who has practiced violin for twenty years will generate musical responses more readily than a non-musician, but which specific musical response emerges on a given occasion is not fixed in advance.

**(b) Habitual but not deterministic.** Habits bias action selection without determining it. The habitual coffee drinker reaches for coffee most mornings but is not compelled to do so. The bias is real; the compulsion is not.

**(c) Generates candidate actions.** Level 2 produces a field of possible actions -- a set of candidates among which higher levels may select or which, in the absence of higher-level intervention, proceed to execution by default. Most actions are Level 2 actions: they are generated, selected by default biases, and executed without meta-level review.

**(d) Is genuinely free.** This is a crucial point that the free-will literature has systematically obscured. Level 2 action is *free*. It is not externally determined, not mechanistically necessitated, and not merely reactive. It is indeterminate, agent-generated, and responsive to reasons (even if not reflectively so). The fact that Level 2 freedom is not *reflective* does not make it unfree. It makes it unreflective.

### 2.4 Level 3: Meta-Information / The MIM Layer

Level 3 is the domain of meta-information proper -- the level at which the system monitors, evaluates, and represents its own Level 2 processing. This is where the original MIM located consciousness. The present revision retains this level but situates it within a richer hierarchy.

Level 3 generates what this paper terms *Meta-Relations* (MRs) -- structured evaluative states that relate first-order states to meta-level assessments. Examples include:

- Confidence about an emotion: "I feel angry, and I'm fairly sure this anger is justified."
- Doubt about a belief: "I believe the project will succeed, but I have reservations about that belief."
- Monitoring without cancellation: "I notice that I'm procrastinating, but I'm not going to intervene right now."

Level 3 monitoring is continuous, relatively low-effort, and often operates in the background. It does not necessarily lead to action modification. One can be aware of one's anxiety without doing anything about it. Level 3 creates the *representational space* in which meta-executive decisions become possible, but it does not itself make those decisions.

### 2.5 Level 4: Meta-Executive Consciousness (The Free-Will Veto)

Level 4 is the highest level of the vertical agency architecture. It is the locus of what this paper calls the *free-will veto*: the capacity to cancel, redirect, or override actions generated at Level 2 and monitored at Level 3.

Key properties of Level 4 processing:

**(a) Cancels or redirects actions.** Level 4 does not generate actions *de novo*. It operates on the candidate actions produced by Level 2, using the meta-information supplied by Level 3, to veto or modify actions that conflict with higher-order values, goals, or assessments.

**(b) Weighs meta-values.** Level 4 evaluates actions against meta-level criteria: error risk, moral significance, long-term consequences, coherence with self-concept. These are not first-order desires but second-order evaluations of desires.

**(c) Rare, effortful, and event-triggered.** Level 4 does not operate continuously. It is activated by specific triggers: detection of error, perception of high stakes, conflict between competing Level 2 candidates, or explicit self-interrogation. Most of the time, Level 4 is silent. Actions proceed from Level 2 through Level 3 monitoring without Level 4 intervention.

**(d) Subjectively felt as "I stopped myself."** The phenomenology of Level 4 is distinctive. It is experienced as effortful self-control, as the conscious override of impulse, as the moment of saying "no" to a desire that was already in progress. This phenomenology is what gives rise to the folk notion of "willpower."

## 3. The Free-Will Veto

### 3.1 Defining the Veto

The free-will veto is defined as a freely willed modification of an otherwise default agentive process. It is not the initiation of action but the *arbitration* of action -- the capacity to intervene in a process that would otherwise proceed automatically (though not deterministically) from Level 2.

The veto presupposes that Level 2 has already generated a candidate action and that Level 3 has registered meta-information about that action (e.g., its riskiness, its moral status, its consistency with long-term goals). The veto is the decision, at Level 4, to cancel or redirect the candidate.

### 3.2 Resolving the Libet Confusion

Benjamin Libet's (1983) famous experiments showed that the brain's readiness potential (RP) -- a neural signature of motor preparation -- precedes the subject's conscious awareness of the intention to act by approximately 350 milliseconds. This finding was widely interpreted as evidence against free will: if the brain "decides" before consciousness kicks in, then consciousness is epiphenomenal and free will is an illusion.

The Vertical Agency Model dissolves this confusion entirely. Libet's RP is a Level 1 phenomenon -- subpersonal motor preparation. The subject's reported "awareness of intention" is a Level 3 phenomenon -- meta-information about the candidate action. The supposed gap between RP and awareness is not a gap between determinism and freedom; it is a gap between two different *levels* of the vertical hierarchy.

Critically, Libet himself noted that subjects retained the ability to *veto* the action even after the RP had begun. This veto is a Level 4 phenomenon. Libet's own data thus supports the Vertical Agency Model: Level 1 prepares, Level 2 generates candidates, Level 3 monitors, and Level 4 vetoes. The experimental setup, by asking subjects to report the moment of "conscious intention," conflated Level 2 generation with Level 3 monitoring and missed Level 4 entirely.

### 3.3 The Rarity Objection and Its Dissolution

A common objection to free will is that meta-executive override is *rare*. Most of the time, humans act on impulse, habit, or default without conscious deliberation. If free will only shows up occasionally, the objection runs, then most human action is unfree.

The Vertical Agency Model shows why this objection is confused. Level 2 action is *already free*. It is indeterminate, agent-generated, and not externally compelled. The rarity of Level 4 intervention does not mean that Level 2 action is unfree -- it means that Level 2 action is *unvetoed*. An unvetoed free action is still a free action. A president who rarely exercises a veto is not thereby shown to be powerless; the legislative process operates freely, and the veto exists as a reserve capacity that becomes operative when needed.

The confusion arises from equating "free will" exclusively with "conscious, reflective, effortful choice." On the Vertical Agency Model, this equation is an error. Free will exists at Level 2 as default agency and at Level 4 as meta-executive override. Level 4 is rarer because it is costlier, more effortful, and activated only by specific triggers. Its rarity is a feature of efficient design, not evidence of its nonexistence.

### 3.4 Vertical Freedom

We do not merely have free will; we have *vertical freedom* -- the capacity to exercise freedom at multiple levels of the agentive hierarchy, including the capacity to modify our own default free will. Level 4 can override Level 2 not because Level 2 is unfree but because Level 4 operates at a higher level of abstraction, with access to meta-information that Level 2 lacks.

This vertical structure explains phenomena that flat models of free will cannot:

- **Weakness of will (akrasia):** Level 3 monitoring registers that the Level 2 candidate action conflicts with Level 4 values, but Level 4 fails to activate (due to fatigue, distraction, or insufficient triggering). The agent acts against their own higher-order judgment -- not because they lack free will but because the vertical override mechanism was not engaged.

- **Self-transformation:** Level 4 can modify the biases that shape Level 2 processing. Through deliberate practice, therapy, or moral education, agents reshape their default tendencies. This is freedom operating on itself -- meta-freedom.

- **Moral responsibility:** Responsibility attaches primarily to cases where Level 4 *could have* been activated but was not. The agent who acts impulsively is responsible not for lacking Level 4 (all agents have it) but for failing to engage it when the stakes warranted engagement.

## 4. Boundary States and Within-Consciousness States

### 4.1 The Distinction

Not all changes in consciousness are of the same kind. This paper distinguishes two fundamentally different categories of conscious-state transitions:

**Boundary states (threshold states)** are transitions that alter the *structure* of consciousness itself -- they change what kinds of information can be represented, what meta-operations are available, and which levels of the vertical hierarchy are accessible. Examples include:

- Falling asleep / hypnagogia: Level 4 and Level 3 progressively disengage; representational capacity narrows.
- Waking: Levels re-engage; the full vertical hierarchy becomes accessible.
- Ego dissolution (psychedelic states, meditative absorption): The boundary between self-model and world-model becomes permeable; Level 3 self-monitoring temporarily dissolves.
- Sudden insight ("aha!" moments): A rapid reconfiguration of representational structure; previously unconnected information becomes integrated.
- Panic override: Level 4 is forcibly overridden by Level 1 survival circuits; meta-executive control is temporarily suspended.

**Within-consciousness states** are variations in content within a fixed representational structure. Examples include:

- Emotions: anger, joy, sadness, fear.
- Beliefs: propositional attitudes about the world.
- Sensations: pain, warmth, pressure, color.
- Intentions: directed plans for action.
- Imagery: visual, auditory, or other modality-specific representations.

### 4.2 The Key Insight

Boundary states determine *which meta-operations are even possible*. Within-consciousness states determine *what is being processed*. The distinction is analogous to the difference between the operating system and the applications running on it. A boundary-state transition is like changing the operating system -- it alters the fundamental capacities of the system. A within-consciousness state transition is like switching between applications -- it changes the content without altering the underlying architecture.

This distinction has profound implications for the study of consciousness. Much of the consciousness literature conflates boundary phenomena with content phenomena. Theories that attempt to explain "consciousness" as a unitary phenomenon are forced to account for both the transition from sleep to waking (a boundary change) and the difference between seeing red and seeing blue (a content change) with the same mechanism. The present framework suggests that these require different explanations because they operate at different structural levels.

### 4.3 Within-Consciousness Complexity

Within-consciousness states are not simple or isolated. They can:

- **Conflict:** One can feel angry and guilty simultaneously, with the anger and guilt existing at the same level but pulling in different directions.
- **Coexist:** One can hold a belief and an emotion about the same object without one canceling the other.
- **Be nested:** One can have a feeling about a feeling -- anxiety about one's anger, for instance. This nesting is a within-consciousness version of the vertical structure.
- **Be overridden:** A Level 4 veto can suppress a within-consciousness state, though the suppressed state may persist at lower levels (repression, in psychodynamic terms).

### 4.4 Boundary States as Meta-Operational Gates

Boundary states function as *gates* on the meta-operational repertoire. During ordinary waking consciousness, the full four-level hierarchy is accessible: Level 1 generates impulses, Level 2 produces candidate actions, Level 3 monitors, and Level 4 stands ready to veto. During sleep, the gate closes on Levels 3 and 4: dreaming involves Level 1 and Level 2 processing without meta-executive oversight, which is why dream behavior is often impulsive and uncritical. During meditative absorption, the gate on Level 3 self-monitoring may be deliberately narrowed, producing states of "selfless awareness" in which meta-information about the self-model is suspended while perceptual clarity is maintained or enhanced.

The gate metaphor clarifies why altered states of consciousness feel so qualitatively different from ordinary waking life. It is not that the *content* changes dramatically (dreams contain familiar objects, emotions, and scenarios) but that the *meta-operational architecture* changes. Losing access to Level 4 veto capacity is a structural transformation, not a content transformation. This is why boundary-state transitions feel like changes in the *kind* of consciousness rather than changes in what consciousness is about.

## 5. Psychology's ABC Reframed

### 5.1 The Standard Model

Introductory psychology textbooks routinely partition mental life into three domains: Affect (emotions and feelings), Behavior (actions and responses), and Cognition (beliefs, reasoning, and judgment). These three domains are typically presented as laterally adjacent -- three parallel channels of mental processing that interact but remain fundamentally distinct.

### 5.2 The Vertical Reframing

The Vertical Agency Model reveals that the ABC model is not wrong but *misoriented*. Affect, Behavior, and Cognition are not three horizontal modules; they are three aspects of a single vertical hierarchy.

**Affect** is not separate from cognition -- it *is* cognition operating at the meta-evaluative level. Emotions are the system's rapid, holistic assessments of situations relative to goals and values. They are meta-information about the agent's relation to the environment. Fear is not a non-cognitive add-on; it is cognitive processing that evaluates a situation as threatening relative to the agent's survival goals. The felt quality of emotion -- what it is "like" to feel afraid -- is what vertical cognition feels like from the inside when operating at Level 3.

**Cognition** in the narrow sense (propositional reasoning, belief formation) is Level 2 and Level 3 processing operating on representational content. It is not separate from affect but continuous with it. The difference between "I believe this bridge is unsafe" (cognition) and "I feel this bridge is unsafe" (affect) is not a difference in kind but a difference in the level at which the evaluation operates and the format in which it is represented.

**Behavior** is the output of the entire vertical hierarchy. Most behavior is Level 2 output -- default, unvetoed action. The meta-version of behavior is the free-will veto: the Level 4 decision to cancel, redirect, or modify an otherwise default action. Behavioral inhibition, impulse control, and moral self-regulation are all forms of meta-behavior -- behavior operating on behavior.

### 5.3 Implications for Clinical Psychology

The vertical reframing has implications for clinical practice. Therapeutic interventions that target "cognition" (as in cognitive-behavioral therapy) and interventions that target "affect" (as in emotion-focused therapy) are not targeting separate systems -- they are targeting different levels of the same vertical hierarchy. The resistance that patients sometimes show to cognitive restructuring may reflect the fact that cognitive interventions target Level 3, while the relevant processing is occurring at Level 2 (habitual patterns) or Level 1 (autonomic responses). Effective therapy may require interventions that operate at the appropriate *level*, not merely the appropriate *domain*.

## 6. Meta-Relations

### 6.1 Definition and Structure

A Meta-Relation (MR) is a structured evaluative state in which a higher-level process relates to a lower-level state. Formally:

> MR(S_lower, E_meta) = the state in which the system holds first-order state S_lower while simultaneously holding meta-evaluative stance E_meta toward S_lower.

Examples:

- MR(confidence, uncertainty) = "I feel confident [Level 2 emotional state] but I'm uncertain about whether this confidence is warranted [Level 3 meta-evaluation]."
- MR(anger, doubt) = "I'm angry [Level 2] but I doubt that this anger is appropriate [Level 3]."
- MR(intention, hesitation) = "I intend to act [Level 2] but I'm hesitating about whether I should [Level 3/4]."

### 6.2 Non-Cancellation Principle

A critical feature of Meta-Relations is that the meta-state does not cancel the lower-level state. Feeling uncertain about one's confidence does not eliminate the confidence; it layers uncertainty *on top of* confidence. The two states coexist at different vertical levels without mutual annihilation.

This non-cancellation principle explains why human psychology is so often characterized by ambivalence, mixed feelings, and internal conflict. The system can act AS IF confident (Level 2 output proceeding as default) while simultaneously monitoring uncertainty (Level 3 meta-evaluation) and reserving the option to veto (Level 4 standing by). These are not contradictions -- they are the natural operation of a vertically structured system.

### 6.3 Arbitration Weight

When Meta-Relations involve conflicting evaluations, the meta-state carries higher *arbitration weight*. If Level 2 generates confidence and Level 3 registers doubt, the doubt has greater authority over action modification -- not because it is "more true" but because it operates at a higher level of the evaluative hierarchy and has access to a broader informational context.

This asymmetry explains the phenomenology of "overriding one's gut feeling." The gut feeling (Level 2) is not irrational -- it is the output of a genuine evaluative process. But the reflective doubt (Level 3) has access to information that the gut feeling lacks: knowledge of base rates, awareness of cognitive biases, memory of past errors. The higher level's authority derives from its informational advantage, not from any intrinsic superiority.

## 7. Neurocognitive Mapping

### 7.1 Level 1: Subpersonal Processes

Level 1 maps onto phylogenetically ancient neural structures: primary motor cortex (motor preparation), limbic structures including the amygdala (affective impulses), brainstem nuclei (autonomic regulation), and early sensory cortices (perceptual salience). These structures operate with minimal cortical oversight and respond rapidly to environmental stimuli.

### 7.2 Level 2: Default Free Will

Level 2 maps onto the basal ganglia (action selection among competing candidates), orbitofrontal cortex (value-based decision making), and the supplementary motor area (intention formation). These structures mediate the generation of candidate actions and the default selection among them based on learned value associations and habitual patterns.

### 7.3 Level 3: Meta-Information

Level 3 maps onto the insular cortex (interoceptive awareness, emotional meta-representation), medial prefrontal cortex (self-referential processing, default mode network hub), and the posterior cingulate cortex (autobiographical memory integration). These structures support the continuous background monitoring of one's own cognitive and affective states -- the generation of Meta-Relations.

### 7.4 Level 4: Meta-Executive Consciousness

Level 4 maps onto the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (executive control, working memory), anterior cingulate cortex (conflict monitoring, error detection), and frontopolar cortex (meta-cognitive evaluation, prospective reasoning). These structures are activated when the system detects conflict between Level 2 candidates, when error signals exceed a threshold, or when explicit self-interrogation is initiated. They are metabolically expensive, fatigue-prone, and activated selectively rather than continuously -- consistent with the model's prediction that Level 4 is rare and effortful.

### 7.5 Empirical Predictions

The neurocognitive mapping generates testable predictions:

1. Lesions to dorsolateral PFC and anterior cingulate should selectively impair Level 4 (veto capacity) while leaving Level 2 (default free will) intact. This predicts a dissociation between impulsive action (Level 2 without Level 4 override) and loss of action generation (which would require Level 2 damage).

2. Default mode network activity (Level 3) should correlate with meta-representational states -- monitoring without action modification. Increased DMN activity during rest reflects ongoing Level 3 processing in the absence of Level 4 engagement.

3. Anterior cingulate activation should spike at the moment of veto -- the instant when a Level 2 candidate action is cancelled by Level 4 intervention. This is consistent with existing data on ACC activation during response inhibition tasks.

## 8. Integration with TI and Tralse Logic

### 8.1 The MIM Hierarchy as Tralse

Within the Tralse-Informational framework, tralse denotes a truth-value that is simultaneously true and false -- not because of logical contradiction but because the proposition in question captures a genuine ontological ambiguity. The MIM hierarchy is tralse in a precise sense: it is BOTH flat and vertical simultaneously.

It is *flat* in the sense that all four levels process information. There is no categorical break between levels -- no point at which "mere information processing" ends and "consciousness" begins. The hierarchy is continuous, and the boundaries between levels are functional rather than ontological. Information is information at every level.

It is *vertical* in the sense that higher levels operate on lower levels, have greater arbitration weight, and exercise meta-control over lower-level outputs. The hierarchy is genuinely hierarchical: Level 4 can override Level 2 in a way that Level 2 cannot override Level 4.

The flatness and the verticality are both true. Neither description alone captures the full structure. This is a paradigmatic case of tralse ontology: the system is and is not hierarchical, depending on the aspect under consideration.

### 8.2 Free Will as Arbiter, Not Originator

The TI framework holds that information is ontologically fundamental -- not an abstraction from matter but the ground of all existence. Within this framework, free will is reconceived not as the mysterious origination of action from nothing (the libertarian picture) nor as the deterministic output of physical causes (the compatibilist or hard determinist picture) but as the *arbitration* of informational states.

Consciousness, on this view, does not create actions. It arbitrates among candidate actions generated by the agentic core (Level 2), using meta-information (Level 3) as its evidential base and meta-executive evaluation (Level 4) as its adjudicative mechanism. The philosophical dictum that emerges is:

> *Consciousness is not the origin of action -- it is the arbiter of actions. Free will exists at multiple vertical levels, and the highest one exists precisely so it can remain mostly silent.*

The silence of Level 4 is not evidence of its absence. It is evidence of its *efficiency*. A well-designed arbitration system intervenes only when intervention is warranted. The rest of the time, the lower levels operate freely and effectively on their own.

### 8.3 Tralse Epistemology and the Veto

Tralse epistemology holds that the evaluation of truth-claims must accommodate genuine indeterminacy. The free-will veto is an epistemological act as well as a practical one: when Level 4 vetoes a candidate action, it is implicitly judging that the meta-information supplied by Level 3 warrants override. This judgment is not certain -- it is itself subject to error, revision, and meta-meta-evaluation. The veto is tralse in the sense that it is both a definitive action (the candidate is cancelled) and an uncertain evaluation (the cancellation may have been unwarranted). This recursive uncertainty is not a defect; it is an honest reflection of the epistemic condition of finite agents in an open-ended world.

## 9. Objections and Responses

### 9.1 "This Is Just Higher-Order Theory Rebranded"

**Objection.** Higher-order theories of consciousness (Rosenthal, 2005; Lau & Rosenthal, 2011) already hold that consciousness is a matter of higher-order representations of lower-order mental states. The Vertical Agency Model is just higher-order theory with different vocabulary.

**Response.** Higher-order theories focus on the representational relation between levels -- a mental state is conscious when it is the target of a higher-order representation. The VAM shares this relational structure but goes beyond it in three respects. First, VAM specifies *four* functionally distinct levels, not merely two (first-order and higher-order). Second, VAM introduces the free-will veto as a distinctive meta-executive function that higher-order theories do not address. Third, VAM integrates vertical structure with the ABC reframing and with tralse logic, embedding the model in a broader epistemological and ontological framework. Higher-order theories describe a representational relation; VAM describes an agentive architecture.

### 9.2 "Level 2 Cannot Be Both Free and Unreflective"

**Objection.** Freedom requires reflection. An action that is not reflectively endorsed by the agent is not genuinely free -- it is merely spontaneous. Level 2, being unreflective, is therefore not free.

**Response.** This objection begs the question by defining freedom as reflective endorsement. The VAM holds that freedom consists in indeterminacy, agent-generation, and responsiveness to reasons -- conditions that Level 2 satisfies. Reflection adds a further dimension (meta-freedom, Level 4) but does not constitute the only form of freedom. To insist otherwise is to deny freedom to animals, young children, and adults acting spontaneously -- a result that is counterintuitive and empirically implausible.

### 9.3 "The Neurocognitive Mapping Is Too Neat"

**Objection.** Real brains are messy. Mapping four conceptual levels onto discrete neural structures oversimplifies the neuroscience.

**Response.** Granted. The mapping is schematic and intended as a first approximation. Neural processing is distributed, overlapping, and context-dependent. The claim is not that Level 4 *is* the dorsolateral PFC but that dorsolateral PFC function is *characteristic of* Level 4 processing. The mapping generates testable predictions (Section 7.5) that can refine or refute the schematic picture. All theoretical frameworks begin with idealization; the question is whether the idealizations are productive.

## 10. Conclusion

### 10.1 Summary of the Revision

The revised MIM holds that consciousness is not a separate category from cognition but its highest form. The four-level Vertical Agency Model replaces the flat information/meta-information distinction with a hierarchically structured account of agency in which freedom operates at multiple levels and the highest level -- meta-executive consciousness -- functions as the arbiter of actions rather than their originator.

### 10.2 Core Contributions

1. **The Vertical Agency Model** distinguishes four levels of agentive processing with distinct functional profiles, temporal dynamics, and neural correlates.
2. **The free-will veto** resolves confusions in the Libet-era debate by showing that the rarity of meta-executive override does not entail the absence of lower-level freedom.
3. **Meta-Relations** formalize the coexistence of conflicting evaluative states at different vertical levels.
4. **The boundary/within-consciousness distinction** separates structural changes in consciousness from content changes within a fixed structure.
5. **The ABC reframing** replaces the horizontal modular model with a vertical integration of affect, behavior, and cognition.
6. **Integration with tralse logic** grounds the model in TI epistemology, showing that the MIM hierarchy is both flat and vertical simultaneously.

### 10.3 The Central Claim

Consciousness is not the origin of action. It is the arbiter of actions. Free will exists at multiple vertical levels, and the highest one exists precisely so it can remain mostly silent. The silence is not impotence -- it is the mark of a system that trusts its lower levels to operate freely, intervening only when the stakes demand it. This is what it means for consciousness to be the highest form of cognition: not a separate substance, not an epiphenomenal byproduct, but the apex of a hierarchy that is cognitive all the way down.
