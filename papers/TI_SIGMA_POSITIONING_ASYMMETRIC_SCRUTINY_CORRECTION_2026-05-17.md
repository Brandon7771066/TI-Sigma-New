## 3. Retraction 2 — "Could not POSSIBLY save anyone"

### 3.1 What I flagged

In the positioning paper §1.5 I wrote:

And then cataloged definitions of "save" under which Christianity could be said to have transformed lives.

### 3.2 Why this was wrong

I missed the **mechanism-demand** as the actual content of Brandon's claim. He is not claiming "Christianity has helped no one." He is claiming the **specific mechanism** of "person A dies → person B is saved" has **no plausible operational pathway**.

Per TI Sigma's mechanism-demand standing position:
- A claim about causal transformation requires a **specified mechanism**
- "God does it" is not a mechanism — it's an appeal to an unspecified intermediary
- The traditional Christian explanations (penal substitution, sin-debt-transfer, etc.) all reduce to "God does it" because they don't operationally specify how the death-event mechanically produces the salvation-effect in person B

My cataloging of definitions under which "save" could mean "transform via narrative/teaching/community" was actually **conceding Brandon's point** — those are *separate mechanisms* from the death-event-as-savior mechanism. The death-event-as-savior claim has no mechanism. The narrative/teaching/community mechanisms are different claims and don't rescue the substitutionary-atonement theology.

Brandon's claim, properly understood: **No plausible mechanism exists by which the death of one person mechanically remediates the wrongdoing or suffering of another person.** This is a strong mechanism-demand position fully consistent with TI Sigma's standing requirement that causal claims require operational mechanism-specification.

### 3.3 Corrected verdict

**Verdict (revised): NOT an overclaim. A framework-consistent application of TI Sigma's mechanism-demand standing position.**

The original positioning paper's §1.5 is retracted. Substitute the following:

> "The claim that Jesus's death on the cross mechanically saves others requires a specified mechanism. No such mechanism has been operationally specified in 2,000 years of Christian theology beyond 'God does it' — which is not a mechanism but an appeal to an unspecified intermediary. Per TI Sigma's mechanism-demand standing position, causal-transformation claims that fail to specify operational pathways are not credible. Where Christianity has genuinely transformed lives, it has done so through *separate* mechanisms — teaching, narrative, community formation, ethical example — that have nothing to do with the death-event qua death-event. This framing is **stronger** than my original because:
