## Abstract

The randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial (RCT) — "the gold standard of medical evidence" — was designed to answer a specific epistemic question: is the observed effect of an intervention causally attributable to the intervention's pharmacological mechanism rather than to expectation, observer bias, or selection effects? This is the **mechanistic attribution question**. It is a legitimate scientific question. It is, however, a completely different question from the **pragmatic efficacy question**: does this intervention produce net positive outcomes for patients who receive it? Medical regulatory frameworks have systematically conflated these two questions — requiring an affirmative answer to the mechanistic question before permitting access to the pragmatic benefit. The placebo-control criterion for psychedelic research exemplifies this conflation at its most consequential and most perverse: psychedelics cannot be adequately double-blinded because the subjective experience is unmistakable, and this methodological fact is used to indefinitely delay access to treatments that demonstrably help patients. This paper argues that the conflation is a category error — a philosophical error with life-and-death consequences — and that the correct epistemic framework separates pragmatic and mechanistic questions, answers each with appropriate methods, and permits pragmatic access on pragmatic evidence without requiring mechanistic certainty as a precondition.

---

## 1. The Two Questions Medical Science Conflates

### 1.1 The Mechanistic Attribution Question

The mechanistic attribution question asks: **Is the observed effect of this treatment causally produced by the treatment's specific pharmacological mechanism, rather than by expectation, placebo response, regression to the mean, or other non-specific factors?**

This is the question the RCT was designed to answer. It answers it through three methodological innovations:

1. **Randomization:** eliminates selection bias — ensures that the treatment and control groups are similar in expectation across all measured and unmeasured variables
2. **Blinding of researchers:** eliminates observer bias — ensures that the people measuring outcomes do not know which participants received treatment, preventing unconscious bias in assessment
3. **Blinding of participants:** eliminates expectation effects — ensures that participants do not know whether they received the active treatment, so that any observed effect cannot be attributed to the belief that they received effective treatment

The RCT is an excellent instrument for answering the mechanistic attribution question when all three conditions can be met. It is the appropriate scientific tool for isolating the specific pharmacological contribution of a treatment, net of all non-specific effects.

### 1.2 The Pragmatic Efficacy Question

The pragmatic efficacy question asks: **Does this intervention produce net positive outcomes — relative to available alternatives — for patients who receive it in real-world conditions?**

This is the question patients, clinicians, and policymakers actually need answered. A cancer patient with depression wants to know whether psilocybin-assisted therapy will help them feel less afraid of death and more at peace with their remaining time. They do not want to know the isolated pharmacological contribution of psilocybin, net of the expectation effects of receiving a novel treatment in a supportive therapeutic context. They want to know whether the complete experience — drug, set, setting, therapeutic relationship, expectation — will help them.

The pragmatic efficacy question is answerable by methods that do not require mechanistic attribution: open-label trials, real-world evidence, patient-reported outcomes, comparative effectiveness research. It asks whether a treatment works in practice, for real people, in conditions that include all the factors that accompany real treatment.

### 1.3 The Conflation and Its Consequences

Medical regulatory frameworks — the FDA, EMA, and their analogs — have elevated the mechanistic attribution question to the position of necessary precondition for the pragmatic efficacy question. The operational logic: a treatment may not be considered pragmatically effective for regulatory purposes unless its effect can be mechanistically attributed to its pharmacological mechanism via double-blind RCT.

This is not a necessary logical relationship. It is a policy choice. And it is, in the specific case of psychedelics — and in many others — a catastrophically wrong one.

## 2. The Placebo Problem: Why Psychedelics Fail the Wrong Test

### 2.1 The Blinding Impossibility

For most pharmacological compounds, adequate double-blinding is achievable: a pill that looks and tastes identical to the active drug can be manufactured, and most drugs do not produce such immediate and distinctive subjective effects that participants can reliably determine which group they are in.

Psychedelics are categorically different. A full dose of psilocybin produces one of the most distinctive, unmistakable subjective experiences available to human beings: profound perceptual alterations, dissolution of ordinary ego boundaries, radically altered temporal experience, often overwhelming emotional and spiritual content. No known substance produces a credible placebo for this. Niacin — the most commonly used "active placebo" — produces a skin flush and some warmth. It is not remotely comparable. Every serious researcher in the field acknowledges this: participants in psychedelic trials know, with near-certainty, whether they received the drug.

The conclusion drawn by critics using the gold-standard framework: psychedelic research cannot adequately control for expectation effects. Therefore, positive findings may reflect placebo response. Therefore, the evidence base is insufficient for approval.

### 2.2 Why This Conclusion Is the Category Error

The conclusion does not follow from the premise. What follows from the blinding impossibility is that psychedelic research cannot fully answer the **mechanistic attribution question** by conventional RCT methodology. It does not follow that psychedelic research cannot answer the **pragmatic efficacy question**.

The distinction matters enormously. If a patient with treatment-resistant depression receives psilocybin-assisted therapy and experiences a sustained remission — reduced depression scores, improved quality of life, functional recovery — the mechanistic attribution question asks: how much of this improvement is attributable to psilocybin's pharmacological action (5-HT2A agonism, neuroplasticity induction) versus to the expectation of receiving an effective treatment? The pragmatic efficacy question asks: did this patient get better?

Demanding an answer to the mechanistic attribution question before permitting the pragmatic benefit is not scientific rigor. It is the sacrifice of real patients to methodological purity.

## 3. The Expectation Effect Is Not a Confound — It Is Part of the Mechanism

### 3.1 Expectation as Pharmacological Partner

The standard framing treats expectation effects as contaminants to be eliminated from the "true" pharmacological effect. This framing assumes a sharp metaphysical distinction between:

(a) The "real" pharmacological effect — psilocybin's action on 5-HT2A receptors, BDNF upregulation, default mode network disruption
(b) The "confounded" expectation effect — the therapeutic benefit produced by the belief that one has received an effective treatment

But this distinction is biologically naive. Expectation effects operate through identifiable neurobiological mechanisms: endogenous opioid release, dopaminergic activation, prefrontal modulation of amygdala response, neuroimmune effects. These are not "fake" effects. They are real neurobiological changes produced by cognitive states — including the cognitive state of expecting effective treatment.

The complete treatment package — psilocybin pharmacology + therapeutic set + supportive setting + expectation of benefit — produces a neurobiological state that differs from any of its components alone. The attempt to isolate the "pure" pharmacological component and designate it the "real" effect is not scientific precision. It is the arbitrary privileging of one mechanistic pathway (receptor binding) over others (expectation-mediated neurobiological change) on the basis of a philosophical prejudice about what counts as "real" causation.

### 3.2 The Therapeutic Alliance Parallel

Psychotherapy research has long recognized that a substantial portion of therapy's efficacy is attributable to "non-specific factors" — the therapeutic alliance, the client's expectation of benefit, the act of attending to one's mental state in a supportive context. These effects are real, robust, and reproducible. They are not considered a reason to deny approval to psychotherapy.

The demand that psychedelics prove their efficacy net of expectation effects — when no other approved treatment is held to this standard — is selective application of a methodological criterion that, if applied uniformly, would invalidate many of medicine's most widely accepted treatments.

## 4. The Selective Application of the Gold Standard

### 4.1 Approved Treatments That Fail Their Own Standard

If the gold-standard RCT requirement were applied uniformly — if demonstrable mechanistic attribution were truly required before approval — the pharmaceutical landscape would look dramatically different:

**SSRIs and SNRIs:** The serotonin hypothesis of depression — that SSRIs work by correcting a serotonin deficiency — has been substantially discredited (Moncrieff et al., 2022). The mechanistic basis for the most prescribed class of antidepressants is contested. Furthermore, SSRI trials are compromised by imperfect blinding: participants can often detect treatment assignment from side effects (dry mouth, sexual dysfunction, sleep changes), producing expectation effects in the active arm. By the logic used against psychedelics, SSRI approval is methodologically compromised. They remain approved. They are first-line treatment worldwide.

**Lithium for bipolar disorder:** Approved and widely used for decades before any plausible mechanism was identified. It works — demonstrably, robustly, across many populations. The mechanism remains disputed. Pragmatic efficacy without mechanistic certainty was, in this case, sufficient for clinical acceptance.

**Aspirin:** Used for effective pain relief for decades before COX inhibition was understood. Mechanistic attribution followed pragmatic efficacy by half a century. The pragmatic evidence was sufficient for widespread use.

**Most anesthetics:** The mechanism by which general anesthetics produce loss of consciousness remains an open scientific question. Anesthesia is one of the most widely used medical interventions in history. Pragmatic efficacy without mechanistic certainty has been, in this case, entirely sufficient.

**Metformin:** Mechanism debated for decades after widespread adoption for type 2 diabetes. Pragmatic efficacy — it clearly works — drove adoption. Mechanistic certainty followed.

### 4.2 The Pattern of Selective Application

The pattern is clear: the gold standard of mechanistic attribution is applied stringently to treatments that are politically inconvenient or threaten existing pharmaceutical revenue streams, and applied loosely to treatments that are politically acceptable or economically convenient. This is not science. It is political economy wearing a white coat.

Psychedelics are politically inconvenient — associated with the counterculture, Schedule I controlled substance status, decades of federal research prohibition, and the Drug War ideology. They are also, in principle, a profound threat to the pharmaceutical business model: a small number of supervised sessions producing lasting improvement is not a recurring prescription revenue stream.

The selective application of the gold standard is not random noise. It is a systematic bias with identifiable beneficiaries.

## 5. The Correct Epistemic Framework

### 5.1 Separate the Questions

The correct framework answers the pragmatic efficacy question and the mechanistic attribution question separately, using methods appropriate to each:

**For pragmatic efficacy:**
- Open-label trials measuring real-world outcomes
- Patient-reported outcomes over meaningful time horizons
- Comparative effectiveness research: psychedelics vs. current standard of care (not vs. placebo)
- Long-term naturalistic studies of practitioners who use psychedelics legally (where available) or historically
- The Johns Hopkins, NYU, and Imperial College studies, taken collectively, constitute a substantial pragmatic evidence base

**For mechanistic attribution:**
- Pharmaco-fMRI and EEG studies isolating neural mechanisms
- Dose-response studies
- Biomarker research (BDNF, neuroplasticity markers)
- Active placebo comparisons where achievable at sub-perceptual doses
- Computational modeling of receptor occupancy and downstream effects

These are complementary research programs. The mechanistic research is scientifically valuable. It should be funded and pursued. It should not be a gatekeeping requirement for pragmatic access.

### 5.2 The Correct Approval Standard

The correct question for regulatory approval is not: "Has the mechanistic attribution of this treatment's efficacy been established beyond confounding by non-specific factors?"

The correct question is: "Does this treatment produce net positive outcomes for the patients who receive it, relative to available alternatives, with an acceptable safety profile?"

This standard permits: pragmatic approval based on pragmatic evidence, ongoing mechanistic research as a parallel track, and regulatory decisions calibrated to actual patient benefit rather than methodological purity.

It prohibits: indefinite denial of access to demonstrably effective treatments on the grounds that we cannot mechanistically attribute their efficacy with the precision demanded by a methodology that was never designed to be applicable in their case.

### 5.3 The Pragmatic Certainty Connection

This argument is a direct application of the Pragmatic Certainty Theorem (URB #422) to the domain of medical epistemology. The theorem established: for human agents making real-world decisions, acting from pragmatic evidence (first-order certainty about outcomes) while maintaining second-order awareness of mechanistic uncertainty is superior to withholding action until mechanistic certainty is achieved.

The application: a patient, a clinician, and a regulator who observe that psilocybin-assisted therapy produces sustained remission in treatment-resistant depression should act on that pragmatic evidence (permit access) while maintaining second-order mechanistic awareness (continue researching attribution). The demand to achieve second-order mechanistic certainty before permitting first-order pragmatic action is exactly the error the Pragmatic Certainty Theorem identifies: treating the calibration phase (mechanistic research) as a precondition for the action phase (clinical access), when the correct relationship is sequential rather than conditional.

## 6. The Ethical Dimension

### 6.1 The Cost of Methodological Purity

Every year that psychedelic access is denied on methodological grounds, specific categories of patients are denied access to the most promising treatments available for their conditions:

- **Treatment-resistant depression:** 100 million people globally; current treatments fail approximately 30% of patients; psilocybin trials show response rates of 50-70% in this population
- **Terminal illness anxiety:** Patients dying of cancer with profound existential distress; psilocybin produces lasting reductions in death anxiety in a majority of patients who receive a single session
- **PTSD:** Veterans, abuse survivors, trauma patients for whom SSRIs, CBT, and EMDR have failed; MDMA-assisted therapy shows 67-71% response rates vs. 32-48% for placebo in the most rigorous trials available

These are real people experiencing real suffering. The argument that they must continue to suffer until mechanistic attribution meets a methodological standard that was designed for a different class of compounds — and that is selectively applied to politically inconvenient treatments — is not a scientific position. It is an ethical failure dressed in scientific language.

### 6.2 Autonomy and the Pragmatic Standard

The deepest ethical argument is about autonomy. An adult patient who has been informed of the pragmatic evidence (these treatments appear to work for these conditions), the mechanistic uncertainty (we don't fully know why or how much of the effect is pharmacological), and the safety profile (psychedelics are among the safest substances known when used in appropriate settings) has, in principle, all the information needed to make a rational decision about whether to use them.

Withholding that option — not because the treatment is dangerous, not because there is no evidence of benefit, but because the evidence of benefit doesn't satisfy a mechanistic attribution standard that most approved treatments also fail — is paternalistic interference with rational adult decision-making in the service of methodological ideology.

The pragmatic standard respects autonomy: if it works, and you choose it with full information, you should have access to it. The mechanistic attribution standard does not: even if it works, and you choose it with full information, you cannot have access until we resolve a philosophical question about the precise causal pathway of your benefit.

---

## 7. Conclusion: The Category Error and Its Correction

The gold standard RCT was designed to answer the mechanistic attribution question. It is the right tool for that question. It is the wrong gatekeeping requirement for the pragmatic efficacy question. Using one as a proxy for the other is a category error — a confusion of epistemic categories with life-and-death consequences.

The correction is simple in principle, though politically difficult in practice: separate the two questions, answer each with appropriate methods, and permit pragmatic access on pragmatic evidence. Continue mechanistic research in parallel. Do not subordinate the suffering of real patients to the resolution of a philosophical question about causal attribution that most approved treatments have not resolved either.

The argument that psychedelics cannot meet the gold standard because their subjective effects make adequate blinding impossible is not a finding about the drugs. It is a finding about the mismatch between the methodology and the subject matter. The methodologically correct response is to adapt the methods to the subject matter — not to condemn the subject matter for failing to conform to methods designed for a different class of phenomena.

The psychedelic placebo problem is, in the end, a problem for the gold standard — not for the psychedelics.

URB #529 (*Pragmatism as Epiphenomenon: The Four Truth Dimensions Account*) establishes that the mechanistic attribution question corresponds to **existential truth** (LCC — does the treatment actually cause the outcome?), while the pragmatic efficacy question corresponds to the **four-dimensional GILE integral** (does the treatment produce overall truth value across existential, moral, valence, and aesthetic dimensions?). This paper's core claim — that pragmatic access does not require mechanistic certainty as a precondition — maps precisely to URB #529's claim that existential truth is one of four dimensions, not the only one. A treatment with uncertain mechanistic LCC but strong moral, valence, and aesthetic truth scores a defensible GILE integral, which is the correct basis for pragmatic access decisions.
