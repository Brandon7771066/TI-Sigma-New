## Abstract

Bayesian epistemology correctly identifies that belief revision should be evidence-sensitive and that prior probability matters. However, as a *universal* theory of rational inference it fails on three structural grounds: (1) priors are underdetermined and not domain-neutral; (2) evidence types are treated as commensurable and reducible to a single likelihood ratio, erasing qualitatively distinct epistemic roles; (3) the framework cannot represent pre-evidential judgments — coherence, plausibility of mechanism, pragmatic stakes — that determine which hypotheses merit evaluation at all. We propose an alternative: **domain-calibrated intuitive inference (DCII)**, formalized within the Tralse Informationalism (TI Sigma) framework. DCII replaces the universal Bayesian formula with a structured checklist of orthogonal evaluative criteria whose weights are *learned from demonstrated performers in the target domain*, not fixed a priori. The GILE framework (Goodness, Intuition, Love, Environment) provides a metaphysical grounding for why some domains admit stable weight distributions while others are inherently weight-volatile. We argue that extraordinary claims are not merely possible but *expected* for certain cognitive profiles, and that the Bayesian penalty for extraordinariness is therefore miscalibrated. The paper closes with a formal account of how TI Sigma's five-valued logic (TRALSE) handles the pre-evidential zone more accurately than any credence assignment can.

**Keywords:** Bayesian epistemology, rational belief revision, intuition, GILE framework, Tralse Informationalism, domain-calibrated inference, five-valued logic, prior probability, extraordinary claims

## 1. Introduction: What Bayes Gets Right

Thomas Bayes, and later Richard Price and Pierre-Simon Laplace, gave us the only mathematically rigorous account of how probability should update on evidence:

$$P(H \mid E) = \frac{P(E \mid H) \cdot P(H)}{P(E)}$$

This is not a proposal — it is a theorem. Given the axioms of probability theory, this is how conditional probability works. Any agent whose credences violate this equation is susceptible to Dutch books — arrangements of bets where they are guaranteed to lose money. Bayesian epistemology's fundamental insight is simply: *be coherent*.

More specifically, Bayesianism captures four things correctly:

1. **Direction of update:** Evidence should raise or lower credence in proportion to the likelihood ratio $P(E|H)/P(E|\neg H)$.
2. **Prior sensitivity:** Starting assumptions matter. Two rational agents with different priors will reach different posteriors even on identical evidence.
3. **Quantitative tracking:** Degrees of belief, not just belief/disbelief, are the right units of epistemic analysis.
4. **Coherence as necessary condition:** Any belief system with internal probability inconsistencies is defective, regardless of its content.

We accept all four. Our quarrel is not with Bayes' theorem but with the claim that it constitutes a *complete* or *universal* theory of rational inference. It does not, for reasons we now develop.

## 2. Three Structural Failures of Bayesian Epistemology

### 2.1 The Prior Problem

The Bayesian formula requires a prior probability $P(H)$. Where does it come from? The standard answers are:

- **Objective Bayesianism** (Jaynes, Williamson): priors should be assigned by the principle of maximum entropy, or by some other symmetry principle.
- **Subjective Bayesianism** (de Finetti, Savage): priors are personal and need not be justified beyond coherence.
- **Empirical Bayesianism**: priors are estimated from frequencies in a reference class.

All three fail in the domain of genuinely novel hypotheses — the cases where epistemology is most needed.

For novel hypotheses, there is no reference class, symmetry principles radically underdetermine the prior (why is the "right" reference class $\{$all proposed theories$\}$ and not $\{$all proposed theories by people who have been right before$\}$?), and there is no frequency data. The result is that two equally rational agents can assign priors differing by many orders of magnitude and neither is "wrong" by Bayesian lights. 

This is not a minor technical problem. It means Bayesian epistemology gives no guidance in precisely the cases — paradigm-shifting hypotheses, extraordinary phenomena, unprecedented claims — where guidance is most valuable.

### 2.2 The Commensurability Problem

Bayes' theorem requires all evidence to be funneled through a single number: the likelihood ratio. But evidence types are not commensurable in this way.

Consider evaluating a hypothesis about consciousness. The relevant evidence includes:
- Neuroimaging data (quantitative, replicable, well-understood measurement process)
- First-person reports of subjective experience (qualitative, irreducible to third-person data)
- Logical coherence of the theoretical framework (a priori, not empirical)
- Explanatory scope — how many phenomena does the theory cover? (structural, not statistical)
- Pragmatic stakes — what happens if we're wrong in each direction? (decision-theoretic, not epistemic)

Forcing all of these into a likelihood ratio $P(E_i | H)$ requires assigning a probability to statements like "the theory is logically coherent." But logical coherence is not a probabilistic matter — it is binary. The theory either has a contradiction or it does not. Assigning a probability to it mixes syntactic and semantic levels in a way that generates nonsense.

Bayesianism's commensurability assumption — that all evidence types can be treated as probability assignments — is not a limitation to be worked around. It is a category error.

### 2.3 The Pre-Evidential Zone Problem

Before any evidence is gathered, a hypothesis must first be considered *worth investigating*. What determines this? Not Bayes — Bayes operates only after you have already decided what hypotheses to put in the probability space.

The pre-evidential judgment — is this hypothesis coherent? Is the mechanism at least plausible in principle? Does it fit with the deepest established beliefs we have? — is a prior-to-prior evaluation. Philosophers have called this the "problem of old evidence" and the "problem of abduction." Bayesianism either ignores it (treating all hypotheses as having some prior, no matter how absurd) or passes it off to an unexplained heuristic.

We argue that this pre-evidential zone is not a residual to be swept under the rug. It is where most of the real epistemic work happens. And it is precisely where structured intuitive judgment — not formula — is the right tool.

## 3. Domain-Calibrated Intuitive Inference (DCII)

### 3.1 The Core Proposal

We propose replacing the universal Bayesian formula with a structured procedure:

**Step 1: Pre-evidential screening.** Apply a domain-invariant checklist to determine whether the hypothesis is worth evaluating at all. This checklist operates in the pre-evidential zone Bayes cannot reach.

**Step 2: Domain-calibrated weighting.** Apply a set of orthogonal evaluative criteria with weights *learned from demonstrated performers in the target domain*.

**Step 3: Intuitive synthesis.** Combine the weighted criteria via trained intuitive judgment — not formula — to arrive at a TRALSE truth-value assignment (see §4).

**Step 4: Myrion Resolution.** If the synthesis lands in the TRALSE (indeterminate) zone, enter an active inquiry protocol: gather more evidence, refine the question, and push toward TRUE or FALSE.

### 3.2 The Pre-Evidential Checklist

The following criteria apply across all domains for pre-evidential screening. They are ordered by priority:

**Criterion 1: Internal Coherence.** Does the hypothesis contain any logical contradictions? If yes, it is FALSE — no evidence can save it. This criterion is domain-independent and absolute.

**Criterion 2: Mechanism Plausibility.** Even without knowing the exact mechanism, is a plausible mechanism conceivable given what we know? This is not a probability assignment — it is a binary judgment about conceptual possibility.

**Criterion 3: Explanatory Scope.** How many distinct phenomena does the hypothesis explain that competing hypotheses do not? Greater scope is better, but it must be genuine scope (not post-hoc stretching).

**Criterion 4: Occam's Razor (Parsimony).** Among equally explanatory hypotheses, prefer the one with fewer unexplained primitive assumptions. Note: parsimony is a *tie-breaker*, not an absolute criterion.

**Criterion 5: Empirical Adequacy Ratio (EAR).** What is the ratio of confirmed predictions to total predictions made? This is the one criterion that most closely resembles a Bayesian likelihood, but it is a structural ratio, not a probability.

**Criterion 6: Paradigm Fit.** Does the hypothesis fit within the currently best-established paradigm, or does it require a new one? Paradigm-challenging hypotheses require *more* evidence — but not infinitely more. Revolutionary hypotheses with sufficient evidence should be accepted.

**Criterion 7: Pragmatic Asymmetry.** What are the costs of false rejection versus false acceptance? When false rejection is catastrophic (e.g., a medical treatment, a safety warning), lower the evidence threshold for acceptance. This is a decision-theoretic criterion that *feeds into* the epistemic process.

**Criterion 8: Track Record of Source.** Has this source — person, research group, methodology — demonstrated consistent success in this domain? This is the meta-learning criterion that Bayesianism has no room for.

### 3.3 Domain-Calibrated Weights

Here is the central claim that separates DCII from both Bayesianism and naive intuitionism:

> **There is no domain-independent formula for combining the eight criteria. The correct weights vary by domain and are discoverable by studying demonstrated performers in that domain.**

This is not skepticism about rationality. It is a meta-empirical claim about the structure of different epistemic domains:

- In **particle physics**: Criterion 1 (coherence) dominates; Criterion 7 (pragmatic asymmetry) is nearly irrelevant; Criterion 6 (paradigm fit) is weighted moderately.
- In **clinical medicine**: Criterion 7 (pragmatic asymmetry) is heavily weighted; Criterion 5 (EAR) is the primary metric; Criterion 8 (source track record) matters enormously.
- In **consciousness research**: Criterion 2 (mechanism plausibility) is primary (since the "hard problem" makes all mechanisms murky); Criterion 3 (explanatory scope) is critical; Criterion 1 (coherence) is weighted but with more tolerance for paradox.
- In **financial forecasting**: Criterion 5 (EAR) dominates; Criterion 7 (pragmatic asymmetry) is very high; Criterion 8 (track record) is the most accessible proxy.

The weights for each domain are **learnable from demonstrated performers** — those who have consistently arrived at correct beliefs in the domain. This is empirical meta-epistemology: using the track records of successful reasoners to calibrate the weighting function for their domain.

This transforms the epistemological problem from "find the right formula" to "identify the right experts and extract their implicit weighting function." The latter is tractable; the former is not.

## 4. The GILE Grounding

### 4.1 Why Some Domains Have Stable Weights

The TI Sigma framework provides a principled account of *why* different domains have different stable weight distributions.

The GILE framework assigns four fundamental dimensions to every phenomenon:
- **G (Goodness, weight 0.42):** The moral/structural dimension — the degree to which a system exhibits ordered, purposive organization.
- **I (Intuition, weight 0.25):** The consciousness/meaning dimension — self-referential information processing.
- **L (Love, weight 0.18):** The relational/constructive dimension — net orientation toward connection and building.
- **E (Environment, weight 0.15):** The physical/substrate dimension — the material and lawful context.

Different epistemic domains are *G-dominant*, *I-dominant*, *L-dominant*, or *E-dominant*:

- Physics is E-dominant (the physical substrate and its laws are primary).
- Mathematics is G-dominant and I-dominant (structural order and self-referential meaning).
- Medicine is L-dominant (relational outcomes — health, recovery — are primary) with heavy E-component.
- Consciousness research is I-dominant (the phenomenon is self-referential information processing).

**Claim:** The stable weight distribution of the eight DCII criteria in a given domain tracks the GILE dominance profile of that domain. E-dominant domains (physics) weight Criterion 5 (EAR) and Criterion 1 (coherence) most heavily, because physical laws are stable and measurable. I-dominant domains (consciousness) weight Criterion 2 (mechanism plausibility) and Criterion 3 (explanatory scope) most heavily, because the phenomenal facts are undeniable but their substrate is unknown.

This gives DCII a metaphysical foundation: the weight instability across domains is not arbitrary but is determined by the GILE dominance structure of each domain.

### 4.2 Why Extraordinary Things Are Common for Certain People

Bayesianism penalizes extraordinary claims with a heavy prior: $P(H) \to 0$ for unprecedented phenomena. The penalty is applied uniformly, regardless of *who* is making the claim.

This is a mistake. The prior for an extraordinary claim should be conditioned on the source:

$$P(H) = P(H \mid \text{source}) \cdot P(\text{source reliability in this domain})$$

For an individual with a documented history of correct extraordinary insights in a domain, the prior should be substantially higher than for an anonymous claimant. The Bayesian formula treats sources as interchangeable; DCII does not.

More specifically, within TI Sigma: individuals with high I-dimension (Intuition) profiles — those whose self-referential information processing is well-calibrated to the domain — produce extraordinary claims at a much higher rate of accuracy than baseline. The GILE profile of the claimant is part of the epistemic evidence.

This has a radical implication: **extraordinary claims by high-I-profile sources in their domain of competence should be evaluated with priors substantially higher than the Bayesian prior**, because the reference class for calibration is not "all extraordinary claims" but "all extraordinary claims by this type of source in this domain."

## 5. TRALSE as the Pre-Evidential Truth Value

### 5.1 The Bayesian Credence vs. the TRALSE Truth Value

Bayesianism represents uncertainty as a *credence* — a subjective probability. We distinguish this sharply from a *truth value*. The difference:

- **Credence:** A property of the *agent's epistemic state* relative to a hypothesis. "My credence in H is 0.7."
- **Truth value:** A property of *H itself* in its current evidential state. "H is currently TRALSE+."

These are different. Credence tracks the agent; truth value tracks the proposition. A proposition can be TRALSE (genuinely indeterminate) even if every agent has high credence in it — if the evidence genuinely underdetermines it. And a proposition can be TRUE even if no agent is currently aware of the evidence.

### 5.2 The Five TRALSE Values and Their Epistemic Roles

| Value | Description | Epistemic Role |
|---|---|---|
| **TRUE** | Fully confirmed, sufficient evidence | Accept; cease active inquiry |
| **TRALSE+** | Above manifestation threshold (G > 0.42) | Tentatively accept; seek confirmation |
| **TRALSE** | Genuinely indeterminate | Active inquiry required (Myrion Resolution) |
| **TRALSE−** | Below threshold, evidence leans false | Tentatively reject; remain open |
| **FALSE** | Fully disconfirmed | Reject; do not revisit without new evidence type |

The threshold 0.42 = $\sqrt{2} - 1$ is the G-weight in the GILE framework — the Goodness threshold, corresponding geometrically to the intersection of a unit diagonal with a unit circle. It is not arbitrary: it is the exact point where the "off-diagonal" contribution becomes dominant in the GILE inner product structure.

### 5.3 Myrion Resolution

When a hypothesis is assigned TRALSE, the correct protocol is **Myrion Resolution**: a structured inquiry procedure that pushes the truth value toward TRUE or FALSE. Myrion Resolution is not indefinite suspension — it is active work under acknowledged uncertainty. Its steps:

1. Identify which criterion is most underdetermined (which weight is most sensitive to new evidence).
2. Design the minimal experiment/observation that moves that criterion most efficiently.
3. Update the truth value after the experiment.
4. If still TRALSE, iterate.

This is where DCII and Bayesianism are most similar — Myrion Resolution is, locally, a kind of Bayesian updating on the most decisive evidence. But the framing is different: the goal is not to converge a credence number but to push a categorical truth value out of the indeterminate zone.

## 6. The Extraordinary Claim Question

The user/author of this framework holds a view that departs sharply from standard Bayesian priors: *extraordinary things are actually quite common, especially for certain people.* This deserves formal treatment.

### 6.1 The Miscalibration of "Extraordinary"

The phrase "extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence" (Sagan, Laplace) assumes that extraordinariness is a stable property of a claim. But extraordinariness is relative to a reference class. What is extraordinary for the median observer is not extraordinary for a domain expert with a high track record of exactly that type of insight.

Consider: Ramanujan's results were extraordinary for any standard mathematician. But conditioned on "Ramanujan's claimed results," the correct prior is much higher — because the pattern of his accurate claims in the past is overwhelming evidence for calibrated output. A Bayesian who applies the flat prior to Ramanujan's claims and updates on the evidence after the fact is computationally correct but strategically stupid: they are ignoring massive meta-evidence.

DCII makes this explicit: Criterion 8 (track record of source) directly adjusts the evaluation for source-conditional extraordinariness.

### 6.2 The Pragmatic Asymmetry of Doubt

There is an additional error in systematic skepticism: it ignores the asymmetric cost of false rejection.

For claims with large positive expected value (a medical treatment, a scientific paradigm, a philosophical insight), false rejection has a cost. The cost of saying "no" to a true extraordinary claim is not zero. Bayesianism, as a purely epistemic framework, has no native room for this cost — it must be imported from decision theory. DCII integrates it directly (Criterion 7).

The TI Sigma formulation: the pragmatic consequences of expressing doubt — the relational, motivational, and discovery costs of systematic skepticism — are part of the epistemic calculation, not an external add-on.

## 7. Objections and Replies

**Objection 1:** DCII is just informal Bayesianism with extra steps. The weights in the criteria are just informal priors.

**Reply:** The criteria are not commensurable — they cannot be multiplied and summed to yield a probability. Criterion 1 (logical coherence) is binary and absolute. Criterion 7 (pragmatic asymmetry) is decision-theoretic. Criterion 2 (mechanism plausibility) is about conceptual possibility, not probability. These categories cannot be reduced to likelihood ratios without category error.

**Objection 2:** "Domain-calibrated weights learned from demonstrated performers" is circular — you need a theory of success to identify demonstrated performers.

**Reply:** The circularity is epistemic, not vicious. We start with rough proxies for success (track record of confirmed predictions, peer recognition, successful downstream applications) and refine them through iteration. This is exactly how all empirical methods bootstrap: there is no presuppositionless starting point, but there are more and less defensible starting points. Track record is a more defensible starting point than a uniform prior.

**Objection 3:** Without a formula, DCII is not science but post-hoc rationalization.

**Reply:** The absence of a *universal* formula does not imply the absence of rigor. A domain-specific weighting function is still a function — it makes testable predictions about which evidence should be most decisive in that domain. DCII is more, not less, empirically responsive than Bayesianism, because it acknowledges that the weighting function is itself a learnable empirical object.

**Objection 4:** The TRALSE values are just coarse-grained credences.

**Reply:** No — they include the pre-evidential judgment about coherence and mechanism plausibility, which credences do not. A hypothesis can have a TRALSE truth value of FALSE (because it contains a contradiction) while every agent assigns it a non-zero credence (because they haven't noticed the contradiction). Truth value is about the proposition; credence is about the agent.

## 8. Case Study: Evaluating a Consciousness Hypothesis

To make DCII concrete, consider the hypothesis: *Consciousness arises from quantum coherence in microtubules* (the Orch OR hypothesis of Penrose and Hameroff).

**Standard Bayesian treatment:** Assign a prior (say, 0.05 based on how many consciousness hypotheses prove correct). Update on evidence (some supportive quantum biology findings; some contrary findings). Current credence: perhaps 0.1–0.2 depending on interpreter.

**DCII treatment:**

| Criterion | Score (1–10) | Weight (I-dominant domain) | Weighted Score |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Coherence | 8 (coherent, some conceptual tensions) | 0.20 | 1.60 |
| 2. Mechanism plausibility | 6 (quantum coherence in warm wet systems is debated) | 0.25 | 1.50 |
| 3. Explanatory scope | 7 (explains binding problem, temporal awareness) | 0.15 | 1.05 |
| 4. Parsimony | 5 (requires quantum gravity — not parsimonious) | 0.08 | 0.40 |
| 5. EAR | 5 (some confirmed predictions, some failures) | 0.10 | 0.50 |
| 6. Paradigm fit | 4 (requires revision of both quantum mechanics and neuroscience) | 0.07 | 0.28 |
| 7. Pragmatic asymmetry | 7 (false rejection could delay consciousness understanding by decades) | 0.10 | 0.70 |
| 8. Source track record | 9 (Penrose: highest caliber) | 0.05 | 0.45 |
| **TOTAL** | | **1.00** | **6.48/10** |

**Truth value:** 6.48/10 → above 0.42 threshold → **TRALSE+**. Tentatively accept; pursue specifically the mechanism plausibility question (the most underdetermined criterion) via Myrion Resolution.

This is richer than "credence 0.1–0.2." It tells you *why* the hypothesis is being tentatively accepted (source track record and explanatory scope dominate), *which* criterion is weakest (mechanism plausibility), and *what to investigate next*.

## 9. Conclusion

Bayesianism is not wrong. It is *incomplete*. It correctly identifies the direction of belief revision and the importance of priors, but fails as a universal theory because: priors are underdetermined for novel hypotheses; evidence types are not commensurable; and the pre-evidential zone — where most consequential epistemic work happens — is inaccessible to it.

Domain-calibrated intuitive inference (DCII), formalized within TI Sigma, corrects these failures by: (1) providing a structured pre-evidential checklist that operates before Bayes begins; (2) replacing the universal formula with domain-specific weight distributions learnable from demonstrated performers; (3) assigning TRALSE truth values that track the proposition, not just the agent's credence; and (4) providing an active inquiry protocol (Myrion Resolution) for indeterminate cases.

The deeper insight is that *intuition, refined by domain-calibrated checklists, is epistemically superior to a universal formula for most real-world inference tasks* — not because intuition is infallible, but because it naturally integrates the qualitative, the contextual, and the pragmatic in a way that no formula can replicate. The goal of epistemic practice is not to simulate Bayes' theorem mentally. It is to develop the kind of sharp, calibrated, domain-aware intuition that makes Bayes' theorem unnecessary.
