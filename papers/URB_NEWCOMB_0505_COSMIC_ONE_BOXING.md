## 1. Newcomb's Paradox and the Tipping Point Formula

Newcomb's Paradox proceeds as follows. A predictor of extraordinary but imperfect accuracy has already placed either $1,000,000 or $0 in Box B, depending on whether it predicted you would one-box (take only Box B) or two-box (take both boxes). Box A always contains $1,000. You now choose. The paradox: dominance reasoning says take both boxes (Box B's contents are already fixed, so you always do $1,000 better by taking A as well); expected-value reasoning says take only Box B (if the predictor is accurate, taking both predicts that B is empty).

The resolution of the paradox — which strategy is actually rational — depends critically on the predictor's accuracy p. Setting the expected values equal and solving:

```
EV(one-box)  = p × M
EV(two-box)  = p × m + (1 - p) × (M + m)

One-box wins when EV(one-box) > EV(two-box):
  p × M > p × m + (1 - p)(M + m)
  pM - pm > M + m - pM - pm
  2pM > M + m
  p > (M + m) / (2M)
  p > 1/2 + m/(2M)
```

This is the general Newcomb tipping-point formula. For the standard prize values (m = $1,000, M = $1,000,000):

```
p > 1/2 + 1,000 / 2,000,000
p > 1/2 + 0.0005
p > 0.5005
p > 50.05%
```

Three observations about this formula that will be important:

First, the tipping point approaches exactly 50.0% as M grows relative to m. When the prize asymmetry is infinite (existence vs. literally nothing), the required predictor accuracy collapses to a hairline above pure chance.

Second, the tipping point is entirely determined by the ratio m/M, not by the absolute values of the prizes. Only the relative value of the two options matters.

Third, the formula has a clean decomposition: p = 1/2 + (bias), where the bias = m/(2M). The bias quantifies how far above chance the predictor must be. The smaller m/M, the smaller the required bias.
