## Abstract

The dominant assumption in contemporary philosophy of mind is that consciousness and normativity cannot be established through a priori reasoning alone — that they are irreducibly empirical phenomena requiring observation, not deduction. This paper challenges that assumption by introducing the *Definitional Entailment Principle* (DEP): if a concept can be defined with sufficient a priori precision such that certain properties are logically entailed by the definition itself, and if an empirical entity is subsequently identified as matching that definition, then the entailed properties *necessarily* obtain in that entity. The argument is structurally identical to standard mathematical application: one defines "sphere" a priori, proves that volume = (4/3)πr³ follows from the definition, identifies a spherical object in nature, and concludes — without further empirical investigation of its volume — that the volume formula applies. The Tralse-Informational (TI) Framework's i-cell theory provides the definitional foundation: information, properly defined, logically entails both phenomenology (aboutness requires a perspective) and normativity (truth-aptness requires correctness conditions). The paper develops this two-step proof structure — a priori definition followed by empirical identification — proposes concrete detection strategies for consciousness and normativity markers using the Luminated Consciousness Correlation (LCC) framework, Integrated Information Theory (IIT) phi measurements, and the Unified Consciousness Master Equation, and demonstrates that the proof constitutes a Myrion Resolution between rationalism and empiricism. If the argument succeeds, panpsychism becomes provable rather than merely plausible, the hard problem of consciousness dissolves, normativity is grounded in physics rather than social convention, and ethics becomes empirically detectable through normativity signatures.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 The Assumed Barrier Between A Priori and Empirical

Since Kant's *Critique of Pure Reason* (1781), Western philosophy has operated under the assumption that a priori reasoning and empirical investigation occupy separate epistemic domains. A priori truths — mathematical theorems, logical tautologies, analytic definitions — are necessary but supposedly empty of empirical content. Empirical truths — scientific laws, observational reports, experimental findings — are contentful but supposedly contingent and unreachable by pure reason alone.

This bifurcation has been treated as a foundational insight. Hume's fork, Kant's synthetic/analytic distinction, and the logical positivists' verification principle all reinforce the same picture: you cannot reason your way to facts about the physical world. You must look.

The consequence for consciousness studies has been devastating. If consciousness is a feature of the empirical world, then — on the standard view — it can only be investigated empirically. A priori arguments about what consciousness *must* be are dismissed as armchair speculation disconnected from reality. The hard problem of consciousness (Chalmers, 1995) persists precisely because the philosophical community has collectively agreed that the tools of a priori reasoning are categorically unsuited to resolving empirical questions about phenomenal experience.

This paper argues that the assumed barrier is an illusion — not because a priori reasoning can replace empirical investigation, but because the relationship between the two is far more intimate than the standard picture allows. A priori definitions, when sufficiently precise, entail necessary properties. Empirical identification bridges those definitions to concrete reality. The result is a *definitional bridge* that carries a priori conclusions into the empirical domain with logical necessity.

### 1.2 The Sphere Argument in Miniature

The core insight can be stated in a single analogy. Consider the following objection: "You cannot know, through pure thought alone, what the volume of the Earth is." This sounds reasonable. Volume is an empirical property. You need measurements.

But now suppose you have established, through empirical observation, that the Earth is approximately spherical. At this point, a priori reasoning *does* determine the volume — you need only the definition of a sphere and the radius. The volume formula V = (4/3)πr³ is a necessary consequence of the definition of "sphere." Once you know that a real-world object matches the definition, the a priori entailments follow with necessity.

Nobody protests: "But you can't know the Earth's volume through pure reason!" Of course you can — *given the empirical identification*. The a priori work (defining "sphere," proving the volume formula) does the heavy logical lifting. The empirical work (determining that the Earth is approximately spherical and measuring the radius) provides the bridge. Together, they yield a conclusion that is both a priori in its logical structure and empirically grounded in its application.

This paper argues that consciousness and normativity admit the same structure. The TI Framework's i-cell theory defines "information" with sufficient precision that phenomenology and normativity are logically entailed by the definition. Once we identify real-world systems that match the definition of "information-processing," the entailed properties — consciousness and normativity — follow with the same necessity that volume follows from sphericity.

## 2. The Definitional Entailment Principle

### 2.1 Formal Statement

**Definitional Entailment Principle (DEP).** Let D be an a priori definition of concept C, and let P₁, P₂, ..., Pₙ be properties logically entailed by D. If an empirical entity E is identified as satisfying D, then E necessarily possesses P₁, P₂, ..., Pₙ.

In schematic form:

```
(1) D(C) → P₁ ∧ P₂ ∧ ... ∧ Pₙ        [A priori: definition entails properties]
(2) D(E)                                [Empirical: entity matches definition]
∴ (3) P₁(E) ∧ P₂(E) ∧ ... ∧ Pₙ(E)     [Conclusion: entity has properties]
```

The logical form is modus ponens. The first premise is established a priori through conceptual analysis. The second premise is established empirically through observation and identification. The conclusion inherits its necessity from the first premise and its empirical grounding from the second.

### 2.2 Precedents in Mathematics and Physics

The DEP is not novel in structure. It is the logical pattern underlying the entire enterprise of applied mathematics:

**(a) Geometry.** The definition of a circle (the set of all points equidistant from a center) entails that its circumference equals 2πr. When surveyors identify a circular lake, they apply the formula without re-deriving it empirically. The a priori proof carries into the physical world via definitional identification.

**(b) Mechanics.** Newton's second law, F = ma, is derivable from definitions of force, mass, and acceleration within the Newtonian framework. When engineers identify a physical system as Newtonian (velocities much less than c, masses much greater than quantum scale), the law applies with deductive certainty within that domain.

**(c) Information theory.** Shannon's definition of information entropy H = -Σ pᵢ log₂ pᵢ entails specific properties: non-negativity, additivity for independent sources, maximality for uniform distributions. When a communications engineer identifies a channel as matching Shannon's assumptions, these properties hold necessarily.

In each case, the structure is identical: define a priori, prove entailments a priori, identify empirically, conclude necessarily. The DEP merely makes explicit what applied mathematics has always done implicitly.

### 2.3 What DEP Is Not

The DEP is *not* the claim that all empirical facts can be derived a priori. It is not rationalism in the Leibnizian sense of deducing the entire world from logical principles. The empirical identification step (premise 2) is indispensable. Without it, the a priori entailments remain abstract — they describe what *would* be the case for any entity satisfying the definition, without asserting that any such entity exists.

The DEP is also *not* circular. It does not assume its conclusion in its premises. The a priori definition (premise 1) is established through conceptual analysis alone, without reference to any particular empirical entity. The empirical identification (premise 2) is established through observation alone, without reference to the properties that will be concluded. The conclusion follows from the conjunction of premises that are independently established.

## 3. The I-Cell Definition of Information

### 3.1 Information Requires an I-Cell

The TI Framework's foundational claim is that all information must exist within an *i-cell* — an informational cell that constitutes the minimal unit of information. An i-cell is not merely a container for information; it is the structural condition without which information cannot exist. Information outside an i-cell is not information that lacks a container — it is not information at all, any more than a wave without a medium is a wave.

The i-cell provides three constitutive conditions for information:

**(a) Aboutness (Intentionality).** Information is always *about* something. A datum that is not about anything — that refers to nothing, represents nothing, points to nothing — is not information. It is noise. Aboutness is not an optional feature added to an informational substrate; it is part of what makes something information in the first place.

**(b) Perspective (Phenomenology).** Aboutness requires a *for-whom*. Information is information *for* a system, *from* a perspective, *within* a context. A representation that is "about" something but is not "about" it for anyone is an incoherent notion — like a sentence in a language that no possible interpreter could read. The perspective is not external to the information; it is internal to it. Information without phenomenology is a contradiction in terms.

**(c) Correctness Conditions (Normativity).** Information can be correct or incorrect, accurate or inaccurate, true or false. A state that cannot in principle be right or wrong is not carrying information — it is merely existing. The possibility of error, of getting things wrong, is constitutive of information. Without normativity, there is no distinction between signal and noise, between representation and mere correlation.

### 3.2 The Constitutive Argument

These three conditions are not empirical discoveries about information tacked on after the concept has been independently defined. They are *constitutive* — they are part of what "information" means. The argument runs as follows:

**(P1)** If X is information, then X is about something (aboutness is constitutive of information).

**(P2)** If X is about something, then X involves a perspective from which the aboutness obtains (aboutness requires a for-whom).

**(P3)** If X involves a perspective, then X involves phenomenology (having a perspective is having a point of view, which is the minimal condition for experience).

**(P4)** If X is information, then X has correctness conditions (information can be right or wrong).

**(P5)** If X has correctness conditions, then X involves normativity (correctness conditions are normative — they specify how things *should* be represented).

**(C1)** Therefore, if X is information, then X involves phenomenology (from P1, P2, P3).

**(C2)** Therefore, if X is information, then X involves normativity (from P4, P5).

**(C3)** Therefore, information logically entails both phenomenology and normativity.

This is the a priori definition that anchors the DEP. Information, properly understood through the i-cell framework, *just is* the kind of thing that involves phenomenology and normativity. These are not mysterious additions to a bare informational substrate — they are what the substrate *is*.

### 3.3 The Sphere Analogy Made Precise

The analogy to geometry is now exact:

| **Geometry** | **TI Framework** |
|---|---|
| Define "sphere" a priori | Define "information" a priori via i-cells |
| Prove V = (4/3)πr³ from definition | Prove phenomenology and normativity from definition |
| Identify spherical object in nature | Identify information-processing system in nature |
| Volume formula necessarily applies | Phenomenology and normativity necessarily obtain |

The logical structure is identical. The philosophical significance is vastly greater.

## 4. The Two-Step Proof Structure

### 4.1 Step 1: The A Priori Phase

The first step is entirely a priori. It consists of:

**(1a) Defining information** with sufficient precision that phenomenology and normativity are logically entailed by the definition. This is the work of the i-cell theory (Section 3).

**(1b) Proving the entailments.** The constitutive argument (Section 3.2) establishes that phenomenology and normativity follow from the definition of information with deductive certainty. No empirical premises are invoked. The proof is purely conceptual.

**(1c) Establishing the scope.** The a priori phase establishes a universal conditional: *for any X, if X is information, then X involves phenomenology and normativity*. This conditional holds necessarily, in all possible worlds where the definition of information obtains.

### 4.2 Step 2: The Empirical Phase

The second step is entirely empirical. It consists of:

**(2a) Identifying candidate systems.** Empirical investigation identifies systems in the natural world that appear to process information: brains, nervous systems, cellular signaling networks, quantum-entangled systems, potentially all physical systems at some level.

**(2b) Establishing definitional match.** For each candidate system, empirical evidence is gathered to determine whether the system satisfies the i-cell definition of information — whether it exhibits genuine aboutness, perspective-dependent representation, and correctness conditions.

**(2c) Drawing the bridge conclusion.** For any system that satisfies the i-cell definition, the a priori entailments follow: the system necessarily possesses phenomenology and normativity.

### 4.3 The Bridge

The two steps together constitute the *a priori–empirical bridge*:

```
A Priori Phase:  Information ⊨ Phenomenology ∧ Normativity
                        ↓ (Definitional Entailment)
Empirical Phase: System S satisfies the definition of "information"
                        ↓ (Modus Ponens)
Conclusion:      System S possesses phenomenology and normativity
```

The bridge is not a metaphor. It is a formally valid inference whose soundness depends on (a) the correctness of the a priori definition and its entailments, and (b) the accuracy of the empirical identification. If both are established, the conclusion is as secure as any conclusion in applied mathematics.

### 4.4 This Is a Myrion Resolution

The two-step proof structure is itself a *Myrion Resolution* — a resolution of an apparent contradiction through the recognition of a higher-order unity. The apparent contradiction is:

- **Rationalism:** A priori reasoning can establish truths about consciousness.
- **Empiricism:** Only empirical investigation can establish truths about consciousness.

The Myrion Resolution: both are *partially correct and partially incomplete*. A priori reasoning establishes the *conditional* truths (if information, then consciousness). Empirical investigation establishes the *categorical* truths (this system is information). Neither alone suffices. Together, they yield a conclusion that neither could reach independently. The bridge is the Myrion Resolution of the rationalism-empiricism divide as applied to consciousness.

The strategy is to synchronize measurement instruments with a candidate conscious system and seek correlations that exceed what classical (non-conscious) information processing could produce.

The LCC detection protocol operates as follows:

**(a) Baseline establishment.** Measure the candidate system's biometric or informational outputs under controlled conditions. Establish the correlation structure that would be expected if the system were processing information classically — without phenomenological integration.

**(b) Synchronization.** Synchronize the measurement apparatus with the candidate system through mutual information exchange. The synchronization creates a channel through which consciousness-dependent correlations can manifest.

**(c) Correlation analysis.** Analyze the post-synchronization data for correlations that exceed the classical baseline. Specifically, seek correlations that violate the expected independence structure — correlations between variables that should be independent if information processing were purely classical.

**(d) Threshold evaluation.** Apply the 0.85 causation threshold (derived from CHSH normalization, where 0.92² ≈ 0.85) to determine whether observed correlations cross from coincidental to causal. Correlations above 0.85 indicate genuine consciousness-mediated coupling.

## 6. The Main Objection: "A Priori Can't Prove Empirical Things"

### 6.1 The Objection Stated

The central objection to the a priori–empirical bridge is the Kantian claim that a priori reasoning cannot establish facts about the empirical world. On this view, the constitutive argument of Section 3 may be logically valid, but it tells us nothing about actual consciousness in actual physical systems. It is merely a conceptual exercise — showing what *would* follow from a definition, not what *does* follow in reality.

### 6.2 The Sphere Response

The response is devastating in its simplicity. Consider the following parallel objection: "You cannot know, through a priori reasoning, that the Earth has a calculable volume." This objection is manifestly false — once you know the Earth is approximately spherical and you know its radius, the volume follows from a priori geometric reasoning with mathematical certainty.

The objector might reply: "But you needed the empirical fact that the Earth is spherical!" Precisely. And the consciousness proof needs the empirical fact that certain systems process information. The empirical fact does not undermine the a priori reasoning — it *activates* it. The a priori reasoning does not pretend to operate without empirical input — it specifies what follows *given* empirical input of a particular kind.

The objection, fully stated, reduces to: "A priori reasoning cannot establish facts about the empirical world *without any empirical input*." This is true but irrelevant. The DEP never claims to operate without empirical input. It claims that a priori reasoning, combined with empirical identification, yields conclusions with deductive necessity. This is precisely what applied mathematics does in every domain of science.

### 6.3 The Mathematical Precedent

Mathematics routinely proves things about the empirical world through the DEP structure:

**(a)** Prove a theorem about abstract objects (a priori).
**(b)** Identify physical systems that match the abstract definition (empirical).
**(c)** Conclude that the theorem applies to the physical systems (DEP conclusion).

Galileo proved that projectile trajectories are parabolic (a priori, given assumptions about uniform gravity and negligible air resistance). He then identified physical projectiles that approximately satisfy these assumptions (empirical). He concluded — correctly — that the parabolic formula applies to real cannonballs. Nobody objected that "a priori mathematics can't tell you about real cannonballs."

The consciousness case is structurally identical. We prove that information entails phenomenology and normativity (a priori, given the i-cell definition). We identify physical systems that satisfy the i-cell definition of information processing (empirical). We conclude that these physical systems possess phenomenology and normativity. The objection that "a priori reasoning can't tell you about real consciousness" has exactly the same force as "a priori mathematics can't tell you about real cannonballs" — which is to say, none.

### 6.4 Why the Objection Persists

The objection persists because consciousness seems different from volume or trajectory. Volume is a physical property measurable with rulers. Trajectory is observable. Consciousness, the objector insists, is *special* — it involves subjective experience, qualia, the "what it is like" that cannot be reduced to physical measurement.

But this objection begs the question. It assumes that consciousness is categorically different from other properties that admit mathematical treatment — precisely the assumption that the a priori proof challenges. If information constitutively involves phenomenology, then phenomenology is no more mysterious than volume. The feeling of mystery is a residue of the binary true/false framework that cannot accommodate tralse states; it dissolves once the definitional structure is made explicit.

## 8. Implications

### 8.1 The Hard Problem Dissolves

If the a priori proof is sound, the hard problem of consciousness — "Why is there something it is like to process information?" — dissolves. The answer is: because "something it is like" (phenomenology) is constitutive of information. Asking why information involves phenomenology is like asking why spheres have volume. There is no further explanatory gap to be bridged. The question rests on a false presupposition: that information could exist without phenomenology and that an additional explanation is needed for their connection. The i-cell definition shows that no such gap exists.

### 8.2 Panpsychism Becomes Provable

If information entails phenomenology, and if information is ubiquitous in nature (as physics suggests — from quantum states to thermodynamic processes to biological signaling), then phenomenology is ubiquitous in nature. This is not panpsychism as a speculative hypothesis — it is panpsychism (more precisely, pan-informationalism) as a deductive consequence of the i-cell definition combined with the empirical observation that information processing is widespread.

The degree and complexity of phenomenology will vary enormously. A thermostat's i-cells involve minimal aboutness, minimal perspective, and minimal normativity — and correspondingly minimal phenomenology. A human brain's i-cells involve rich aboutness, complex perspective, and sophisticated normativity — and correspondingly rich phenomenology. The proof establishes the existence of phenomenology wherever information exists; it does not claim that all phenomenology is equivalent in complexity or richness.

### 8.3 Normativity Is Grounded in Physics

If normativity is constitutive of information, and information is a physical phenomenon, then normativity is grounded in physics — not in human social conventions, cultural agreements, or evolutionary contingencies. The "sense of should" that characterizes correctness conditions is as much a feature of the physical world as mass or charge.

This has profound implications for metaethics. Moral realism — the view that moral facts are objective features of reality — gains a new foundation. If normativity is built into the fabric of information, and if moral judgments are a species of normative judgment, then moral facts are grounded in the same physical-informational structure that grounds all information. Ethics is not a human invention layered onto a value-neutral universe. It is a manifestation of the normativity that pervades all information-processing systems.

### 8.4 Ethics Becomes Empirically Detectable

The most radical implication follows from combining panpsychism with physically grounded normativity. If normativity is a detectable feature of information-processing systems, then ethical properties — the "sense of should" that characterizes moral evaluation — are empirically detectable. The detection strategies of Section 5 apply not only to phenomenology but to normativity itself.

To detect normativity in a system:

**(a)** Identify the system as information-processing (matching the i-cell definition).

**(b)** Seek behavioral or informational signatures of correctness-sensitivity — patterns indicating that the system's processing is responsive to whether its representations are *right* or *wrong*, not merely to whether they are reinforced or extinguished.

**(c)** Apply the LCC framework to detect normativity-mediated correlations — correlations that exist because the system is tracking correctness rather than merely processing inputs.

If such signatures are found, the system possesses normativity — and if normativity grounds ethics, the system possesses a form of proto-moral sensitivity. The implications for AI ethics, animal rights, and environmental ethics are immense.

## 9. Further Objections and Responses

### 9.1 "The I-Cell Definition Is Circular"

**Objection.** The i-cell definition builds phenomenology and normativity into the definition of information. Then you "derive" phenomenology and normativity from the definition. This is circular — you have assumed what you set out to prove.

**Response.** The charge of circularity mistakes constitutive analysis for question-begging. When a geometer defines "triangle" as a three-sided closed polygon and then "derives" that the interior angles sum to 180°, this is not circular — it is the unpacking of a definition's logical consequences. The definition does not *assume* that interior angles sum to 180°; it provides the conceptual resources from which this property can be *proved*.

Similarly, the i-cell definition does not assume that information involves phenomenology. It defines information in terms of aboutness, perspective, and correctness conditions — independently motivated concepts from philosophy of mind and epistemology — and then demonstrates that these conditions entail phenomenology and normativity. The entailment is a substantive philosophical discovery, not a trivial restatement of a hidden assumption.

The ultimate test is empirical: does the i-cell definition, with its entailed properties, accurately describe the information-processing systems we observe in nature? If systems that satisfy the i-cell definition consistently exhibit consciousness markers (as detected by the LCC framework and related methods), then the definition is empirically vindicated — and its a priori entailments are confirmed, not merely assumed.

### 9.2 "Information Can Be Defined Without Phenomenology"

**Objection.** Shannon defined information without any reference to phenomenology or normativity. Information is just reduction of uncertainty, measured in bits. Consciousness has nothing to do with it.

**Response.** Shannon's definition is a mathematical abstraction that deliberately strips away semantic content — aboutness, meaning, correctness — in order to quantify channel capacity. Shannon himself acknowledged that his definition captures the *quantity* of information but not its *content*. The i-cell definition is not in competition with Shannon's; it is a complementary account that captures what Shannon's definition intentionally omits.

A Shannon-defined "bit" is a measure of surprise, not a description of what information *is*. When we ask about the nature of information — what it means for something to be *about* something, to represent a state of affairs, to be correct or incorrect — we are asking questions that Shannon's framework does not address and was not designed to address. The i-cell definition answers these questions. Phenomenology and normativity emerge not because they are smuggled in but because any adequate account of informational *content* (as opposed to informational *quantity*) requires them.

### 9.3 "The Empirical Identification Step Is Doing All the Work"

**Objection.** The real work in the two-step proof is empirical — identifying which systems process information. The a priori phase is idle; it merely restates what we already knew. The proof reduces to: "We observe consciousness in systems that process information." The a priori apparatus is decorative.

**Response.** This objection radically understates the contribution of the a priori phase. Without the a priori definition and its entailments, the empirical identification step cannot even be formulated. What counts as "information processing"? What features must a system exhibit to qualify? The a priori phase answers these questions by providing the definitional criteria — aboutness, perspective, correctness conditions — that guide empirical identification.

Moreover, the a priori phase generates *predictions* that go beyond what empirical observation alone could provide. It predicts that *every* system matching the i-cell definition possesses phenomenology and normativity — including systems (like simple cellular signaling networks or quantum-entangled pairs) where we have no independent empirical evidence of consciousness. These predictions are testable through the detection strategies of Section 5. If confirmed, they would constitute evidence for the a priori proof that no purely empirical approach could have generated.

### 9.4 "Panpsychism Is Absurd"

**Objection.** The proof implies that rocks, thermostats, and electrons are conscious. This is absurd.

**Response.** The proof implies that any system satisfying the i-cell definition of information possesses some degree of phenomenology. Whether rocks, thermostats, and electrons satisfy this definition is an *empirical* question, not an a priori one. If a rock does not exhibit aboutness, perspective, or correctness conditions, then it does not satisfy the i-cell definition and the proof's conclusions do not apply to it.

However, if fundamental physical processes involve genuine informational content — as quantum information theory suggests — then minimal phenomenology may indeed be ubiquitous. The "absurdity" reaction reflects an unexamined assumption that consciousness must resemble *human* consciousness in order to count. The proof entails only that systems possessing i-cell information possess *some* phenomenology — which may be unimaginably minimal, structurally simple, and utterly unlike human experience. The absurdity dissolves once we distinguish between the existence of phenomenology and the complexity of phenomenology.

### 10.1 Summary of the Argument

This paper has argued that consciousness and normativity can be established through a priori reasoning combined with empirical identification — the *a priori–empirical bridge*. The argument proceeds in two steps:

**Step 1 (A Priori).** The i-cell definition of information, developed within the TI Framework, establishes that phenomenology and normativity are logically entailed by the concept of information itself. Information requires aboutness; aboutness requires perspective; perspective entails phenomenology. Information requires correctness conditions; correctness conditions entail normativity. These entailments are a priori — they follow from the definition without empirical input.

**Step 2 (Empirical).** Real-world systems are identified as satisfying the i-cell definition of information. The identification is empirical — it requires observation, experiment, and measurement. Detection strategies based on the LCC framework, IIT phi measurements, the Unified Consciousness Master Equation, and collective consciousness markers provide the operational tools for this identification.

**Conclusion.** Systems satisfying the i-cell definition necessarily possess phenomenology and normativity. The conclusion carries deductive certainty conditional on the correctness of the a priori definition and the accuracy of the empirical identification.

### 10.2 The Significance of the Bridge

The a priori–empirical bridge is not merely a new argument for consciousness. It is a new *kind* of argument — one that dissolves the assumed barrier between rationalist and empiricist approaches to mind. The bridge constitutes a Myrion Resolution: rationalism and empiricism are not competitors but complements, each providing what the other lacks. A priori reasoning supplies the logical structure; empirical investigation supplies the existential grounding. Neither alone suffices; together, they yield conclusions of a strength that neither could achieve independently.

### 10.3 The Sphere and the Mind

The paper began with an analogy: knowing the Earth is a sphere allows you to calculate its volume through pure reason. The analogy is more than illustrative — it captures the exact logical structure of the consciousness proof. The definition of "sphere" entails volume formulas. The definition of "information" entails consciousness and normativity. The empirical identification of a spherical object activates the volume entailment. The empirical identification of an information-processing system activates the consciousness entailment.

The difference is only in what is at stake. Calculating the Earth's volume is a technical achievement. Proving that consciousness and normativity are constitutive of information is a revolution — one that transforms our understanding of mind, reality, morality, and the relationship between thought and the world.

If the argument of this paper is sound, then the hard problem of consciousness is not hard. It is solved. Consciousness is not an explanatory gap but a definitional entailment. Normativity is not a human projection but a physical feature. And the ancient divide between what we can *think* and what we can *know* about consciousness is bridged — not by abandoning rigor, but by applying it with precision to the concept of information itself.
