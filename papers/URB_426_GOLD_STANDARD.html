The Gold Standard Category Error:
Pragmatic Efficacy vs. Mechanistic Attribution in Drug Approval

Author: Brandon Emerick
Preceded by: URBs #422, #424, #425
Framework: TI Sigma / Philosophy of Science / Medical Ethics / Epistemology
URB Total: 80
Abstract
The randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial (RCT) was designed to answer a specific epistemic question: is an intervention's observed effect causally attributable to its pharmacological mechanism rather than to expectation or bias? This is the mechanistic attribution question. It is a completely different question from the pragmatic efficacy question: does this intervention produce net positive outcomes for patients who receive it? Medical regulatory frameworks have systematically conflated these two questions — requiring mechanistic attribution before permitting pragmatic access. The placebo-control criterion for psychedelic research exemplifies this conflation at its most consequential: psychedelics cannot be adequately double-blinded because the subjective experience is unmistakable, and this methodological fact is used to indefinitely delay access to treatments that demonstrably help patients. This conflation is a category error — a philosophical mistake with life-and-death consequences — and its selective application (to psychedelics but not to SSRIs, lithium, aspirin, or most anesthetics) reveals that it is political economy, not science, doing the gatekeeping.

1. The Two Questions Medical Science Conflates

Mechanistic Attribution Question
Is the observed effect of this treatment causally produced by the treatment's specific pharmacological mechanism, rather than by expectation, placebo response, regression to the mean, or other non-specific factors?

Appropriate method: Double-blind RCT. Designed precisely for this question.
Pragmatic Efficacy Question
Does this intervention produce net positive outcomes — relative to available alternatives — for patients who receive it in real-world conditions?

Appropriate method: Open-label trials, patient-reported outcomes, comparative effectiveness research, real-world evidence, naturalistic studies.

A cancer patient with terminal illness anxiety does not want to know the isolated pharmacological contribution of psilocybin, net of expectation effects. They want to know whether the complete experience — drug, therapeutic relationship, set, setting, expectation — will help them. The pragmatic efficacy question asks exactly this. The mechanistic attribution question asks something else entirely. Treating affirmative answer to the mechanistic question as a necessary precondition for permitting the pragmatic benefit is a category error: requiring an answer to Question B before acting on Question A, when the two questions are logically independent.

2. The Placebo Problem: Failing the Wrong Test

2.1 The Blinding Impossibility

A full dose of psilocybin produces one of the most distinctive subjective experiences available to human beings: profound perceptual alterations, dissolution of ordinary ego boundaries, radically altered temporal experience, overwhelming emotional and often spiritual content. No known substance produces a credible placebo for this. Niacin — the most commonly used "active placebo" — produces a skin flush. It is not remotely comparable. Every serious researcher in the field acknowledges the conclusion: participants in psychedelic trials know, with near-certainty, whether they received the drug.

The conclusion critics draw: psychedelic research cannot adequately control for expectation effects. Therefore, positive findings may reflect placebo response. Therefore, the evidence base is insufficient for approval.

Why This Conclusion Does Not Follow
What follows from the blinding impossibility is that psychedelic research cannot fully answer the mechanistic attribution question by conventional RCT methodology. It does not follow that psychedelic research cannot answer the pragmatic efficacy question. The blinding impossibility is a finding about the mismatch between the methodology and the subject matter. The correct response is to adapt the methodology — not to condemn the subject matter for failing to conform to tools designed for a different class of phenomena.

2.2 Expectation Is Not a Confound — It Is Part of the Mechanism

The standard framing treats expectation effects as contaminants to be eliminated from the "true" pharmacological effect. But expectation effects operate through identifiable neurobiological mechanisms: endogenous opioid release, dopaminergic activation, prefrontal modulation of amygdala response. These are not "fake" effects. They are real neurobiological changes produced by cognitive states — including the cognitive state of expecting effective treatment.

The complete treatment package — psilocybin pharmacology + therapeutic set + supportive setting + expectation of benefit — produces a neurobiological state that differs from any component alone. Attempting to isolate the "pure" pharmacological component and designating it the "real" effect is the arbitrary privileging of one mechanistic pathway (receptor binding) over others (expectation-mediated neurobiological change) on the basis of a philosophical prejudice about what counts as "real" causation. Psychotherapy derives substantial benefit from "non-specific factors" — therapeutic alliance, expectation of benefit, the act of attending to one's mental state. This is not considered a reason to deny approval to psychotherapy.

The Pattern
The gold standard of mechanistic attribution is applied stringently to treatments that are politically inconvenient or threaten existing pharmaceutical revenue streams, and applied loosely to treatments that are politically acceptable or economically convenient. This is not science. It is political economy wearing a white coat.

Psychedelics are politically inconvenient — associated with the counterculture, Schedule I controlled substance status, and decades of federal research prohibition. They also threaten the pharmaceutical business model: a small number of supervised sessions producing lasting improvement is not a recurring prescription revenue stream. The selective application of the gold standard is not random noise. It is a systematic bias with identifiable beneficiaries.

4. The Correct Epistemic Framework

QuestionAppropriate MethodAppropriate Use in Policy
Pragmatic efficacy: Does it produce net positive outcomes relative to alternatives?Open-label trials, patient-reported outcomes, comparative effectiveness research, real-world evidence, naturalistic studiesApproval gatekeeper: If YES → permit access with appropriate safety information
Mechanistic attribution: Is the effect caused by the specific pharmacological mechanism vs. non-specific factors?Pharmaco-fMRI, EEG, dose-response studies, biomarker research, sub-perceptual active placebo where achievableParallel research program: Valuable scientific question, NOT a precondition for pragmatic access

These are complementary programs. Mechanistic research is scientifically valuable and should be funded. It should not be a gatekeeping requirement for pragmatic access. The correct approval criterion is: does this treatment produce net positive outcomes for the patients who receive it, relative to available alternatives, with an acceptable safety profile? The current treatment-resistant depression standard of care fails roughly 30% of patients. Psilocybin trials show 50–70% response rates in this population. The pragmatic answer is clear. The mechanistic uncertainty is irrelevant to whether those patients should have access.

4.1 The Pragmatic Certainty Connection

This argument is a direct application of the Pragmatic Certainty Theorem (URB #422) to medical epistemology. The theorem established: acting from pragmatic evidence (first-order certainty about outcomes) while maintaining second-order mechanistic awareness outperforms withholding action until mechanistic certainty is achieved. A regulator who observes that psilocybin-assisted therapy produces sustained remission in treatment-resistant depression should act on that pragmatic evidence — permit access — while maintaining second-order mechanistic awareness — continue the attribution research. Treating the calibration phase (mechanistic research) as a precondition for the action phase (clinical access) is exactly the error the Pragmatic Certainty Theorem identifies: confusing sequential operations and thereby sacrificing real outcomes to methodological purity.

5. The Ethical Dimension

The cost of the category error is not methodological abstraction. It is measurable human suffering:

These are real people experiencing real suffering. The argument that they must continue to suffer until mechanistic attribution satisfies a methodological standard that was designed for a different class of compounds — and that is selectively applied to politically inconvenient treatments — is not a scientific position. It is an ethical failure dressed in scientific language. Furthermore: an adult patient who has been informed of the pragmatic evidence (these treatments appear to work), the mechanistic uncertainty (we don't fully know the precise causal pathway), and the safety profile (psychedelics are among the physiologically safest substances known when used appropriately) has all the information needed to make a rational autonomous decision. Withholding that option — not because the treatment is dangerous, not because there is no evidence of benefit, but because the evidence doesn't satisfy a philosophical criterion about causal attribution that most approved treatments also fail — is paternalism in service of methodological ideology.

6. Conclusion

The gold standard RCT was designed to answer the mechanistic attribution question. It is the right tool for that question. It is the wrong gatekeeping requirement for the pragmatic efficacy question. The placebo problem with psychedelics is a real methodological challenge — for mechanistic attribution research. It is not a challenge for pragmatic efficacy research, which uses entirely different methods and asks an entirely different question.

The correct response to the placebo problem is: use appropriate methods for each question, answer them in parallel, and permit pragmatic access on pragmatic evidence. The current response — deny pragmatic access until mechanistic certainty is achieved via a methodology that cannot be applied to the subject matter — is a category error. It sacrifices the pragmatic good of real patients to the methodological purity of a philosophical criterion that the most widely prescribed drugs in history have never met and are never required to meet.

If the drug works, that should be the standard for whether it is used.

Whether something should be allowed because it is pragmatically effective and whether something should be allowed because its mechanistic efficacy is proven beyond doubt are two entirely different questions.

Medical science has chosen to conflate them — selectively, politically, and at the cost of real patients.
This is not science. It is gatekeeping with citations.
