## Abstract

The dominant operating philosophy of modern business is implicitly consequentialist: actions are evaluated by their outcomes, values are instrumentalized as tools for producing better results, and organizational health is measured by metrics of performance rather than by the quality of the human experience being created. This paper argues that this inversion — results first, values as means — is not merely an ethical problem. It is a strategic one. Drawing on Self-Determination Theory (SDT), virtue ethics, deontological moral philosophy, biblical wisdom, and the GILE framework (Goodness, Intuition, Love, Environment), we argue that the correct ordering is precisely reversed: values first, results as natural consequence. This is not anti-intellectual idealism. It is the structure that empirical psychology, philosophical tradition, and practical organizational experience all independently confirm produces the most durable and meaningful success. The problem with modern business is not insufficient data, analytics, or discipline. It is that an extraordinary amount of discipline is being applied toward the wrong primary criterion.

## 1. Introduction: The Inversion at the Heart of Modern Business

Every business has a theory of value, whether articulated or not. The theory governs what gets optimized, what gets sacrificed when tradeoffs arise, and what kind of human experience the organization creates in the process of pursuing its goals.

The dominant theory in contemporary business is this: results — revenue, growth, customer retention, shareholder return — are the primary criterion. Everything else, including values, culture, and the wellbeing of the people involved, is evaluated instrumentally: does it contribute to the results? If so, it is worth cultivating. If not, it is a luxury the business cannot afford.

This paper argues that this theory is inverted. Not slightly miscalibrated. Structurally reversed.

The correct theory, confirmed by psychology, philosophy, and scripture alike, is the opposite: values — specifically the four dimensions of GILE (Goodness, Intuition, Love, and Environment) — are the primary criterion. Results are the natural downstream consequence when values are primary. The pursuit of results as the primary criterion, by contrast, tends to corrode the values that would have generated the results organically, producing short-term gains and long-term institutional decay.

This is what Proverbs means when it says: *"Trust in the Lord with all your heart, and lean not on your own understanding; in all your ways acknowledge him, and he will make your paths straight."* This is what Jesus means when he says: *"Do not worry about tomorrow, for tomorrow will take care of itself. Seek first the kingdom of God and his righteousness, and all these things will be given to you as well."* These are not commands to be passive, naive, or unserious about the world. They are precise descriptions of the correct priority ordering — and the psychological research of the last 50 years has been independently confirming them ever since.

## 2. The Consequentialist Structure of Modern Business

Consequentialism, as a formal ethical theory, holds that the moral rightness of an action is determined entirely by its outcomes. The most familiar version is utilitarianism: maximize aggregate wellbeing, measure wellbeing by preference satisfaction or pleasure, and evaluate every action by whether it produces more of it.

Modern business is not formally utilitarian, but its operating logic follows the same structure. The primary question in nearly every business meeting, strategy session, and performance review is: *what produces the desired outcome?* Values, culture, and employee experience are discussed — extensively, in many organizations — but they are discussed as *inputs* to the desired output. "Our culture is what attracts top talent, which drives innovation, which drives revenue." "Customer satisfaction is what builds loyalty, which reduces churn, which improves margins."

In this framing, nothing has intrinsic value. Everything is a means. The customer is a means to revenue. The employee is a means to productivity. Culture is a means to retention. Even the product is, in this framing, ultimately a means to return on capital.

The problem is not that business should ignore outcomes. Organizations do need to be sustainable, and sustainability requires resources. The problem is the *direction of the causal arrow*. When results are primary and values are instrumental, the system is structurally unstable — because values instrumentalized in service of results can always be abandoned the moment a cheaper means to the same result becomes available.

This is precisely what happens: businesses that "value" their employees until a technology emerges that makes those employees redundant; companies that "value" sustainability until it becomes expensive; executives who "value" honesty until honesty becomes costly. These are not individual failures of character. They are the predictable output of a system where values are means, not ends.

## 3. The Deontological Alternative: Values as Ends in Themselves

Immanuel Kant's great contribution to ethics was the insistence that persons — and by extension, the values that constitute genuine personhood — have dignity, not price. *"Act so that you treat humanity, whether in your own person or in that of another, always as an end and never as a means only."* (Groundwork for the Metaphysics of Morals, 1785).

This categorical distinction between dignity and price is what modern business systematically erases. When an organization treats an employee as a means to productivity, or a customer as a means to revenue, it is violating what Kant identified as the most fundamental moral constraint: the prohibition against treating persons merely instrumentally.

But Kant's insight goes deeper than a prohibition. It is also a description of what genuine value is. Things with price can be substituted for each other — if something can be replaced by something else of equal or greater value, it has price. Things with dignity cannot be substituted — they are irreplaceable, and any action that treats them as substitutable is a fundamental misunderstanding of their nature.

The values that SDT identifies as Basic Psychological Needs — competence, autonomy, and relatedness — have dignity in precisely this sense. They are not substitutable by money. An employee whose autonomy is violated is not compensated by a pay raise — the violation is a different kind of harm entirely, one that occurs in a dimension money cannot reach. An organization that offers high salaries while systematically undermining competence and relatedness is offering a trade across incommensurable dimensions — and its employees know it, even if they cannot articulate why they feel something is missing.

The deontological case for values-first business is therefore not merely about ethics in the narrow sense. It is about ontological accuracy: correctly identifying what kind of things values are (ends, not means), what kind of things persons are (dignified, not merely useful), and building organizations that are structured around correct ontology rather than convenient inversions of it.

## 4. Self-Determination Theory: The Empirical Validation of Values-First

Self-Determination Theory (Deci & Ryan, 1985, 2000) is one of the most rigorously validated frameworks in all of psychology. Its central finding, replicated across thousands of studies in education, healthcare, sports, organizational behavior, and multiple cultures, is this:

**Human beings have three Basic Psychological Needs — competence, autonomy, and relatedness — and the degree to which these needs are satisfied in any context determines the quality of motivation, engagement, performance, and wellbeing produced in that context.**

This seems simple. Its implications are radical.

When Basic Psychological Needs are satisfied, people develop *intrinsic motivation* — engagement driven by genuine interest, meaning, and internalized values. This produces: higher quality performance, greater creativity, deeper learning, stronger persistence through obstacles, and greater wellbeing. These effects are large and durable.

When Basic Psychological Needs are frustrated — even in pursuit of well-intentioned external rewards — people develop *controlled motivation* — engagement driven by pressure, fear, contingent reward, or performance metrics. This produces: shorter-term compliance, reduced creativity, diminished intrinsic interest, and eventual disengagement or burnout. These effects are also large and durable.

The organizational implication is stark: results-first business, by instrumentalizing people and subordinating their psychological needs to performance metrics, *systematically produces controlled motivation*. It is disciplined, it is measurable, and it reliably degrades the very psychological substrate that produces the best performance.

### 4.1 The "Trying to Please Each Other" Problem

There is a specific pathology that emerges from results-first culture that deserves particular attention: what might be called the *pleasing-performance loop*. When results are primary, the most visible path to survival in an organization becomes the appearance of delivering results — which quickly mutates into the performance of doing whatever others want (managers, clients, investors) rather than the genuine pursuit of what actually works.

This is the business version of the "banality of evil" — not grand villains making terrible choices, but ordinary people doing whatever the system rewards, which turns out to be an endless chain of trying to appear successful to people who are themselves trying to appear successful. The entire system becomes self-referential, losing contact with the actual substance — the product, the customer's genuine need, the employees' real capabilities — that was supposed to be the point.

SDT's autonomy need is precisely what this structure violates. Genuine autonomy means action that originates from one's own values and judgment, not from the pressure to conform to external expectations. An organization that has substituted pleasing for genuine value-creation has systematically undermined autonomy at every level, producing a structure that looks productive but is increasingly decoupled from the reality it is supposed to serve.

## 5. The Biblical Wisdom Connection: Sophisticated Epistemology, Not Naivete

It would be easy to dismiss the biblical passages cited in this paper's introduction as pre-scientific religious prescription — comforting, perhaps, but not relevant to serious business thinking. This dismissal would be an error.

The Proverbs tradition is not anti-intellectual. It is, if anything, hyper-intellectual — deeply concerned with the conditions under which genuine understanding is possible and the structural failures of human cognition that prevent it. *"Lean not on your own understanding"* is not a command to stop thinking. It is a diagnosis of a specific cognitive failure mode: the collapse of understanding into the self-referential loop of one's own prior assumptions, values, and goals.

When Proverbs counsels trust in God over reliance on one's own understanding, it is counseling the same thing SDT identifies as the distinction between intrinsic orientation (values-led, open to reality) and controlled orientation (outcome-led, closed to disconfirming information). The person who has made results their primary criterion is, in a meaningful sense, leaning on their own understanding — they have decided in advance what matters and are filtering all incoming information through that pre-committed criterion. Reality consistently refuses to fully conform. The cracks emerge slowly, then catastrophically.

### 5.1 "Tomorrow Will Take Care of Itself"

The Matthew 6:34 passage is even more directly relevant to organizational behavior. The entire Sermon on the Mount context is about the correct ordering of attention and concern: *"Seek first the kingdom of God and his righteousness, and all these things will be given to you as well."*

This is an empirical claim about priority ordering, not a magical promise. It is saying: if you correctly identify what is primary and direct your full attention there, the secondary concerns will tend to resolve themselves. If you instead direct your attention primarily toward the secondary concerns (food, clothing, shelter — or revenue, margins, market share), you will find that anxiety about those concerns expands to fill the space, while the primary concerns (righteousness, genuine human flourishing) go unaddressed, and paradoxically the secondary concerns also fail to resolve.

The organizational parallel is precise. Companies that make values genuinely primary — not performatively, not as brand positioning, but as the actual operating criterion that governs decisions — tend to find that the results take care of themselves. The mechanism is not mysterious: genuine values attract genuine people, who do genuine work, who create genuine products, which produce genuine loyalty, which generates durable revenue. The chain is causal, not magical.

Companies that make results primary tend to find that the anxiety about results expands to dominate organizational life, while the values that would have produced the results erode, and the results themselves become increasingly difficult to sustain.

## 6. The GILE Framework: A Formal Structure for Values-First Business

The GILE framework provides a rigorous, four-dimensional specification of the values that belong in the primary position:

**G — Goodness:** the moral dimension; the commitment to act in ways that are genuinely beneficial, that reduce suffering, that create real value rather than the appearance of value. In business terms: does this decision actually help the people it is supposed to help, or does it help the organization appear to help them?

**I — Intuition:** the epistemic dimension; the commitment to access information that is not available through explicit analytical channels — through genuine listening, through pattern recognition that outruns conscious articulation, through the signal that arrives before the reasoning is complete. In business terms: what is actually happening that the metrics are not capturing?

**L — Love:** the relational dimension; the commitment to genuine care for the people involved — customers, employees, partners, communities. Not transactional care, not performative care, but the form of attention that actually registers the other person as a person. This is SDT's Relatedness need elevated to a primary operational value. In business terms: do we actually care about this person's flourishing, or are we acting as if we care because it serves our objectives?

**E — Environment:** the systemic dimension; the commitment to the health of the larger systems within which the organization operates — the industry, the community, the natural world, the culture. In business terms: are we contributing to or extracting from the conditions that make our own continued existence possible?

When GILE is primary, business decisions are evaluated first by whether they honor these four dimensions, and results are understood as the downstream output of getting the dimensions right. This is not naive: a business that is insolvent cannot pursue GILE at all. Sustainability remains a constraint. But there is a profound difference between treating results as a floor (necessary for survival) and treating results as the ceiling (the highest aspiration). GILE-first business maintains results as the floor while making the four dimensions the ceiling.

## 7. The Performance Paradox: Why Values-First Outperforms Results-First

The most counterintuitive finding of the organizational psychology literature is that values-first management — management that prioritizes psychological needs, genuine meaning, and intrinsic motivation over performance metrics — tends to outperform results-first management on the very metrics that results-first management is explicitly optimizing for.

Ryan & Deci (2000) summarized the evidence: intrinsic motivation predicts higher-quality performance, greater creativity, stronger persistence, and more positive affect — compared to controlled motivation produced by external rewards and performance pressure. Gagné & Deci (2005) demonstrated this across organizational contexts. Grant (2013) showed that prosocial motivation — helping others as a genuine value, not a strategy — predicts both performance and wellbeing more reliably than self-interested motivation.

The mechanism is not surprising once the inversion is recognized: when people act from intrinsic values, they bring their full cognitive and emotional resources to the work. When people act under controlled motivation (for the reward, to avoid punishment, to satisfy an external evaluator), they bring the minimum required to obtain the outcome. The difference in the quality of energy and attention brought to the work is substantial and persistent.

This is the performance paradox: the direct pursuit of performance tends to undermine performance, because it creates the controlled motivation that degrades the very capabilities performance depends on. The indirect pursuit of performance — through genuine values, genuine care, and the creation of conditions where Basic Psychological Needs are met — tends to produce superior performance precisely because it does not treat performance as the primary object.

The analogy to happiness is precise. Philosophers from Aristotle to John Stuart Mill have observed that happiness cannot be pursued directly — it is the byproduct of engagement with activities and relationships that have genuine value. Those who pursue happiness as their primary goal tend to find it elusive. Those who pursue genuine excellence, genuine connection, and genuine contribution tend to find that happiness follows. The same structural relationship holds between values and results in organizational life.

## 8. What GILE-First Business Looks Like in Practice

Saying "values first" without specifying what changes is insufficient. Concretely, a GILE-first organization operates differently in the following ways:

**In hiring:** The primary question is not "can this person deliver the required results?" but "does this person genuinely share the values that make our work meaningful?" Capability is necessary but not sufficient. Genuine alignment on Goodness, Love, and Environment is primary. Result-first organizations hire capable people who may be indifferent to the mission; GILE-first organizations sometimes hire less immediately capable people who are genuinely committed to it, betting (correctly, per the SDT evidence) that genuine commitment produces capability growth.

**In strategy:** The primary question is not "what market opportunity maximizes return?" but "what genuine human need are we positioned to address?" The market opportunities that are most durable tend to be those that align with a genuine unmet need — genuine Goodness. The misalignment between "profitable market opportunity" and "genuine human need" is one of the primary sources of organizational dysfunction and eventual failure.

**In management:** The primary instrument is not the performance metric but the conversation — the genuine, Intuition-informed, Love-directed conversation with the person whose work is being managed. What do they find meaningful? What is frustrating their competence? What is making them feel disconnected? These questions, consistently asked and genuinely heard, tend to produce more performance improvement than any metric-based performance management system.

**In partnership:** The primary filter is not "what can this partner do for our results?" but "do we genuinely respect and care about this person and their mission?" Partnerships built on mutual genuine care tend to be resilient through difficulty. Partnerships built on mutual utility tend to dissolve the moment the utility calculus shifts.

---

## 9. Conclusion: The Right Hierarchy

The argument of this paper is not that results do not matter. They matter enormously. A company that cannot sustain itself cannot pursue any values at all. The argument is about hierarchy: which comes first, which is the primary criterion, which is the lens through which everything else is evaluated.

The modern business world has the hierarchy inverted. Results are primary; values are instrumental. The consequences are visible everywhere: employee disengagement (Gallup consistently finds 70%+ of employees are not engaged with their work), organizational cultures that produce performance anxiety rather than genuine contribution, products that optimize for engagement metrics rather than genuine human flourishing, and an almost universal background sense that "something is missing" even in the most financially successful organizations.

The correct hierarchy — confirmed by SDT, by deontological ethics, by virtue ethics going back to Aristotle, by the biblical wisdom tradition — is the reverse: values primary, results downstream. GILE first. Goodness, Intuition, Love, and Environment as the four primary constraints within which all business decisions are made. Results as the floor, not the ceiling.

*"Trust in the Lord with all your heart, and lean not on your own understanding."* This is not a counsel of ignorance. It is a counsel of correct hierarchy — put the primary things first, and let the secondary things, which cannot be grasped by direct pursuit anyway, emerge from the integrity of the primary commitment.

*"Seek first the kingdom."* The kingdom is not a result. It is a condition — a way of being in the world that is characterized by genuine Goodness, genuine Love, and genuine alignment with what is most real. Business built on that foundation does not need to worry about tomorrow.

Tomorrow takes care of itself.
