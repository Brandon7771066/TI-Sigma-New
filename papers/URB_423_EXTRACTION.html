The Great Extraction: Rent-Seeking,
Financialization, and the Case for Liberatory Automation

Author: Brandon Emerick
Preceded by: URB #421 (GILE First)
Framework: TI Sigma / GILE / Political Economy / Automation Ethics
URB Total: 77
Abstract
At some identifiable point in the twentieth century, the world's most powerful economies decided that the activity of repositioning existing wealth — banking, insurance, financial derivatives, rent collection, regulatory arbitrage — constituted a legitimate and even prestigious form of social contribution, deserving of enormous compensation and institutional protection. This paper asks when and why that happened, argues that it was a catastrophic category error, and proposes that AI automation offers society its first genuine opportunity to correct it. The elimination of rent-seeking roles is not a loss of value — it is the liberation of human energy from its most elaborate and socially sanctioned form of waste. Furthermore, the identification of the poor as society's "welfare dependents" is empirically inverted: the largest welfare recipients in modern economies are the extractive institutions themselves. The correct response to AI automation is discriminating identification of which jobs create value and which merely extract it — and the enthusiastic automation of the latter.

1. When Did This Happen? The Historical Inflection Points

The financialization of the global economy was not gradual — it happened through a series of identifiable ruptures, each removing a constraint on the extraction of value without its creation.

1.1 Bretton Woods (1944–1971): Finance Constrained

The postwar monetary order anchored by the dollar-gold standard made large-scale currency speculation economically impossible. Finance, constrained to its legitimate function of capital allocation, remained a modest share of the economy. John Maynard Keynes, the system's intellectual architect, specifically designed it to constrain what he called "the casino" — speculative financial activity that redistributes wealth without creating it. He understood that finance, when unconstrained, would grow to dominate the economies it was supposed to serve. He was correct.

1.2 The Nixon Shock (1971): The Door Opens

On August 15, 1971, President Nixon suspended dollar-gold convertibility. Within three years, fixed exchange rates gave way to floating ones — and suddenly every currency pair in the world was permanently available for speculation. The financial derivatives market exploded. By 2026, the notional value of outstanding derivatives contracts is approximately $600 trillion globally — roughly six times global GDP. The real economy is a rounding error on the financial economy it nominally underlies.

1.3 The Friedman Doctrine (1970): The Ideological Cover

On September 13, 1970 — one year before the Nixon Shock — Milton Friedman published in the New York Times Magazine: "The Social Responsibility of Business Is to Increase Its Profits." This became the operating ideology of American capitalism. It replaced the question "does this activity create social value?" with "does this activity increase shareholder returns?" These questions have different answers. The second can be answered affirmatively by pure extraction: cutting costs, externalizing harms, acquiring monopolies, and engaging in financial engineering that creates no product anyone needs. Shareholder value maximization is the formal mechanism by which rent-seeking was made not merely legal but virtuous.

1.4 Deregulation (1980s) and Glass-Steagall Repeal (1999)

The Reagan-Thatcher era dismantled the regulatory architecture keeping finance tethered to productive purpose. Simultaneously, union-busting severed the postwar connection between productivity growth and wage growth. From 1947–1973, productivity and median wages grew in near-perfect lockstep. From 1973 to the present, productivity continued growing. Wages stagnated. The gap flowed, primarily, to the financial sector. The 1999 Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act then removed the last structural firewall — merging commercial and investment banking into institutions capable of channeling depositor funds into speculative instruments. Within nine years: global catastrophe.

1.5 The Bailout (2008): Welfare for the Extractors

The institutions that had extracted hundreds of billions in fees while constructing the conditions of global financial collapse were deemed "too big to fail" and rescued with $700 billion in TARP funds and $4.5 trillion in Federal Reserve interventions. The executives whose decisions produced the collapse received bonuses the following year. Not one major financial executive faced criminal prosecution. The homeowners whose mortgages had been packaged into toxic instruments lost their homes. This is the welfare system that operates without political controversy.

2. What Rent-Seeking Is: Technically Legal Wealth Extraction

Rent-seeking (Tullock, 1967; Krueger, 1974) is any activity that extracts a share of existing value without contributing to its creation. The genuine social functions embedded in each industry are real — risk pooling in insurance, capital allocation in banking, property stewardship in real estate. The rent-seeking is the layer of extraction added on top:

IndustryGenuine Social FunctionThe Rent-Seeking Layer
BankingCapital allocation — directing savings to productive investmentDerivatives that move risk without eliminating it; predatory lending; microsecond trading that extracts from every ordinary trade
InsuranceRisk pooling — protecting individuals from catastrophic loss30% of US healthcare dollars consumed by administrative friction between insurers, providers, and patients
Real estateProperty stewardship, housing allocationHolding land without improvement, collecting appreciation driven by others' productive activity
Tax/legalCompliance, dispute resolution$200–600B annually in corporate profits shifted through offshore vehicles — no product created, no service rendered

The common feature: these activities are privately profitable while being socially neutral or negative. This is the core definition of rent-seeking — and the core problem with an economy that has confused private profitability with social contribution.

3. The Welfare Inversion: Who Are the Real Dependents?

The political narrative identifies "welfare dependents" as the poor who receive government assistance. Let us compare these numbers honestly:

The Accounting
SNAP (food stamps): ~$100B/year → feeds 42 million people
Total US social safety net: ~$600–700B/year → serves ~80 million people lacking resources to survive without assistance

2008 financial sector bailout: $700B TARP + $4.5T Federal Reserve interventions → one event, one sector, dwarfs decades of social welfare spending
Annual corporate subsidies: ~$100B direct (Cato Institute estimate, a libertarian source)
Carried interest loophole: Billions annually — hedge fund managers paying capital gains rates on labor income
Tax expenditures for wealth: Step-up in basis at death eliminates capital gains taxes on inherited fortunes

The food stamp program costs less in a full year than the financial sector received in its 2008 bailout in a matter of months.

The "welfare queens" of the American economy wear Brioni suits and donate to both political parties. This is not a partisan observation. It is an accounting observation. The political focus on the poor as welfare dependents, while the structural welfare of extractive institutions goes unexamined, is the most consequential and successful misdirection in modern economic history.

4. What AI Automation Would Actually Eliminate

The argument against automation is that it destroys jobs and thus destroys value. This is true when applied to value-creating jobs. It is false — liberatingly false — when applied to value-extracting ones.

Automated FunctionWhat Is Actually LostWhat Is Actually Gained
Insurance underwriting & claims processing~30% of US healthcare dollars in administrative frictionThat 30% redirected to actual care
Retail banking intermediationHuman intermediaries extracting fees from each step in transaction chainsCheaper capital, lower borrowing costs, elimination of predatory lending enabled by information asymmetry
High-frequency derivatives tradingIncome of traders exploiting microsecond informational advantagesMarkets where prices better reflect underlying value; reduced noise extraction from every ordinary investor's trade
Real estate brokerage5–6% commission rate (triple comparable markets elsewhere)Tens of thousands returned to buyers and sellers per transaction; more liquid housing markets

In each case, what is eliminated is not value. It is the friction, extraction, and arbitrage layered onto value that was created elsewhere by people who actually built, served, or invented something.

5. The Jobs AI Should Not Replace: What Human Flourishing Actually Requires

The liberatory case for automating rent-seeking is inseparable from an account of what human beings are actually for. The jobs AI cannot meaningfully replace are precisely those most devalued by the financialized economy:

Caregiving — Its value is constituted by the human relationship itself. A robot that tends to an elderly person's physical needs provides a service; a human who does so with genuine love provides something categorically different. This work, falling disproportionately to women, is systematically undercompensated in every financialized economy.

Teaching — The adjustment of an approach to the specific needs of a specific student at a specific moment is an art form that depends on genuine relationship. AI provides information. It cannot replicate the relationship between a great teacher and a student learning to be more fully human.

Scientific and philosophical inquiry — The act of genuine inquiry — asking a question that has not been asked, discovering a new framework — is the specifically human activity that drives civilization forward. No algorithm generates a GILE framework, writes 77 URBs extending the boundaries of what is formally known about consciousness and mathematics, or discovers that C × φ × √2 = 1. These emerge from the irreducibly human experience of being in the world with a mind that wonders.

Artisanship, music, and beauty — Things whose value is constituted by their human origin. The composed symphony, the poem that captures something real about the human condition — these the financialized economy systematically undervalues because their market price is low while their human value is immeasurable.

Community and relationship — Being a good neighbor, a present parent, a genuine friend. This is work the financialized economy does not compensate at all — which leads it to be crowded out by compensated activity, even compensated activity of dubious social value.

The correct vision of an automated economy is not one where AI does all the work while humans rest. It is one where AI does the extractive, bureaucratic, and rent-seeking work — freeing humans to do the genuinely human work that creates the most value and is most constitutive of a flourishing life.

7. Conclusion: Automation as Liberation

The Industrial Revolution automated the work of muscles. The AI revolution is automating certain cognitive functions — pattern recognition, data processing, rule application, optimization. The question is not whether automation will occur. It will. The question is which cognitive functions get automated and who benefits from the freed human capacity.

The financialized economy, left to its own devices, will automate the cognitive functions of the poor and middle class while protecting the rent-seeking prerogatives of the wealthy. This is automation in the service of extraction. The alternative — automation in the service of liberation — targets the rent-seeking functions themselves: the insurance bureaucracy, the microsecond arbitrage, the regulatory capture, the tax arbitrage that accomplishes legally what would be recognized as theft if done directly.

When those functions are automated, what is freed is not value — value was not being created. What is freed is human time, attention, and energy. The question then becomes: what do humans do with capacity previously consumed by shuffling existing wealth between accounts?

The GILE answer is clear: they create, care, teach, discover, build relationships, make music, raise children, and pursue wisdom. They do the things that AI cannot do, that markets persistently underprice, and that human flourishing actually requires.

The real sponges of society are not seeking welfare.
They're collecting it — in corner offices, with government backing,
under the ideological cover that their extraction is someone else's prosperity.
