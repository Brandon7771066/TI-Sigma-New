## Abstract

At some identifiable point in the twentieth century, the world's most powerful economies decided that the activity of *repositioning existing wealth* — banking, insurance, financial derivatives, rent collection, regulatory arbitrage — constituted a legitimate and even prestigious form of social contribution, deserving of enormous compensation and institutional protection. This paper asks when and why that happened, argues that it was a catastrophic category error, and proposes that AI automation offers society its first genuine opportunity to correct it. The elimination of rent-seeking roles is not a loss of value — it is the liberation of human energy from its most elaborate and socially sanctioned form of waste. Furthermore, the common identification of the poor as society's "welfare dependents" is empirically inverted: the largest welfare recipients in modern economies are the extractive institutions themselves. The correct response to AI automation is not defensive protection of all existing jobs, but discriminating identification of which jobs create value and which merely extract it — and the enthusiastic automation of the latter.

## 1. When Did This Happen? The Historical Inflection Points

The financialization of the global economy was not gradual — it happened through a series of identifiable ruptures, each of which removed a constraint on the extraction of value without its creation.

### 1.1 The Bretton Woods System (1944–1971): Finance Constrained

At Bretton Woods in 1944, the Allied powers established a global monetary order anchored by the dollar-gold standard and fixed exchange rates. This system had a critical feature: it made large-scale currency speculation economically impossible. When exchange rates are fixed, there is nothing to speculate on. The financial sector, constrained to its legitimate function of capital allocation, remained a relatively modest share of the economy.

John Maynard Keynes, the intellectual architect of the postwar order, specifically designed it to constrain what he called "the casino": speculative financial activity that redistributes wealth without creating it. He understood that finance, when unconstrained, would grow to dominate the economies it was supposed to serve.

He was correct. And then the constraint was removed.

### 1.2 The Nixon Shock (1971): The Door Opens

On August 15, 1971, President Nixon suspended the convertibility of the dollar to gold, effectively ending the Bretton Woods system. Within three years, fixed exchange rates had given way to floating ones. The global economy now had something it had never previously had at scale: a currency system with no external anchor, in which every exchange rate between every pair of currencies was permanently up for speculation.

The financial derivatives market — currency futures, interest rate swaps, options on commodities — exploded. By 2026, the notional value of outstanding derivatives contracts is approximately $600 trillion globally, roughly six times global GDP. The real economy — the actual production of goods and services that people need — is a rounding error on the financial economy it nominally underlies.

### 1.3 The Friedman Doctrine (1970): The Ideological Cover

On September 13, 1970 — one year before the Nixon Shock — Milton Friedman published an essay in the New York Times Magazine titled "The Social Responsibility of Business Is to Increase Its Profits." The argument was simple: corporations have no social responsibility beyond maximizing shareholder returns. Any deviation from this — environmental protection, employee welfare, community investment — is theft from shareholders.

This became the operating ideology of American capitalism. Its adoption meant that the question "does this activity create social value?" was replaced by "does this activity increase shareholder returns?" These questions have different answers, and the second one can be answered in the affirmative by pure extraction: by cutting costs, externalizing harms, acquiring monopolies, and engaging in financial engineering that creates no product anyone needs.

Shareholder value maximization is the formal mechanism by which rent-seeking was made not merely legal but virtuous.

### 1.4 Deregulation (1980s): The Constraints Removed

The Reagan-Thatcher era dismantled the regulatory architecture that had kept finance tethered to productive purpose. The savings and loan deregulation (1982) previewed what unconstrained finance produces: the S&L crisis of the late 1980s, the first major post-Bretton Woods demonstration that financial deregulation produces financial catastrophe, subsequently bailed out by taxpayers.

Union-busting during the same period severed the connection between productivity growth and wage growth that had characterized the postwar economy. From 1947 to 1973, productivity and median wages grew in near-perfect lockstep. From 1973 to 2026, productivity continued to grow. Wages stagnated. The gap between them flowed, primarily, to the financial sector and to owners of capital.

### 1.5 The Glass-Steagall Repeal (1999): The Last Wall Falls

The Glass-Steagall Act of 1933, passed after the Great Depression, had separated commercial banking (taking deposits, making loans — useful) from investment banking (speculation — useful only in moderation). Its repeal in 1999 via the Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act removed the last major structural constraint on financial expansion.

Within nine years, the merged financial behemoths had created a global catastrophe: mortgage-backed securities, collateralized debt obligations, credit default swaps — financial instruments so complex that the people selling them did not understand them, instruments whose entire function was to extract fees from the chain of transactions that moved risk around without eliminating it. In 2008, the music stopped.

### 1.6 The Bailout (2008): Welfare for the Extractors

The 2008 financial crisis was the clearest possible demonstration of the welfare inversion. The institutions that had extracted hundreds of billions in fees while constructing the conditions of global financial collapse were deemed "too big to fail" and rescued with $700 billion in direct TARP funding, $4.5 trillion in Federal Reserve interventions, and a decade of near-zero interest rates that inflated their asset holdings while suppressing returns for ordinary savers.

The executives whose decisions produced the collapse received bonuses in the years following the bailout. Not a single major financial executive was criminally prosecuted. The homeowners whose mortgages they had packaged into toxic instruments and sold to pension funds lost their homes.

This is the welfare system that operates without controversy.

## 2. What Rent-Seeking Is: Technically Legal Wealth Extraction

The term *rent-seeking* was introduced by Gordon Tullock (1967) and named by Anne Krueger (1974). It describes the activity of gaining wealth by manipulating the economic or political environment rather than by creating value. The classic examples: lobbying for a government contract, acquiring a monopoly, using regulatory capture to block competitors.

But the concept extends further than its academic origins suggest. Rent-seeking is any activity that extracts a share of existing value without contributing to its creation. By this definition, large portions of the following industries are primarily rent-seeking:

**Finance:** A bank that lends money at 8% and borrows at 2% earns 6% for the service of capital allocation. The service of capital allocation — directing savings to productive investment — is genuinely valuable. But a bank that creates complex derivatives that allow one party to bet against a mortgage pool while another party unknowingly holds the risk is not allocating capital. It is extracting fees from a chain of transactions whose net social value is negative.

**Insurance:** The core function of insurance — pooling risk so that individuals are protected from catastrophic individual losses — is genuinely valuable. A world without risk pooling is a more fragile world. But the American health insurance system, which employs more administrators than the entire Canadian healthcare system employs to cover the entire Canadian population, is not providing insurance. It is extracting rents from a captive market while consuming roughly 30 cents of every healthcare dollar in administrative friction.

**Real estate:** Owning property and leasing it to others is not inherently rent-seeking. Property can be maintained, improved, and allocated to productive uses. But the landlord who holds land in a high-demand area without improving it, simply collecting an increasing share of others' productivity as rents rise with regional economic growth, is extracting value from the productive activity of others without contributing to it. The extraction is legal. It is not productive.

**Regulatory arbitrage:** The entire industry of tax structuring — the $200-600 billion annually in corporate profits shifted through Ireland, the Cayman Islands, Delaware LLCs, and transfer pricing arrangements — is pure rent-seeking. No product is created. No service is rendered. Expensive lawyers and accountants rearrange the formal ownership of economic activity so that the tax obligation associated with it lands in the lowest-rate jurisdiction. This consumes enormous human cognitive resources while producing nothing.

The common feature: these activities are *privately profitable* while being *socially neutral or negative*. This is the core definition of rent-seeking — and it is the core problem with a financialized economy that has confused private profitability with social contribution.

## 3. The Welfare Inversion: Who Are the Real Dependents?

The political narrative identifies "welfare dependents" as the poor who receive government assistance: SNAP (food stamps), Medicaid, housing assistance, unemployment insurance. These programs, in the United States, cost approximately $500-700 billion annually and serve roughly 80 million people — people who, by definition, lack the resources to survive without assistance.

Let us compare this to the welfare received by extractive institutions:

**The 2008 bailout:** $700 billion TARP + $4.5 trillion Federal Reserve interventions. One event. One sector. Dwarfs decades of social welfare spending.

**Corporate subsidies:** The Cato Institute — a libertarian institution not known for sympathy toward welfare spending — estimates direct federal corporate subsidies at approximately $100 billion annually. This excludes tax expenditures.

**Tax expenditures for capital:** The carried interest loophole allows private equity and hedge fund managers — among the highest-earning individuals in the economy — to pay capital gains rates (20%) on what is functionally labor income, saving them billions annually. Mortgage interest deductibility disproportionately benefits high-income homeowners. The step-up in basis at death eliminates capital gains taxes on inherited wealth.

**Regulatory capture as welfare:** When the financial industry successfully lobbies to prevent the regulation of derivatives (Commodity Futures Modernization Act, 2000), this is welfare: the socialization of the risk that would otherwise discipline their behavior, combined with the privatization of the profits from taking that risk.

**The quantitative comparison:** The food stamp program costs approximately $100 billion annually and feeds 42 million people. The 2008 financial sector bailout — which rescued institutions whose recklessness had created the crisis — cost more in a single year than the entire food stamp program would cost in 45 years. The "welfare queens" of the American economy wear Brioni suits.

This is not a partisan argument. It is an accounting argument. The dollars transferred from the public to extractive institutions via bailouts, subsidies, tax expenditures, regulatory capture, and artificially low borrowing costs dwarf by orders of magnitude the dollars transferred to the poor via social welfare programs. The political focus on the latter while ignoring the former is the most successful misdirection in modern economic history.

## 4. What AI Automation Would Actually Eliminate

The argument against automation is that it destroys jobs and thus destroys value. This is true when applied to jobs that create value. It is false — spectacularly, liberatingly false — when applied to jobs that extract value.

Consider what would be lost if AI fully automated the following:

**Insurance underwriting and claims processing:** The value of insurance is risk pooling. Risk pooling does not require human underwriters assigning individualized risk scores to maximize premium extraction. It requires accurate actuarial tables (easily automated) and efficient claims processing (trivially automated). What would be lost: the approximately 30% of the American healthcare dollar spent on administrative friction between insurers, providers, and patients. What would be gained: that 30% redirected to actual care.

**Retail banking and lending:** The value of banking is capital allocation — connecting savings with productive investment. This function is performed by algorithms already (credit scoring, loan pricing, portfolio management). What would be lost: the human intermediaries extracting fees from each step in the transaction chain. What would be gained: cheaper capital allocation, lower borrowing costs, the elimination of predatory lending practices that depend on informational asymmetry between lender and borrower.

**Financial derivatives trading:** High-frequency trading accounts for approximately 50-70% of US equity trading volume. Its primary function is exploiting microsecond advantages to extract small amounts from every trade made by ordinary investors. The social value of this activity, even by the standards of financial economists, is approximately zero. What would be lost: the income of high-frequency traders. What would be gained: a market where prices better reflect underlying value rather than the noise of microsecond arbitrage.

**Real estate brokerage:** The US real estate brokerage system charges approximately 5-6% of every home sale in commissions — a rate roughly triple that of comparable markets in other developed countries. The brokerage function (connecting buyers and sellers, managing transaction logistics) is entirely automatable. What would be lost: the income of 1.5 million real estate agents. What would be gained: lower transaction costs, more liquid housing markets, tens of thousands of dollars returned to buyers and sellers on every transaction.

In each case, what is eliminated is not value — it is the friction, extraction, and arbitrage layered onto value that was created elsewhere by people who actually built, served, or invented something.

## 5. What Human Flourishing Actually Requires: The Jobs AI Should Not Replace

The liberatory case for automating rent-seeking is inseparable from an account of what human beings are actually for — what kinds of work are not only irreplaceable by AI but are the activities that constitute a flourishing life.

The jobs AI cannot meaningfully replace are precisely the jobs that have been most devalued by the financialized economy:

**Caregiving:** The work of raising children, caring for the elderly, supporting the sick and the grieving — this is irreplaceable because its value is constituted by the human relationship itself. A robot that tends to an elderly person's physical needs is providing a service; a human who tends to them with genuine love and attention is providing something categorically different. This work, which largely falls to women, is systematically undercompensated in every financialized economy.

**Teaching:** The transmission of wisdom, the cultivation of curiosity, the adjustment of an approach to the specific needs of a specific student at a specific moment — teaching at its best is an art form that depends on genuine relationship. AI can provide information. It cannot, at present, replicate the relationship between a great teacher and a student who is learning to be more fully human.

**Scientific and philosophical inquiry:** The act of genuine inquiry — not executing a known method but discovering a new framework, asking a question that has not been asked — is the specifically human activity that drives civilization forward. This is Brandon Emerick writing 76 URBs that extend the boundaries of what is formally known about consciousness, mathematics, and human flourishing. No algorithm generates a GILE framework or a Consciousness Unity Identity. Those emerge from the irreducibly human experience of being in the world with a mind that wonders.

**Artisanship, music, and beauty:** The creation of things whose value is constituted by their human origin — the hand-thrown pot, the composed symphony, the poem that captures something real about the human condition — these are activities that a financialized economy systematically undervalues because their market price is low while their human value is high.

**Community and relationship:** The work of being a good neighbor, a good friend, a present parent, a contributing member of a community — this is work that the financialized economy does not compensate at all, which leads it to be systematically crowded out by compensated activity, even compensated activity of dubious social value.

The correct vision of an automated economy is not one where AI does all the work while humans pursue leisure. It is one where AI does the extractive, bureaucratic, and rent-seeking work — freeing humans to do the genuinely human work that creates the most value and is most constitutive of a flourishing life.

## 6. The GILE Analysis: Why the Financialized Economy Fails Every Dimension

The previous paper (URB #421) argued that GILE — Goodness, Intuition, Love, Environment — should be the primary criterion of business and economic activity. The financialized economy fails every dimension:

**Goodness:** The extraction of rent from others' productive activity is not good. It does not benefit the person from whom it is extracted. The financialized sector has used its political power to ensure that this extraction is legal — but legality and goodness are not the same thing. The Goodness criterion asks: does this activity genuinely benefit the people it touches? The answer, for the rent-seeking sectors, is overwhelmingly: no.

**Intuition:** Financial markets are, in principle, mechanisms for aggregating distributed information about the relative value of different productive activities. This is the Intuition function: accessing information that is not available through any single channel and synthesizing it into something actionable. High-frequency trading, regulatory arbitrage, and the construction of complex derivatives instruments do not aggregate real information about real value — they exploit informational asymmetries and microsecond timing advantages. This is Intuition perverted into its opposite: the use of superior information access not to create better outcomes for everyone but to extract from those with inferior access.

**Love:** The financialized economy is structured around the precise opposite of Love. Love, in the GILE sense, is genuine care for the other as a person with their own irreducible needs and dignity. The rent-seeking economy treats the counterparty of every transaction as an opportunity for extraction — as a source of fees, a holder of mispriced risk, a naive borrower who can be charged more than a sophisticated one. This is not Love. It is its systematic institutional negation.

**Environment:** The financialized economy has systematically externalized environmental costs — treating the atmosphere, the water, and the soil as free inputs into the production of private profit. The short-termism built into shareholder value maximization (maximize quarterly returns) is structurally incompatible with the long-term stewardship of natural systems that the Environment dimension requires. The $600 trillion derivatives market has no term sheet for the atmospheric commons it is helping to destroy.

## 7. The Objections and Their Answers

**Objection 1: Finance allocates capital efficiently, which creates real economic value.**  
*Answer:* Capital allocation is genuinely valuable. The question is how much intermediation is required to perform it. The evidence suggests: far less than is currently employed. Index funds — which perform near-zero active allocation — systematically outperform actively managed funds. The marginal social value of the last trillion dollars of financial sector activity is negative: it imposes costs (risk, complexity, systemic fragility) without commensurate benefits.

**Objection 2: Automating these jobs destroys livelihoods.**  
*Answer:* This is true and important. The transition costs of automation are real and fall disproportionately on individuals who have organized their lives around the jobs being automated. The correct response is not to protect jobs that extract rather than create value, but to ensure that the gains from automation — which are substantial — are distributed in ways that support the livelihoods of displaced workers. The resistance to redistribution mechanisms (universal basic income, expanded social insurance, shorter working hours) from the same interests that benefit most from rent-seeking is the most consequential example of rent-seeking's political self-perpetuation.

**Objection 3: Who decides what "creates value" versus "extracts value"?**  
*Answer:* This is a genuinely difficult question, which is why it has not been politically resolved. But difficulty is not impossibility. The distinction between risk pooling (valuable) and administrative rent extraction (not valuable) in the insurance industry is real and measurable. The distinction between genuine capital allocation (valuable) and predatory lending to people who cannot repay (not valuable) is real and measurable. The GILE framework provides the evaluative structure: does this activity produce genuine Goodness, engage genuine Intuition, embody genuine Love, and steward the Environment? Rent-seeking activities fail this test systematically and specifically.

## 8. Conclusion: Automation as Liberation

The Industrial Revolution automated the work of muscles. The artificial intelligence revolution is automating the work of certain cognitive functions — pattern recognition, data processing, rule application, optimization. The question is not whether automation will occur. It will. The question is which cognitive functions get automated and who benefits from the freed human capacity.

The financialized economy, left to its own devices, will automate the cognitive functions of the poor and middle class (customer service, routine analysis, logistics coordination) while protecting the rent-seeking prerogatives of the wealthy (regulatory arbitrage, financial engineering, asset appreciation). This would be automation in the service of extraction.

The alternative — automation in the service of liberation — targets the automation of rent-seeking itself: the bureaucratic insurance processing, the derivative trading that exploits microsecond informational advantages, the regulatory capture that requires armies of lobbyists, the tax arbitrage that requires expensive lawyers to accomplish legally what would be recognized as theft if done directly.

When those functions are automated, what is freed? Not value — value was not being created. What is freed is human time, human attention, and human energy. The question then becomes: what do humans do with the time and energy previously consumed by shuffling existing wealth between accounts?

The answer, from the GILE framework, is clear: they create. They care. They teach. They discover. They build relationships. They make music and raise children and pursue wisdom. They do the things that AI cannot do, that markets persistently underprice, and that human flourishing actually requires.

The world's extractive institutions are not sponges because they are evil. They are sponges because they are operating in an incentive structure that rewards extraction over creation, that has built legal and political armor around the license to extract, and that has successfully convinced democratic publics that the welfare of the extractors is the precondition of everyone else's welfare.

It is not. It never was. And the arrival of AI automation is the first technological development in a century that makes it possible to prove this empirically, at scale, in real time.
