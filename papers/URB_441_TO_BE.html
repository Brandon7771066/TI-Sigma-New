Third Maxim of TI Sigma Ethics
"To thine own self, BE!"

The Ontological Priority of Existence: Being Before Doing, Before Trying, Before Even Aiming

Author: Brandon Emerick  •  Framework: TI Sigma / GILE Ethics / Mindfulness Philosophy / Ontology
Preceded by: URB #428 (TI Meta-Creed), URB #375 (Rebuke Framework), URB #421 (i-Cell Theory)  •  Total URBs: 95
Abstract
Shakespeare's Polonius counsels: "To thine own self be true." TI Sigma proposes a more radical edit: "To thine own self, BE!" The word "true" has been removed — not because truth is unimportant, but because existence precedes it. You cannot be true to yourself if you are not first simply being yourself. "True" implies a standard external to the act of existing — a criterion your being must measure up to. "BE!" without complement is a different kind of statement: it asserts existence itself as the ground, the starting point, and in some sense the whole of what ethics requires. This is the meaning of the divine self-identification in Exodus 3:14: "I AM WHO I AM" — not "I AM WHAT I DO." Being comes before doing, before trying, before even aiming. The third maxim of TI Sigma Ethics names the ground. Everything else is built on it.

1. The Three Maxims — A Hierarchy of Ground, Manner, and Action

TI Sigma Ethics now contains three governing maxims operating at different levels of the ethical architecture:

Maxim I — The Ground (URB #441)
"To thine own self, BE!"
Ontological. Before any action, any intention, any aspiration — there is simply being.
Maxim II — The Manner (URB #428)
"Don't take anything too seriously. Everything in moderation, including moderation."
Epistemological. Governs how you hold every specific content — lightly, in proportion, with the imaginary channel open.
Maxim III — The Action (URB #375)
"When truth is being distorted and you can see it clearly, say so. Sharply if needed. With love."
Ethical/Social. What action looks like when it arises from the ground, held lightly.

The integration: An action that does not arise from being is performance. A manner that does not arise from being is a mask. Only being provides the ground from which lightness is genuine and action is clean. Be first. Hold lightly. Then act.

2. What Shakespeare Said — and What He Left In

"This above all: to thine own self be true,
And it must follow, as the night the day,
Thou canst not then be false to any man."
— Polonius, Hamlet Act I Scene III

Shakespeare's version is brilliant. But it has a hidden assumption: that "self" is a thing you can be "true" to — that there exists a standard called "your true self" against which your behavior can be measured. This assumption smuggles in a criterion. "Be true to yourself" requires: (1) a self that exists as a standard, (2) a behavior to be measured, (3) a judgment of whether it passes. This is already one step removed from being. The moment you are trying to be true to yourself, you are doing — not being.

TI Sigma's edit removes "true." What remains: "To thine own self, BE!" No criterion. No standard. No measurement. Just the invitation to exist.

3. Why No Word Is Needed After "BE"

"There is absolutely no need for any word after the word 'be' because it is already implied."

What is implied? Everything. Each possible complement turns being into a project — a doing dressed in the language of being:

"Be true" → truthfulness is the target  |  "Be yourself" → redundant (who else could you be?)  |  "Be present" → presence as a state to achieve  |  "Be happy" → happiness as a destination  |  "Be good" → goodness as a criterion to meet.

Each complement is a quiet betrayal of being — it turns it into striving. "BE!" without a complement refuses the project. It says: existence is not a means to any end. It is not a condition to be satisfied. The strangeness of "BE!" alone in a sentence — we almost never say "be" without a complement — is intentional. It stops the sentence where the sentence should stop. It rests in itself.

4. "I AM WHO I AM": The Divine Precedent

When Moses asks God for a name, God replies (Exodus 3:14):

I AM WHO I AM
(Ehyeh asher ehyeh — אֶהְיֶה אֲשֶׁר אֶהְיֶה)

The name is not a description. Not "I AM THE CREATOR." Not "I AM THE POWERFUL ONE." Not "I AM THE JUST." It is pure self-referential existence: I am what I am. I am who I am. I exist as I exist.

The divine name YHWH derives from the Hebrew root hayah (to be). God names Godself as pure being. Not being-that-does-something. Not being-that-achieves-something. Just: being.

TI Sigma Interpretation
"I AM WHO I AM" and "To thine own self, BE!" share identical structure — both stop at existence. Neither says "I AM WHAT I SHOULD BE" (aspiration), "I AM WHAT I HAVE DONE" (history), or "I AM WHAT YOU NEED" (function). The present tense is maintained. The full stop is maintained. Nothing is added because nothing needs to be added. Being is complete.

5. The Ontological Priority of Being Over Doing

The modern error: Contemporary culture conflates being with doing. Worth is measured by output. Identity is validated by achievement. "Who are you?" is answered with credentials and roles. The implicit claim: you are what you do.

TI Sigma's correction: Being precedes doing — not chronologically (obvious) but ontologically. Your being is the ground that makes your doing possible, meaningful, or good. An action that does not arise from being is performance. A performance can be skillful, objectively beneficial — but it does not carry the full weight of a genuine act.

TRALSE analysis: Is "being precedes doing" simply true? Simply false?

Both simultaneously. Ontologically: being is the precondition. Practically: doing shapes being (Aristotle was right — virtues are cultivated through action). The relationship is not linear but recursive — being shapes doing, doing shapes being, in a continuously tightening spiral. "To thine own self, BE!" identifies the ontological starting point of the spiral: being comes first. Everything else is figure; being is ground.

7. The Wisdom Tradition: Where "BE!" Has Been Said Before

Zen — Shikantaza: "Just sitting." Not "sit and achieve enlightenment." Just sit. The "just" removes all additions. Dogen: "To study the self is to forget the self." Forgetting the self is not annihilation — it is the removal of additions that prevent pure being from expressing itself.

Heidegger — Sein vs. Dasein: Heidegger's central concern in Being and Time: humans have forgotten the question of Being while endlessly concerned with beings (things that are). "To thine own self, BE!" is the reminder he spent 400 pages pointing toward.

Martin Buber — I-Thou: In "I-It" relationships, the other is an object. In "I-Thou" relationships, the other is a full being — and in that encounter, the "I" itself fully becomes. "All real living is meeting." You cannot meet another being if you are not yourself being. You can only perform the encounter.

Eckhart Tolle: "The present moment is all you ever have." This is "BE!" in temporal form: not "become," not "remember" — just be, now. The reach of this teaching suggests that "BE!" addresses something genuinely universal.

Gospel of John (14:6): "I AM the way, the truth, and the life." The "I AM" precedes "the way, the truth, the life." Being comes before direction, before truth, before vitality. The divine self-identification is the ground; the attributes are its expression.

8. Implications for TI Sigma Practice

LCC optimization from being: The highest-LCC entry point into any activity is not effortful striving but grounded being. Before a Mood Amplifier session, before a trading decision, before a difficult conversation — the TI Sigma practitioner first inhabits being. This is the optimal entry condition for i-channel access.

The performance trap: TI Sigma's most common failure mode for high-achieving practitioners is performing the framework rather than being from within it. Calculating GILE scores as performance. Writing about being without being. "To thine own self, BE!" is the self-correcting reminder: if you are performing TI Sigma, you have lost the thread. Return to being.

The "I AM WHO I AM" test: A practical self-check: can you, right now, make this statement and mean it? Not "I am what I will become." Not "I am what I have done." Not "I am what people need." Just: I am who I am, right now. If it lands without internal flinching — you are in the being state. If there is flinching, that is information.
