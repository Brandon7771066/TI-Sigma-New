Working Paper • BlissGene Therapeutics • March 2026

Confidence as a Strategic Asset:
Why Entrepreneurial Enthusiasm Is Not a Liability

Author: Brandon Emerick
Role: CEO, BlissGene Therapeutics
Date: March 2026
Keywords: Confidence • Self-Efficacy • Founder Psychology • Entrepreneurial Passion
Abstract
A persistent myth in business culture holds that confidence should be proportional to evidence — that enthusiasm must be "earned" through demonstrated results before it can be legitimately expressed. This paper argues the opposite: for ambitious ventures operating at the frontier of what is known and possible, confidence is not a reflection of the odds but a determinant of them. Drawing on entrepreneurship research, cognitive psychology, network theory, and historical case studies, we establish that high founder confidence is a strategic asset generating real advantages in talent acquisition, investor relations, partner engagement, and organizational resilience. We distinguish carefully between confidence (a functional internal state calibrated to task requirements) and recklessness (a process failure allowing proprietary information to move without legal protection). The former is not only appropriate but necessary. The latter is a separate, avoidable pathology that has nothing to do with enthusiasm level. The prescription for ambitious founders is not to temper enthusiasm — it is to keep these two categories rigorously distinct.

1. Introduction: The Confidence Criticism

Every transformative founder has heard some version of it. The quiet colleague who says the pitch was "too much." The advisor who suggests you "dial it back." The early team member who worries that you sound "overconfident" to potential partners. These critiques share a common underlying model: confidence should track evidence, and enthusiasm that outruns the current evidence is a liability.

This paper argues that this model, while sensible for routine professional activities, is inverted for ambitious entrepreneurship. In the domain of genuinely transformative ventures — companies pursuing outcomes that have not yet been demonstrated possible — the required confidence level is determined not by past evidence but by the demands of the future goal. Calibrating confidence to past evidence at the founding stage of an ambitious company is not intellectual honesty. It is a structural guarantee of underperformance.

The correct question is never "is this confidence warranted by the evidence?" The correct question is "does this confidence level produce the behaviors that make the goal achievable?"

2. Two Models of Confidence

2.1 Confidence as Display (The Evidence-Tracking Model)

Under this model, expressed confidence is a signal of estimated probability of success, updated by past performance. Confidence = f(track record). This model is appropriate when the domain is well-understood with reliable base rates, the task is routine and repeatable, and performance can be meaningfully predicted from prior performance.

It is the correct model for a factory manager estimating production output, or a professor assigning grades. It is not the correct model for a biotech founder pursuing a therapeutic no one has yet demonstrated.

2.2 Confidence as Tool (The Task-Calibrated Model)

Under this model, expressed confidence is calibrated to the requirements of the current task, not the historical track record. A higher-magnitude goal requires more confidence to sustain the behaviors necessary for success — regardless of prior evidence.

In this domain, confidence is a performance input — one of several variables that directly determine the outcome. Under the tool model, asking whether confidence is "too high" given the current evidence is a category error. The right question: does this person's confidence level produce the behaviors the goal requires?

3. Why Confidence Determines Outcomes in Ambitious Ventures

3.1 Talent Acquisition

Exceptional talent is almost always employed, highly sought after, and risk-averse in career terms. Research on charismatic leadership (Bass, 1985; Conger & Kanungo, 1998) consistently shows that follower commitment is predicted more strongly by perceived leader confidence than by the leader's actual expertise or the project's objective probability of success. People use the leader's confidence as a signal of information they do not themselves possess.

A founder who projects calibrated uncertainty signals: this is a reasonable person managing a difficult situation. The people they need are not looking for reasonable management. They are looking for conviction about something transformative. Enthusiasm is not noise in this signal. It is the signal itself.

3.2 Investor Relations

The venture capital literature consistently identifies "founder conviction" as among the top factors in early-stage investment decisions (Fried & Hisrich, 1994; MacMillan et al., 1985). At seed stage, evidence of product-market fit is by definition absent. Investors are primarily investing in the founder's judgment and resilience.

A founder who presents with measured, evidence-proportional confidence communicates: I will give up when things get difficult, because I am already partially convinced it might not work. A founder who presents with genuine conviction communicates: I will not stop. The former produces polite passes. The latter produces term sheets.

3.3 The Filter Function of Enthusiasm

There is a counterintuitive benefit to expressed enthusiasm: it functions as a filter for the right partners and a repellent for the wrong ones. Partners genuinely aligned with a transformative mission respond to enthusiasm with recognition — they feel it as kindred energy. They lean in. Partners primarily motivated by conservative risk management respond with skepticism. They lean back.

This filtering is valuable. A company pursuing a transformative mission does not want partners whose primary orientation is risk minimization — those partners become obstacles when the company inevitably needs to take risks. The authentic enthusiasm that screens them out is doing exactly what it should.

3.4 Organizational Resilience

Founder confidence is the primary determinant of organizational ability to sustain effort through inevitable setbacks. Bandura's (1977, 1997) foundational self-efficacy research shows that teams led by high-confidence founders demonstrate greater persistence after setback, greater creativity in problem-solving, and lower voluntary turnover during difficult periods. Under the evidence-tracking model, every failure updates confidence downward. Under the task-calibrated model, failures are information about what doesn't work — not about whether the goal is right. The latter model produces organizations that learn and adapt.

4. The Dunning-Kruger Reframe

The Dunning-Kruger effect (Kruger & Dunning, 1999) is typically cited as evidence that novices overestimate their ability. This is accurate. But the standard framing misses the adaptive function. At the founding stage of any ambitious undertaking, the founder cannot have evidence-based confidence because the evidence does not yet exist. The "inflation" between confidence and current ability is not a bias — it is a bootstrapping mechanism.

Without above-evidence confidence at the founding stage, the founder would not sustain the behaviors required to develop the evidence. The confidence advance is the mechanism by which the track record is eventually established.

Evidence-proportional confidence at founding stage is the surest route to a company that never develops the evidence that would justify higher confidence. The sequence is necessarily: confidence first, evidence second — not the reverse. This does not mean all confidence claims are equally valid. It means the appropriate level at founding is higher than a strict evidence-tracking model prescribes — and this is not a bug. It is how transformative companies get built.

5. The Crucial Distinction: Confidence vs. Recklessness

Concept Definition Status
Confidence Sustained belief in the mission's importance and the team's ability to pursue it, maintained independent of short-term evidence fluctuations, expressed to attract talent and partners Strategic asset — maximize
Recklessness Allowing proprietary information to move without contractual protection; failing to verify credentials of partners; bypassing due diligence processes Process failure — eliminate independently

These are independent variables. High confidence does not require or produce recklessness. Rigorous due diligence does not require or produce low confidence. A founder can simultaneously express genuine, high conviction about the company's mission and implement rigorous IP protection protocols and verify the credentials of new partners before fully engaging. The key word is simultaneously — these are not tradeoffs.

When a founder expresses enthusiasm in a welcome email, in pitches, in partnership conversations — that is the expression of exactly the conviction that the company's success requires. Dampening it does not make the company more professional. It makes it less compelling. Criticism of enthusiasm that conflates it with recklessness must be challenged, because the conflation is factually wrong and strategically harmful.

Three Factual Clarifications Often Missed in This Debate

First: the source of the vision matters. When a founder articulates a grand strategic vision — in this case, a Gene Therapy Alliance integrating BlissGene with the Innovative Genomics Institute to form a mainstream gene therapy umbrella organization — it is worth asking whether that vision was invented opportunistically or emerged organically from the partnership itself. In the specific case under discussion, the IGI collaboration concept was the partner's original and firm belief about what BlissGene would become. The founder was not fabricating enthusiasm to impress the other side. He was recognizing, amplifying, and articulating a vision the partner had already independently reached. This is precisely what strong founders do — they absorb the best thinking of the people around them and give it shape and velocity.

Second: IP was never actually at risk. A common error in due diligence discussions is conflating voluntary disclosure with forced disclosure. No external partner can compel a company to reveal its scientific secrets. The question is only ever about what the company chooses to share and whether those voluntary disclosures are contractually protected. An NDA governs the latter. It does not need to be a perfect institutional agreement to provide meaningful legal protection for information voluntarily shared under it. A signed NDA was in place before any sensitive information was exchanged — providing exactly the protection it was designed to provide.

Third: the communication itself contained explicit IP protection language. The email that was criticized as exhibiting "too much enthusiasm" contained the following sentence, written in its body by the founder:

"We agreed that we would save the complex details and secrets for future meetings with the IGI, after a further NDA with the organization as a whole is obtained."

This is not the language of a reckless communicator. This is a founder who simultaneously conveyed maximum enthusiasm about a transformative mission and explicitly reserved proprietary science for a future institutionally-protected context. Both were present in the same document, in the same sitting. Criticizing the enthusiasm without acknowledging this restraint is an incomplete reading of the evidence.

On the Limits of Skepticism

Initial skepticism about a new partner's credentials is not only appropriate but prudent. A co-founder who flags concerns about an unknown contact is doing exactly the right thing — and that instinct should be honored. The concern becomes professionally counterproductive when it persists after thorough vetting has been completed, credentials verified, documentation provided, and the founding CEO has reached a considered judgment. At that point, continued skepticism is no longer due diligence. It is a failure to trust the judgment of the person whose role is to make exactly these assessments. There is a meaningful line between protective caution and a sustained refusal to accept that a peer's judgment can be trusted. The former strengthens a company. The latter fragments it.

6. Historical Evidence

The claim that "people don't care about enthusiasm" is an empirical claim. The evidence from the most consequential companies in history runs directly against it:

Founder The Criticism at the Time The Outcome
Steve Jobs "Reality distortion field" — colleagues said he was delusional about what was technically possible, dangerously overconfident Apple; NeXT; Pixar. Changed four industries.
Elon Musk Multiple aerospace engineers told him his SpaceX plan was impossible; investors considered him naive at best World's most capable launch provider; first private company to reuse orbital rockets.
Jennifer Doudna Significant skepticism that biochemical CRISPR mechanism would translate to practical gene editing Nobel Prize 2020. Created the foundation for the gene therapy era — directly relevant to BlissGene's domain.
Richard Branson Described repeatedly as reckless, too enthusiastic, insufficiently serious for "real" business Virgin Group: 35+ companies, $5B+ revenue.

In every case, the confidence was not proportional to the evidence at the time. It was proportional to the goal. And the goal's demands — in talent, in partners, in resilience — required exactly that level of conviction.

7. The Asymmetry of Errors

Error Type Description Cost
Underconfidence Exceptional talent chooses a competitor; investors sense the founder will quit; team morale drops during setbacks; founder abandons pursuit that would have succeeded Affects every relationship, permanently
Overconfidence Small number of risk-averse partners self-select out; occasionally a path needs earlier correction Filters out misaligned partners who would have been problems anyway

In expected value terms, for an ambitious venture, high confidence is the dominant strategy. Underconfidence errors are systematically more costly — they affect every relationship the company builds and compound over time. Overconfidence errors primarily filter out relationships where misalignment existed anyway, and in doing so, they may be performing useful selection work.

8. Evidence from the Literature

Self-Efficacy (Bandura, 1977, 1997)

Bandura's foundational research established that self-efficacy — an individual's belief in their ability to achieve specific outcomes — is one of the strongest predictors of actual performance, across sports, academic achievement, and professional performance. Critically, self-efficacy influences performance through behavioral channels: high self-efficacy produces greater effort, greater persistence, more creative problem-solving, and better recovery from setback. This is the mechanism by which founder confidence produces results: not by changing the objective probability of success, but by changing behavior in ways that make success more likely.

Entrepreneurial Passion (Cardon et al., 2009, 2013)

Research on entrepreneurial passion shows it predicts venture survival and growth through multiple channels: it increases founder persistence (Cardon & Kirk, 2015); it is perceived by investors and attracts funding (Chen et al., 2009); it transmits to employees and increases team commitment (Cardon, 2008); and it improves creative cognition for opportunity recognition (Cardon et al., 2013). The same qualities that lead external observers to describe a founder as "too enthusiastic" are, in the research evidence, among the strongest predictors of venture success.

Charismatic Leadership (Bass, 1985; Conger & Kanungo, 1998)

Transformational and charismatic leadership research consistently shows that leader enthusiasm, confidence, and emotional expressiveness predict follower motivation and performance — independent of the leader's technical expertise or the organization's current results. Leaders who constrain their expressed enthusiasm to match current evidence are deliberately suppressing one of their primary leadership instruments.

9. Recommendations

For founders of ambitious ventures:

  1. Maintain confidence calibrated to the goal, not the current evidence. The goal's demands — the talent it requires, the resilience it demands, the partnerships it needs — determine the appropriate confidence level.
  2. Distinguish confidence from recklessness rigorously. Implement IP protection, due diligence processes, and legal safeguards. These are independent of confidence and both should be maximized simultaneously.
  3. Use enthusiasm as a filter. Partners deterred by genuine conviction about a transformative mission are self-selecting out of the right team. Let them.
  4. Resist the pressure to "dial it back." Advisors operating under the evidence-tracking model are applying the right model to the wrong domain.
  5. Recognize that confidence is a team asset. Talent pipelines, investor conversations, and partner relationships are all downstream of the conviction the founder projects.

10. Conclusion

The most consequential entrepreneurs in history were, at their founding stage, confidently pursuing things that the available evidence did not fully justify. This was not a character flaw they overcame. It was a strategic asset they deployed.

Confidence proportional to past evidence is appropriate for professionals managing known processes toward known outcomes. It is a structural liability for founders pursuing genuinely novel goals under high uncertainty.

The correct question is never "is this confidence warranted by the evidence?" The correct question is "does this confidence level produce the behaviors that make the goal achievable?" For ambitious ventures, the answer is almost always: raise it, not lower it — while keeping it cleanly separated from the independent discipline of due diligence and IP protection.

"The people who are crazy enough to think they can change the world are the ones who do." — Steve Jobs

Enthusiasm is not the enemy of rigor. The enemies of rigor are specific failures of process — information moving without contractual protection, relationships advancing without verification. Those failures must be addressed directly and urgently. They have nothing to do with the conviction and energy that every transformative founder has carried into every relationship that changed the world.
