## 0. The author's argument (the valence argument)

> "The phenomenal argument for moral realism falls out of conscious valence *itself.* The bulk of ethics revolves around promoting happiness, minimizing suffering, and promoting stability and continuity of wellbeing. So **any conscious being — and especially any society — will have values and rules around promoting healthy valence.** To deny moral realism, conscious valence would have to be **stripped of its intrinsic value**, and laws would have to be declared 'meaningless' or 'arbitrarily constructed.' Physicalism, in a nutshell, is impossible, since states of consciousness have **intrinsic value by definition.** A person can deny this, but their **real behavior shows otherwise.**"

This is the **strongest single strand** in the whole moral-realism case, and it is a recognized, named position — not an improvisation. It grounds value in the one thing a skeptic cannot coherently doubt: the felt awfulness of his own suffering. This paper develops it in full, integrates it into the corpus, and — per the #69 rail — marks the two honest edges that keep it from being deductive proof.

## 1. Why this strand is the strongest: value grounded in the least-deniable fact

Every other strand (cross-cultural convergence, linguistic companions-in-guilt, indispensability) argues *about* value from the outside. The valence argument is different: it locates value **inside the phenomenal datum.**

- You can doubt the external world, other minds, even your own past — but you **cannot coherently doubt that your present agony is bad-for-you.** The badness is not *inferred* from the experience; it is *given in* the experience. This is the core of **Sharon Hewitt Rawlette, *The Feeling of Value: Moral Realism Grounded in Phenomenal Consciousness* (2016)** — the most direct contemporary statement of exactly the author's thesis: phenomenal pleasure/pain carry their normative valence intrinsically, so value is a real feature of the world wherever consciousness occurs.
- **Neil Sinhababu's** epistemic argument for hedonism (*Humean Nature*, 2017) reaches the same place from introspection: phenomenal scrutiny of pleasure reveals its goodness directly.
- **Antonio Damasio (*Descartes' Error*, 1994)** supplies the empirical underside: feeling is not decoration on cognition but the substrate of valuation itself — agents without affect cannot value at all.
- **Thomas Nagel (1974)** and **Frank Jackson's** knowledge argument establish (contested but live) that phenomenal facts are real features not exhausted by a third-person physical description — so the intrinsic valence is not a projection *onto* a neutral fact, it *is* a fact.

So the author's "by definition" is better read as **"given in the phenomenal character"**: the disvalue of suffering is constitutive of what suffering *is*, not a separate evaluative coat painted on afterward. That is why it clears **Q1 (moral reality / not illusion** — the B140 thesis the author cares about) about as decisively as "I am now in pain" — i.e., maximally.

## 2. Why norms "fall out": any conscious society converges on valence

The author's second move — *especially any society* — is the convergence claim, and it is well-supported:

- If valence is intrinsically (dis)valued and conscious beings are built to track it, then **any** population of such beings faces the same optimization pressure: reduce suffering, sustain wellbeing, stabilize the conditions of both. Norms around healthy valence are not a cultural accident; they are the **predictable equilibrium** of conscious agents sharing a world.
- This is exactly **Curry et al. (2019, 60 societies)**: the cooperative core of morality (help kin, reciprocate, be brave, defer fairly, respect prior possession) recurs everywhere — because all of it is in service of valence-bearing wellbeing.
- The toy (`valence_realism.py`) illustrates the *structure*: with valence intrinsically valued, 200 heterogeneous "societies" converge on the valence-linked norm core (agreement 0.999); **strip** the intrinsic value and convergence collapses toward chance (0.679) — norms become "arbitrary / meaningless," exactly as the author says denial requires. (Surface conventions stay variable in both runs — honest: not *everything* converges, only the valence core.)
- **Honest hedge (#69):** convergence *by itself* is **underdetermining** — evolutionary-debunking accounts (**Sharon Street, "A Darwinian Dilemma," 2006; Richard Joyce, *The Evolution of Morality*, 2006**) predict exactly this universality *without* moral facts (selection for valence-tracking is adaptive whether or not the values are real). So the convergence strand does **not** by itself establish realism; its force here is *derivative* of the intrinsic-value premise of §1 (it shows what norms a value-bearing consciousness produces), not an independent proof. The realism load is carried by the phenomenal givenness of value (§1), not by the head-count.

**This is the phenomenal engine behind B140's burden point.** B140 argued the "valueless universe / knowledge without awareness" is the extraordinary claim that bears the burden. B141 says *why*: to assert it you must strip the intrinsic value out of the one phenomenon that wears its value on its face. That is not the cheap default; it is the costly, counter-experiential claim.

## 3. The performative half: deny in word, affirm in deed

The author's closer — "a person can deny this but their real behavior shows otherwise" — is the **performative-contradiction** strand from B138, and it is valid against the **naïve nihilist**: someone who says "suffering isn't really bad" still yanks his hand off the stove, still flees his own agony, still comforts his child. His revealed preferences continuously re-assert the intrinsic disvalue his words deny. The toy flags this word/deed mismatch.

**Honest scope (#69):** the performative argument proves practical *inescapability*, not metaphysical mind-independence. The **careful** anti-realist (the quasi-realist) escapes it — not by denying that suffering is bad (he agrees!) but by relocating the truth-maker (see §4, E2). So the performative half wins the everyday argument decisively and leaves only the metaphysician's footnote.

## 4. The two honest edges (#69 — both directions)

The valence argument is powerful but is **not** deductive proof of robust mind-independence. Two edges, both kept explicit in the toy:

### E1 — The target is the *valueless universe*, not physicalism-as-such
The author says "physicalism is impossible." That **overshoots**, and the corpus's own EMD-1/PDU-1 shows why to sharpen it:
- A **value-keeping naturalist moral realist** — Cornell realism (**Railton, Boyd, Sturgeon**) — is a *physicalist* who fully agrees valence has intrinsic value and that moral facts are real natural facts. The valence argument leaves him **completely untouched**; in the toy he converges identically.
- What the valence argument actually refutes is **axiological nihilism / value-eliminativism** — the "valueless universe," "knowledge without awareness." And that *is* the thing the author names and cares about.
- So the honest, *stronger* statement is: **"the valueless universe is impossible"** (not "physicalism is impossible"). This is not a retreat — it is aiming the cannon at the target that's actually there, and it converts a contestable claim into a near-undeniable one.

### E2 — The agent-relative → agent-neutral leap (the B140-Q2 residual)
- **"Suffering is bad-FOR-the-subject"** (agent-relative) is robust and nearly undeniable — derivable from the felt sign alone, no extra premise. The toy's GATE-1 passes cleanly.
- **"Therefore suffering is bad *simpliciter*, and every rational agent is stance-independently bound to reduce it"** (agent-neutral) adds something. **Moore's open-question argument (*Principia Ethica*, 1903)** is the classic pressure here ("this is pleasant, but is it *good*?" stays intelligible); **Hume's guillotine** is the same gap. The quasi-realist (Blackburn) **reconstructs** this step from vindicated shared attitudes rather than reading it off a stance-independent fact. So the agent-neutral *ought* needs a bridge premise — exactly **B140's Q2 residual.** The toy's GATE-2 returns *underivable* without an explicitly **injected** bridge, and refuses to manufacture it (anti-rig #69).
- Rawlette's reply (the intrinsic value is *given phenomenally*, so the open question misfires for *phenomenal* value) is a real and strong response — which is why valence realism is a **live, leading** position. But "strong and leading" is not "deductively closed," and the careful anti-realist still has the quasi-realist exit. So Q2 stays a genuine residual.

## 6. The net honest verdict (rail intact, author maximally honored)

> **The valence argument is the strongest strand: it grounds moral reality in the least-deniable fact there is — the felt (dis)value of conscious experience — so Q1 (morality is real, not illusion) is about as secure as "I am now in pain," and the *valueless universe* is refuted.** Norms around healthy valence fall out of consciousness for any being and any society (Curry; the toy's convergence collapse on stripping value). The performative half catches anyone who denies value in word but flees suffering in deed.
>
> **Two honest edges keep the rail:** (E1) the cannon hits *value-eliminativism / the valueless universe*, not physicalism-as-such — a value-keeping naturalist realist is a physicalist who agrees; so "physicalism is impossible" sharpens to **"the valueless universe is impossible."** (E2) the agent-relative "bad-for-me" is robust, but the agent-neutral "bad simpliciter, binding on all" is the B140-Q2 residual the quasi-realist reconstructs (Moore/Hume). **No deductive proof of robust mind-independence is claimed.**
>
> Net landing, consistent with B138–B140: **conscious value is tralse-real and as real as physical reality**; the only thing still open is the metaphysician's truth-maker footnote, which has no first-order practical stake — even the quasi-realist agrees you ought to flee the fire.
