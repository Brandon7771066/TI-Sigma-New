# PDU-1 — Physical-Definitional Instability ("Physical" Is Undefined)

**Pass 77 · Batch B127 · 2026-06-23 · CANDIDATE (NOT ratified; canonical principle count unchanged at 79)**

> Plain-language, secular. Written to the #69 brutal-honesty standard: framework-internal claims, contested positions, and illustrative examples are flagged as such; every named outside argument below is real and is reported accurately, not invented.

## The claim in one sentence

Physicalism — the thesis that *everything that exists is physical* — cannot give a fixed, non-circular definition of its own central word "physical," defends that word by quietly expanding it whenever a new phenomenon is forced on it, and at the one boundary where expansion is hardest (the abstract, and consciousness in particular) substitutes a relabeling ("epiphenomenon") for an explanation. The result is that physicalism is not a stable thesis at all — far less settled, and far less neutral, than its confident presentation suggests.

This does **not** prove that a non-physical reality exists. It shows that the *case for* physicalism is much weaker than advertised, and removes one of the main reasons people treat it as the obvious default.

## The argument, step by step

### 1. "Physical" is a moving target

Ask a physicalist what "physical" means and the usual answer is "matter and energy." But what counts as matter and energy has changed continuously across the history of science. The modern atomic model was only formalized in the early twentieth century. Quantum theory, antimatter, fields, and the rest of the modern menagerie were unknown not long before that. Each time something new was discovered, the inventory of "the physical" was simply enlarged to include it. So "everything is physical" decodes to "everything is whatever physics currently happens to recognize" — a definition that rewrites itself every few generations.

### 2. Hempel's Dilemma (a real, named argument)

This is not a fringe complaint. It is a well-known argument in the philosophy of mind, **Hempel's Dilemma**, named for the philosopher Carl Hempel and discussed widely in the literature (e.g., Crane & Mellor; the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry on *Physicalism*). It runs:

- **Horn 1 — define "physical" by today's physics.** Then physicalism is almost certainly *false*, because present physics is admittedly incomplete (the measurement problem in quantum mechanics is a century old and unresolved; dark matter and dark energy are ~95% of the universe's mass-energy and remain unexplained).
- **Horn 2 — define "physical" by some future, completed physics.** Then the thesis is *empty*: we have no idea what the final theory will contain, so "everything is physical" reduces to "everything is whatever the finished science will eventually include" — a promissory note, not a claim with present content.

Either horn defeats the *confident* version of physicalism. **(Hempel's Dilemma is reported here accurately; it is standard academic philosophy, not original to TI Sigma.)**

### 3. The relabeling double standard

The history reveals a revealing asymmetry. The label "non-physical" has repeatedly been used to *resist* a new phenomenon — to keep it out of respectable science — right up until the evidence forced acceptance, at which point the very same phenomenon was quietly reclassified as having been "physical" all along. Magnetism, electromagnetic fields, and radiation each made roughly that trip from suspect to respectable.

The lesson is uncomfortable for the physicalist: whatever we call "non-physical" today could perfectly well be filed under "physical" tomorrow, the moment it is understood and accepted. (A careful person might gesture at, say, intuition, or contested traditions like the chakra system — **illustrative examples only; their reality is exactly what is disputed, and nothing here asserts they are real.**)

But notice the *price* of that flexibility. If *anything* that ever gets confirmed automatically counts as "physical," then physicalism rules nothing out — it predicts nothing, forbids nothing, and can never be shown wrong. A thesis that absorbs every future discovery *by definition* has stopped being a substantive claim about reality and become a label we agree to keep reapplying. This is the same point as Horn 2 of Hempel's Dilemma, arrived at from the historical side.

### 4. The one wall the elasticity cannot cross: the abstract

There is a boundary where this absorb-everything strategy meets genuine resistance, and it is the one that matters most. The abstract features of reality — meaning, mathematics, and above all *consciousness* — are not obviously the kind of thing that can be built up out of matter and energy at all.

The useful technical distinction is between:

- **Weak emergence** — a higher-level pattern that is, in principle, fully derivable from its physical parts (a traffic jam from individual cars). This is the kind physicalism can comfortably accommodate.
- **Strong emergence** — a feature that is *not* derivable from the parts even in principle.

Felt experience looks, to many philosophers, like the one serious candidate for *strong* emergence: no amount of detail about which neurons fire seems to *entail*, by itself, why there is something it is like to undergo them. (This is the same gap as David Chalmers's "hard problem," and the weak/strong-emergence framing is also associated with his work. **The claim that consciousness is *strongly* emergent is a live and contested position, not a settled result — flagged as the framework's stance.**)

### 5. "Epiphenomenon" is a category move, not an explanation

Physicalism's standard reply to consciousness is to call it an **epiphenomenon** — a causally-idle by-product thrown off by physical machinery, like the whistle of a steam engine that does no work. But that is arguably a *category mistake*. The abstract is not one more item *inside* the physical inventory waiting to be located at some address; it is, prima facie, *its own kind of thing* — met first-hand and a priori, not read off a dial. Demoting it to a side-effect of matter does not explain it so much as change the subject. (Epiphenomenalism is a real position in philosophy of mind and carries well-known difficulties of its own, such as the problem of mental causation.)

## Falsifiers (OPEN)

- **PDU-1-F1.** A precise, fixed, non-circular definition of "physical" is supplied that is *neither* hostage to a particular stage of physics *nor* so broad it admits everything — defeating Hempel's Dilemma. (Candidate attempts exist in the literature — e.g., "the physical = the non-mental," or dispositional/structural accounts; PDU-1 predicts each either smuggles in current physics or collapses into vacuity.)
- **PDU-1-F2.** Consciousness (or another abstract feature) is shown to be *weakly* emergent — fully derivable in principle from a physical description — removing the strong-emergence wall.
- **PDU-1-F3.** The historical "resist-then-relabel" pattern is shown to be false or rare, *and* physicalism is shown to make genuinely falsifiable, borne-out predictions that give it real content — restoring it as a substantive thesis rather than an elastic label.
