## 1. We admitted some crystal claims were wrong, and fixed the papers

Earlier the project claimed a quartz-crystal "error-correcting" system had a magic number (0.309) baked into its geometry, and that crystals could *store* energy/information for huge spans of time — maybe even better than water.

We actually tested both. Neither held up:
- **The magic number was an accident.** It only appears if you arrange the math one way; the project's own table arranges it a *different* way, which gives a different number (about 0.248). So "0.309" was a coincidence from mixing two setups, not a real law. We retracted it. (The deep math result it leaned on — that a special 8-dimensional shape packs spheres perfectly — is still true and untouched. We just can't claim our crystal inherits it yet.)
- **Crystals can't store for long.** The physics says any stored charge leaks away in about 4–5 days at most — roughly 80 million times shorter than the "geological timescale" claim. So "crystals store better than water" is false. A short-lived (days) effect is still possible and worth testing, but the grand version is dead.

We also re-marked a crystal "phase ordering" result as **tunable** (you can get whatever order you want by changing one knob), and downgraded the "look, golden-ratio numbers everywhere!" pattern to *suggestive only* — because when we counted honestly, random numbers hit a "special" value about as often by pure chance.

**Bottom line:** we caught our own mistakes and wrote the corrections directly into the papers.

## 2. The Truth-Existence Tradeoff: why being 100% truthful backfires

Big idea: **truth is the one good thing you can't crank to the maximum without it costing you.** Past about 93% "truth/alignment," each extra bit of pure truth-maximizing starts costing you the ability to actually survive and function. So the best spot isn't 100% — it's high-but-not-maxed, with a little **slack**.

Three things fall out of this:

1. **Business & politics — why "imperfect but good" people win.** Someone who is *perfectly* transparent and never compromises gets eaten alive: they leak every plan, can't hold a coalition, can't negotiate. The people who thrive are *highly* trustworthy (that's the foundation that compounds) *plus* a little slack for timing, framing, and discretion. **Important catch:** this is NOT permission to cheat. The slack only works *on top of* a solid honest base. Slack without the base is just fraud.

2. **Christianity — a respectful bridge, not a TI Sigma belief.** *(Corrected per Brandon.)* This one is **not** something TI Sigma claims or derives — it's a diplomatic bridge offered *to* Christians, and it only makes sense if you already believe the Christian idea that "Jesus had to die to save us from our sins." TI Sigma actually **rejects** that idea: one person's self-sacrifice can't erase the real causes or consequences of human suffering, nobody gives a believable mechanism for how it would ("God does it" isn't an explanation), and Brandon doesn't even think Jesus *wanted* to be crucified. What the "perfectly imperfect" idea *can* do is give a Christian who already holds that belief a cleaner way to see why the sacrifice "had to" happen — it justifies the doctrine *for them*, without TI Sigma endorsing the doctrine itself.

3. **Silly vs. foolish.** The *exact same* goofy act is **charming** when a clearly-competent person does it and **stupid** when an incompetent person does it. Silliness is a luxury you earn by being solid first; without that foundation, it's just being a fool.

All three are really *one* rule: **earn the foundation first, then the little bit of slack becomes a strength.** Strip the foundation and the same behavior flips into a weakness. We named the rule the **GILE-Backdrop Discriminator (GBD-1)**.

## 3. We tested GBD-1 the hard way — and the first test failed

You said "both land" — but our rule is we don't get to *say* something passed until we actually run it. So we ran it.

**Test 1 (the money/payoff side).** We built a little simulated society of 4,000 agents playing repeated trust games, and checked: does "slack" pay off more for agents with a strong reputation?

- **The naive version FAILED — backwards.** When we let "slack" mean *getting caught cheating*, it **hurt the trustworthy agents the most** (they had the most to lose) and slightly *helped* the losers (nothing to lose). The opposite of what we predicted.
- **But that failure taught us the real rule.** When "slack" means *cheap discretion* — clever timing/framing that *doesn't* damage your reputation — it works exactly as predicted: the stronger your reputation, the more that slack pays.

So we *repaired* the rule with a condition: **the slack has to be "cheap" — it can't damage the foundation it's standing on.** Bonus insight: this proves "don't cheat" isn't just a moral nicety — getting caught cheating is *mathematically* most destructive to the most trustworthy people. We ratified GBD-1 in this corrected form.

**Test 2 (the judgment side).** We showed AI raters the same goofy act done by a competent vs. an incompetent person — identical wording, only the person's track record changed. Result: the same act scored **4.4 out of 7 from the competent person and 2.2 from the incompetent one** — a big, consistent gap across all 10 scenarios. **Confirmed:** people really do judge the identical act as smart-or-stupid based on who's doing it.

**Bottom line:** GBD-1 survived a real failure, got sharper because of it, and now passes on both the "does it pay?" side and the "how is it judged?" side.

## 4. The one-paragraph version of everything

We *earn* trust by being honest — including honest about our own mistakes (we retracted a crystal claim and reported a failed test out loud). Once that honest foundation is solid, a little slack — discretion, playfulness, even courting controversy — becomes a *strength*, not a liability. Take away the foundation and the exact same behavior becomes fraud, foolishness, or crankery. That single rule ties together how good people thrive, why a sacrifice story makes structural sense, why silliness can be brilliant, and why honesty is the project's whole engine.
