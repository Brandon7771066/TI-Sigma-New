This definition is purely algebraic. It tells us: if you square i, you get −1. It specifies a computational behavior. What it does not tell us is:

  • What kind of thing i is
  • What relationship it represents
  • What i has to do with consciousness or existence in general
  • Why i is the square root of −1, as opposed to an arbitrary algebraic symbol

    • The definition i = √(−1) treats i as if it were defined by its address on the number line — except it has no address on the manifest (real) number line, which is precisely why Descartes called it "imaginary."     2. The Eureka: i = √(Self × Not-Self)

    $$n \times (-n) = -n^2$$

    Take the square root of this:

    $$\sqrt{n \times (-n)} = \sqrt{-n^2} = n\sqrt{-1} = n \cdot i$$

    The result: n · i. Not a new type of number. Not a fiction. The result of asking "what is the geometric mean of a thing and its own negation?"

    The geometric mean of two quantities a and b is √(a·b) — the value that sits in the middle of their multiplicative relationship. When a = n and b = −n (the thing and its opposite), the geometric mean is n·i. The i-factor is what you get, at the unit level (n=1), when the geometric mean of identity and opposition is computed:

    $$i = \sqrt{1 \times (-1)} = \sqrt{\text{self} \times \text{not-self}}$$

    This is not a definition by algebraic rule. This is a definition by ontological content: i is the mathematical form of what it means to take the geometric mean of a thing and its own negation — to hold identity and opposition together in a single value without collapsing to either.

    This is precisely what tralseness means.

    $$\boxed{i = \sqrt{\text{self} \times \text{not-self}} = \text{tralseness at unit scale}}$$

    3. What Multiplication Represents in TI Sigma

    The eureka depends on a claim about the meaning of multiplication: multiplication represents reconciliation — the union of two things into a single value.

    This is not a metaphor. It reflects the mathematical structure of multiplication:

  • Addition combines two quantities by placing them in sequence (a + b = "a and then b")
  • Multiplication combines two quantities by scaling each by the other — each becomes the other's context (a × b = "a as measured by b, and b as measured by a, unified")

    • When you multiply 3 × 4, you are asking: what is 3 taken in the context of 4? What is 4 taken in the context of 3? The answer — 12 — is neither 3 nor 4 but the reconciliation of their mutual scaling.

    When you multiply a thing (n) by its opposite (−n), you are asking: what is n taken in the context of its own negation? The answer — −n² — is a negation of the thing's identity squared. The thing, when fully confronted with its own opposite and reconciled through multiplication, arrives at a negated and scaled version of itself.
