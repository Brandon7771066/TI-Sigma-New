# URB #813 — Consciousness as a Razor 🪒. Why "Stable + Middle-Range" Is Not "Balanced," and Why the Conventional Stability Metric Cannot Distinguish Adaptive Fluctuation from Pathological Fluctuation.

**Author:** Brandon Charles Emerick
**Date:** April 30, 2026
**Series:** Unified Research Brief #813
**Status:** Cognitive-diagnostic note. Identifies a three-way conflation in conventional discourse on "normal" consciousness: **stability** (low variance over time), **middle-range** (mean near the center of the possible range), and **balance** (appropriateness to the current activity) are treated as if they were the same property, when they are three structurally independent properties. ## 1. The insight in one sentence

> **"Stable + middle-range" consciousness is not "balanced" — balance is *appropriateness to the current activity*, which often requires a state that is neither stable nor middle-range. Conventional discourse privileges the wrong property because it implicitly assumes consciousness is non-functional. Consciousness is more like a razor 🪒 — meant to fluctuate significantly (but not wildly) to optimize problem-solving and living in general.**
>
>  — Brandon Charles Emerick, April 30, 2026, crediting Andres Gomez Emilsson (Qualia Research Institute) for the broader observation that normal states of consciousness are unfairly privileged.

## 2. The three-way conflation

Three properties of a person's consciousness over time get treated as if they were one property:

| Property | Definition | Independent of others? |
|---|---|:---:|
| **Stability** | Low variance of state over time. | ✓ |
| **Middle-range** | Mean of state near the center of the possible range. | ✓ |
| **Balance** | State matches what the current activity demands (appropriateness). | ✓ |

These are three independent dimensions. All eight binary combinations are realizable:

| # | Stability | Middle-range | Balance | Description |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | stable | middle | balanced | A person whose life consists of nothing but middle-arousal activities, hit accurately each time. |
| 2 | stable | middle | un-balanced | A person who sits at mid arousal all day regardless of activity. Gets the conventional "stability" credit; fails most activities by mismatched arousal. |
| 3 | stable | non-middle | balanced | A person whose life consists of one activity-type at a non-middle arousal (e.g., a long-distance trucker on a quiet route, sustaining moderate-low alertness). |
| 4 | stable | non-middle | un-balanced | A person stuck at a non-middle level inappropriate for most of their activities (e.g., chronically over-aroused in a low-demand role). |
| 5 | unstable | middle | balanced | A person whose activities themselves cluster around the mid arousal range and who hits the right state for each (common office-worker profile across study, meetings, and decompression). |
| 6 | unstable | middle | un-balanced | A person whose state varies around the middle but in ways uncorrelated with activity (e.g., randomly distractible across mid-arousal tasks). |
| 7 | unstable | non-middle | balanced | A person whose activities span the arousal range and who hits the right state for each. **This is the optimized profile.** Statistically the same shape on stability/range as #8. |
| 8 | unstable | non-middle | un-balanced | A person whose state swings widely without tracking activity demands. **This is the context-uncoupled profile** — what conventional discourse will reach for "unstable / bipolar" labels to describe. |

Profiles **#7 and #8** have **identical statistics on the stability axis** (high variance, off-center distribution) and **opposite verdicts on the appropriateness axis**. The conventional metric (variance over time) cannot tell them apart. The diagnostic that matters (activity-fit, or equivalently the correlation between state and activity-demand) can.

## 3. What "balance" actually means

The English word "balance" suggests middle-ness — a scale with equal weight on each side. That is the geometric metaphor. The functional concept is different: a balanced state is one that **matches the demands of the current activity**, which is a function of *context*, not of *statistics*.

A few activities and their approximate optimal arousal-and-attention states:

| Activity | Optimal state |
|---|---|
| Deep focused study / coding | High alertness, sustained attention, low emotional arousal — "calm focus." Yerkes-Dodson optimum for difficult cognitive tasks sits at moderate arousal with high focus narrowness. |
| Meditation (most types) | Low arousal, low cognitive load, open or single-pointed awareness. |
| High-stakes social engagement | High warmth, high activation, high emotional bandwidth. |
| Winding down for sleep / passive entertainment | Very low arousal, parasympathetic dominance. |
| Creative / divergent problem-solving | Moderate arousal, broad attentional scope (the *opposite* of focused-study state). |
| Crisis response | Very high arousal, narrow focus, time-compressed cognition. |

These optimal states **span the entire arousal range** and **differ qualitatively on attention and affect**. A person whose state never leaves a single mid-arousal point cannot match more than one or two of them. They are not "balanced" — they are *stuck*. They will under-perform their study, fail to actually meditate, miss the warmth required to socialize well, struggle to wind down, and be unprepared for crisis.

The conventional discourse calls this person "stable" or "balanced" anyway, because the only metric it applies is variance-over-time. The metric is technically correct (variance is genuinely low) and pragmatically wrong (the person is not well-served by their own consciousness).

## 4. The razor metaphor 🪒

Brandon's framing: consciousness is more like a razor than like a thermostat.

- A **thermostat** has a setpoint and resists deviation from it. Its job is to be stable. Variance is failure.
- A **razor** has a working edge that must be brought to the right place at the right time, with appropriate force, for the task at hand. A razor sitting permanently in its case is "stable" but useless; a razor swinging wildly without contact with the surface is dangerous; a razor applied with appropriate angle, pressure, and direction does its job. The thing that makes it a *good* razor is fit-for-task, not stability.

If consciousness is razor-shaped, then:
- Stable + middle is the razor in its case — safe, useless.
- Unstable + un-balanced is the razor swinging wildly — dangerous.
- Unstable + balanced is the razor applied with appropriate fit — optimal.

The conventional discourse evaluates the razor by case-time variance. That metric privileges *case-bound* over *applied*. It cannot distinguish *applied-correctly* from *swinging-wildly* — both are "out of the case," both look like instability. The conflation is built into the metric.

## 5. Why the conflation persists: the implicit non-functional assumption

Brandon's diagnosis: the conventional view treats level-of-consciousness as *something to be regulated*, where "regulated" means "kept in a narrow band." This makes sense only if consciousness is assumed to be non-functional — i.e., if no state of consciousness is more or less *useful* than any other state, so the only thing that matters is keeping it from causing trouble.

Once you grant that consciousness is functional — that different states *do* different things, and that activities have *optimal* states — the regulation framing becomes obviously wrong. The right framing is *fit-for-purpose at each moment*, with stability emerging as a side-effect when the activities themselves don't demand variation, not as a goal in itself.

This is the root of the conflation. The non-functional assumption makes "stable + middle" look like the goal; once dropped, it is revealed as one possible side-effect of a *small* range of activities, not a property to optimize for.

The non-functional assumption is also a notable framing in *parts* of conventional psychiatry, much of mainstream wellness culture, and most workplace expectations — though the strength of the claim varies sharply across these three. Mainstream wellness culture often does sell stability as a near-terminal value, and workplace expectations often do reward predictability over fit-for-purpose. Conventional psychiatry is **more nuanced than the wellness-and-workplace cases**: clinical diagnosis of mood disorders incorporates duration, impairment/distress, sleep disruption, psychosis/grandiosity/risk, functional capacity, and safety — not just variance from baseline. The criticism that lands is narrower: at the *measurement* layer (e.g., self-report mood charts, accelerometry-derived "stability indices," some screening instruments), the variance-from-baseline signal is heavily privileged and the activity-fit signal is rarely instrumented. The *clinical* layer downstream of those measurements is more sophisticated, but it inherits whatever the measurement layer captured. So the claim of this URB is best stated at the measurement layer, not as a global indictment of clinical practice.

Andres Gomez Emilsson's work at QRI (Symmetry Theory of Valence; the *energy parameter* of mind; *neural annealing*) is the most developed contemporary articulation of the alternative — that consciousness has a high-dimensional state space, that different states have different functional properties, and that the cultural privileging of "calm" is an artifact rather than a derived optimum. URB #813 is consistent with this lineage and adds the specific diagnostic point that the conventional stability metric is *incapable* of distinguishing adaptive fluctuation from pathological fluctuation.

## 6. The bipolarity / "instability" caveat — done honestly

The argument above does not claim that all psychiatric labels of mood instability are wrong. It claims something narrower and structurally precise: **the variance-over-time metric, applied alone, cannot distinguish adaptive amplitude from pathological amplitude.** Both produce high variance.

The diagnostic that distinguishes them is whether the **fluctuation tracks the activity / context demands**:

- **Adaptive amplitude**: the high arousal happens during the activities that benefit from high arousal; the low arousal happens during the activities that benefit from low arousal. The state-trajectory is a *response* to context.
- **Pathological amplitude (e.g., bipolar I/II manic / depressive episodes)**: the high arousal happens for endogenous reasons regardless of context; the low arousal does the same. The state-trajectory is *uncorrelated* with context demands, often *anti-correlated* with what the person needs at the time.

Both adaptive and context-uncoupled high-amplitude have high variance. Variance alone cannot distinguish them. **The correlation between state and context-demand can — and should be measured *in addition to* the existing diagnostic criteria, not as a replacement for them.**

This is the structurally honest version of Brandon's "but not wildly" qualifier. He is not arguing that wild swings are good. He is arguing that *appropriate* swings — high-amplitude when the activity demands it, low-amplitude when the activity demands that — are good, and that the conventional measurement layer mislabels these as the same thing as inappropriate swings.

The implication for diagnosis is **not** *"discard psychiatric categories"* — that would be irresponsible. The actual clinical machinery for mood-disorder diagnosis already incorporates duration, impairment, sleep disruption, psychosis, grandiosity, risk-taking, functional capacity, and safety, and those criteria do real work that this URB has nothing useful to say about. The implication this URB **does** make is narrower: **add context-correlation as an explicit additional discriminator at the measurement layer**, so that high-amplitude state trajectories that are tightly correlated with activity demands are not lumped together with high-amplitude trajectories that are uncorrelated. Adding this discriminator would reduce the over-flagging of high-amplitude functional people at the screening level, while leaving the rest of the diagnostic apparatus intact for cases where the additional clinical criteria (duration, impairment, sleep, psychosis, risk, etc.) also fire. That is a strictly additive improvement over a variance-heavy measurement layer; it does not replace anything that is currently doing useful diagnostic work.

## 7. The pattern across URBs #811 / #812 / #813

This URB is the third in a short series of results in the **same structural family** — though they are not literally identical. URB #811 sits a little further from #812 and #813: it is about conflating *limit-form syntax* with *raw-arithmetic value semantics* in formal mathematics, which is closer to a type-error than to a metric-selection error. URB #812 and #813 are tighter siblings — both are about applying the *wrong measurement procedure* to a thing whose value is determined by something the measurement does not capture.

| URB | Conventional procedure | What it actually evaluates | What we wanted it to evaluate | Mislabel projected onto |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| #811 | "Substitute and read off the form" | The syntactic shape of a limit at the boundary | Whether the expression has a value | The *expression* (called "indeterminate") |
| #812 | "Does the answer match the asker's expectation?" | E_Q-membership | C_Q-membership (correctness) | The *answerer* (called "miscommunicating") |
| #813 | "What is the variance of state over time?" | Statistical stability | Activity-fit (appropriateness) | The *person* (called "unstable") |

In all three, the conventional procedure is **technically valid as the procedure it actually is**: the substitution does identify the syntactic form; the expectation-match check does check expectation-match; the variance computation does compute variance. The failure is **applying the procedure as if it answered a different question than it does**, and **projecting the resulting mismatch as a defect onto the wrong target** (the expression, the answerer, the person) rather than onto the procedure-mis-selection on the labeler's side.

In TI Sigma 5VL+DT vocabulary, all three are **meta-procedural DT on the labeler's side** masquerading as **F on the answer's / expression's / person's side**. Brandon's phrase from the original observation captures the diagnostic exactly:

> *"Unstable" is technically correct yet pragmatically foolish.*

That is the family signature. Technically-correct application of the wrong procedure, called as if it were the answer to the right question. The fix in every case is the same: **identify which procedure should have been applied, apply that one, and use the conventional label only as a side-fact about the procedure that was actually run.**

## 8. Operational handle

Three usable rules-of-thumb that fall out of this URB:

1. **Distinguish "stable" from "balanced" in your own reporting.** When someone is described to you as "stable" or "balanced," ask: *do they hit the right state for what they are doing right now, or do they hit the same state regardless of what they are doing?* The first is balance; the second is just stable + middle. The conflation is so widespread that simply pulling them apart is informative.

2. **For variance-flagged behavior in yourself or others, run the activity-fit check before the pathology check.** Compute (informally): does the high-amplitude state happen during activities that benefit from high amplitude? Does the low-amplitude state happen during activities that benefit from low amplitude? If yes to both, the amplitude is adaptive and the variance flag is a procedure-mis-selection on the labeler's side. If no, run the pathology check honestly. Most variance-flagged people have not had the first check run on them.

3. **Be skeptical of any framework that scores consciousness solely on stability.** That framework cannot distinguish the optimal profile from the pathological profile (§6). A framework that cannot make the diagnostic distinction that matters most is not a useful framework for the question it claims to answer.
