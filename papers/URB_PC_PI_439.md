## 1. Mathematical Definition

$$\pi = \frac{\text{circumference}}{\text{diameter}} \approx 3.14159265358979...$$

**Equivalent characterizations:**

**(A) Geometric:** π = circumference/diameter for any circle, in any geometry (Euclidean).

**(B) Analytic:** π is the smallest positive zero of sin(x): sin(π) = 0.

**(C) Series (Leibniz):**
$$\frac{\pi}{4} = 1 - \frac{1}{3} + \frac{1}{5} - \frac{1}{7} + ...$$

**(D) Integral:**
$$\pi = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-x^2} dx = \sqrt{\pi} \quad \Rightarrow \quad \pi = \left(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-x^2} dx\right)^2$$

This last form is remarkable: π appears in the Gaussian integral — the area under the bell curve — even though circles seem unrelated to Gaussian distributions. This ubiquity is why π is called a "transcendental" constant in both the technical sense (not algebraic) and the colloquial sense.

**π is not φ:** A common misconception pairs φ and π as "the constants of beauty." They are both fundamental, but for entirely different reasons: φ governs self-similar proportion (1D length ratios); π governs circular symmetry (2D rotation). In TI Sigma, φ appears in the i-channel frequency scaling and C_EMERICK calculation; π appears in the rotation/phase calculations. They are complementary, not competing.
