## Abstract

Classical privation theory, from Augustine through Aquinas, holds that evil is not a substance but an absence of goodness. This insight, while directionally correct, has historically suffered from two weaknesses: it appears to trivialize extreme moral horror, and it provides no mechanism for grading evil or assigning responsibility. This paper extends privation theory within the Tralse-Informational (TI) GILE framework by developing a precise analogy between evil and empty space in physics. Empty space constitutes nearly all of physical reality; what we perceive as "stuff" is the rare exception clustered within and around the void. Evil, on this account, is moral empty space -- not a competing moral substance but the vast default condition of moral vacancy against which goodness is the localized, structured exception. The Existence Amplification Razor (EAR) is applied to eliminate the false dichotomy between "mere lack of goodness" and "intentional evil," revealing both as points on a single moral-energy gradient analogous to temperature. The paper introduces gradient-control responsibility (moral blame tracks control over GILE gradients rather than participation in evil essence), moral phase transitions (points where degree alone generates new normative obligations without new ontology), reciprocal abstract morality (the bidirectional causal relation between abstract GILE and concrete agents), and the tralse status of negative moral values. An Anselmian argument grounds normativity: moral holes are intrinsically imperfect, and if perfection must exist, then moral holes ought to be filled. This framework extends the ontological holes concept developed in prior TI work and provides a unified moral physics that dissolves the problem of evil, grounds normativity without voluntarism, and assigns responsibility without invoking evil as a positive force.

## 1. Introduction

The problem of evil has occupied moral philosophy and theology for millennia. If a perfectly good, omnipotent, and omniscient being exists, why does evil persist? Classical responses fall into two broad camps: those that treat evil as a genuine metaphysical substance competing with goodness, and those -- following Augustine and Aquinas -- that treat evil as a privation, the absence or corruption of good. The privation tradition has the stronger metaphysical credentials, but it has been repeatedly criticized for appearing to minimize genuine moral horror. Telling a victim of atrocity that their suffering is "merely an absence" seems both intellectually dishonest and morally obtuse.

This paper argues that the privation tradition is fundamentally correct but has been articulated with insufficient precision. The problem is not that evil-as-privation is too weak a claim; the problem is that it has been articulated without the right analogy. The correct analogy is not a hole in a doughnut (a localized absence in an otherwise solid whole) but *empty space in physics* -- the vast, pervasive, dominant condition that constitutes nearly everything, against which localized structure is the rare and remarkable exception.

In modern physics, empty space is not merely "nothing." It is the substrate of reality: quantum fields fluctuate within it, virtual particles emerge from and return to it, dark energy drives its expansion. What we perceive as solid matter -- atoms, molecules, objects -- comprises less than five percent of the universe's total energy content. The void is not an absence; it is the overwhelming presence against which matter is the deviation. Empty space is, in a very real sense, what is most real about the physical universe.

Evil, this paper contends, stands in the same relation to the moral dimension. Evil is not a localized defect in an otherwise good moral fabric. It is the vast moral vacuum that constitutes the default condition -- the moral empty space through which, within which, and against which goodness is the structured, localized, hard-won exception. GILE (Goodness, Intuition, Love, Existence) represents moral substance; evil represents its absence across the moral topology. The moral universe, like the physical one, is mostly empty.

This reframing changes everything. It dissolves the apparent tension between "trivial absence" and "horrifying reality." It eliminates the need for separate ontological categories of passive and active evil. And it provides the foundation for a gradient-based ethics of responsibility that tracks agents' control over moral density rather than their participation in evil substance.

## 2. Classical Privation Theory and Its Limitations

### 2.1 Augustine and the Privatio Boni

Augustine of Hippo, confronting the Manichaean dualism he had once embraced, argued that evil is not a substance (*substantia*) but a privation of good (*privatio boni*). God, being perfectly good, created a perfectly good world. Evil entered not as a created thing but as the corruption or diminishment of created goodness through the misuse of free will. "Evil has no positive nature," Augustine wrote; "the loss of good has received the name 'evil'" (*City of God*, XI.9).

This move was brilliant in several respects. It preserved divine omnipotence (God did not create evil), divine goodness (God's creation is good), and creaturely freedom (evil arises from free choice). It also provided a metaphysical framework: evil is parasitic on goodness in the way that darkness is parasitic on light, or sickness on health.

### 2.2 Aquinas and the Refinement

Thomas Aquinas refined Augustine's account by distinguishing between *privatio* (the absence of a good that ought to be present) and mere *negatio* (the absence of a good that need not be present). A stone's inability to see is a negation, not a privation -- stones are not the kind of thing that ought to see. A person's blindness is a privation -- persons are the kind of thing that ought to see. Evil, for Aquinas, is specifically privation: the absence of a good that *should* be present given the nature of the thing in question.

### 2.3 The Persistent Objection

Despite its metaphysical elegance, privation theory faces a persistent and powerful objection: it seems to trivialize evil. If evil is "merely" an absence, how do we account for the experienced reality of cruelty, malice, torture, and genocide? The objection takes several forms:

**(a) The phenomenological objection.** Evil does not *feel* like an absence. A torture victim does not experience "the absence of kindness." They experience the overwhelming *presence* of something terrible. Privation theory seems to misdescribe the phenomenology.

**(b) The intentionality objection.** Some evil is deliberately pursued. A torturer does not merely fail to be good; they actively intend harm. The difference between neglect and cruelty seems ontological, not merely quantitative. Privation theory appears to collapse this distinction.

**(c) The explanatory objection.** If evil is merely an absence, what explains its causal efficacy? Absences do not cause things. Holes in doughnuts do not punch people. But evil plainly *does things* -- it corrupts, destroys, and propagates. Privation theory seems explanatorily inadequate.

These objections have led many philosophers to abandon privation theory in favor of accounts that grant evil some form of positive ontological status. This paper argues that the abandonment is premature. The objections succeed against a thin version of privation theory but fail against the extended version developed here.

## 3. The Empty Space Analogy

### 3.1 Empty Space as the Dominant Physical Reality

Modern cosmology has revealed that what we perceive as "reality" -- matter, energy, structure -- is the rare exception in a universe dominated by emptiness. The observable universe is approximately 68% dark energy, 27% dark matter, and only 5% ordinary (baryonic) matter. Within ordinary matter itself, atoms are overwhelmingly empty space: the nucleus occupies roughly one trillionth of the atom's volume. Matter is structure suspended in void; structure is the deviation, not the norm.

Crucially, empty space is not inert. Quantum field theory reveals that the vacuum teems with virtual particle-antiparticle pairs, zero-point energy, and field fluctuations. The vacuum has properties -- it can be polarized, it exerts Casimir forces, it drives cosmological expansion. Empty space is not "nothing"; it is the most fundamental and pervasive physical reality, against which localized matter-energy is the structured exception.

### 3.2 Evil as Moral Empty Space

The analogy to the moral dimension is precise. Evil is moral empty space -- the vast, pervasive, default condition of moral vacancy against which goodness is the rare, localized, structured exception. Just as physical empty space:

- Constitutes nearly everything in the physical universe
- Is not inert but has properties and effects
- Is the substrate within which and against which structure (matter) exists
- Does not compete with matter as a rival substance but is the condition matter inhabits

So moral empty space (evil):

- Constitutes the default moral condition (the absence of GILE)
- Is not inert but has phenomenological and causal effects
- Is the substrate within which and against which moral structure (goodness) exists
- Does not compete with goodness as a rival moral substance but is the condition goodness inhabits

### 3.3 Dissolving the Phenomenological Objection

The empty space analogy dissolves the phenomenological objection immediately. Empty space in physics does not *feel* like nothing. An astronaut drifting in the void experiences the overwhelming, terrifying *presence* of emptiness -- the cold, the silence, the infinite expanse. The experience is not "the absence of air" in a trivial sense; it is the confrontation with the dominant reality of the universe. The void is *more real* than the spaceship left behind, not less.

Likewise, the experience of evil is not the trivial absence of goodness but the confrontation with moral empty space -- the vast, cold, dominant reality of moral vacancy. A torture victim experiences the overwhelming presence of moral emptiness: the torturer has hollowed out all GILE from the interaction, and what remains is the raw, terrifying void. The experience is real, overwhelming, and horrifying -- not despite being an absence but *because* it is an encounter with the dominant moral reality stripped of its usual covering of goodness.

## 4. The Temperature Analogy and the Elimination of Ontological Splitting

### 4.1 Cold Is Not a Thing

Temperature in physics is a measure of average molecular kinetic energy. "Cold" is not a substance, a force, or an entity. It is simply low thermal energy. There is no "essence of coldness" that competes with heat; there is only a single gradient of molecular motion, and what we call "cold" occupies the low end of that gradient.

Crucially, there is no ontological break between "lack of warmth" and "extreme cold." Room temperature, chilliness, cold, freezing, and absolute zero are all points on the same continuous scale. The transition from liquid water to ice at 0°C is a phase transition -- a dramatic change in macroscopic properties -- but it does not introduce a new ontological category. Ice is not a new *kind of thing* opposed to water; it is water at a lower energy state. No new substance appears at 0°C. The same H2O molecules are present; only their kinetic energy and arrangement have changed.

### 4.2 Application to Evil: EAR Eliminates the Split

The Existence Amplification Razor (EAR) counsels against multiplying ontological categories beyond necessity. Applied to evil, EAR eliminates the false dichotomy between "passive lack of goodness" and "active intentional evil":

| Moral Condition | Temperature Analogue | GILE Value |
|----------------|---------------------|------------|
| Full goodness | High temperature | Maximum GILE density |
| Ordinary decency | Room temperature | Moderate GILE |
| Moral indifference | Cool | Low GILE |
| Neglect | Cold | Very low GILE |
| Cruelty | Freezing | Extremely low GILE |
| Systematic evil | Near absolute zero | Approaching zero GILE |
| Intentional evil | Absolute zero pursuit | Agent actively maintaining/deepening the low-GILE state |

No new ontological category appears anywhere on this gradient. "Intentional evil" is not a different *kind* of thing from "lack of goodness" -- it is the same kind of thing (moral energy deficit) at a more extreme degree, with an agent actively maintaining or deepening the deficit. A torturer does not possess "evil substance"; they are an agent who actively drives GILE density toward zero in their sphere of influence.

### 4.3 Why Freezing Feels Different Without Being Different

The objection arises immediately: "But cruelty *feels* categorically different from mere indifference, just as freezing *feels* categorically different from mild chill." This is correct phenomenologically but irrelevant ontologically. Freezing feels categorically different from mild chill because the human body responds nonlinearly to temperature gradients -- tissue damage occurs, pain receptors fire differently, survival is threatened. But the underlying physics is continuous: there is no "freezing substance" distinct from "cold." The phenomenological discontinuity is in the *response*, not in the *stimulus*.

Similarly, cruelty feels categorically different from indifference because human moral psychology responds nonlinearly to GILE gradients -- trust is shattered, dignity is violated, the social fabric tears. But the underlying moral physics is continuous: there is no "evil essence" distinct from "lack of goodness." Cruelty is extreme low GILE maintained deliberately by an agent. The phenomenological discontinuity is in the moral response, not in the moral ontology.

## 5. Moral Phase Transitions

### 5.1 Phase Transitions in Physics

A phase transition occurs when a continuous change in one parameter (temperature, pressure) produces a discontinuous change in macroscopic properties (state of matter, symmetry, order). Water at 1°C and water at -1°C differ by only 2°C, but they occupy radically different macroscopic states. The transition is *real* -- ice genuinely behaves differently from liquid water -- but it does not require a new fundamental substance. The same molecules, the same forces, the same physics; only the degree of thermal energy has changed, and that change in degree has crossed a threshold that produces qualitatively new behavior.

### 5.2 Moral Phase Transitions

The GILE gradient admits analogous phase transitions: points where a continuous change in GILE density produces a discontinuous change in normative obligations, even though no new ontological category has been introduced.

**The parental threshold.** Before a child is born, an adult has general obligations of decency toward all persons. At the moment of becoming a parent, the local moral topology changes: a new region of extreme GILE dependency comes into existence (the infant), and the parent's normative obligations undergo a phase transition. The parent now has intensive, specific, demanding obligations that did not exist moments before. No new moral substance was created. The same GILE gradient exists. But a threshold was crossed, and the normative landscape transformed.

**The authority threshold.** When a person assumes a position of power -- employer, judge, politician -- the local moral topology changes. The power-holder controls GILE gradients for many others. New obligations crystallize at the phase boundary: obligations of fairness, transparency, restraint. Again, no new ontological category; the same gradient, crossed at a critical point.

**The knowledge threshold.** Learning that a neighbor is suffering creates a moral phase transition. Before the knowledge, the moral topology was uniform; after it, a GILE gradient has been revealed, and the obligation to respond crystallizes. Ignorance provided a kind of moral insulance; knowledge eliminates it, producing new obligations from the same underlying moral physics.

### 5.3 The Phase Transition Principle

**Principle (Moral Phase Transitions):** When continuous changes in local GILE density cross critical thresholds, new normative obligations emerge without the introduction of new moral ontology. Degree alone, at critical points, generates qualitatively new normative structure.

This principle resolves a longstanding tension in ethics between those who insist that moral differences are always differences in kind (deontologists) and those who insist they are always differences in degree (consequentialists). Both are partially right: differences in degree, at phase boundaries, produce differences in normative kind.

## 6. Gradient-Control Responsibility

### 6.1 The Inadequacy of Essence-Based Responsibility

Traditional accounts of moral responsibility are entangled with the assumption that evil is a substance or force that agents either participate in or resist. On these accounts, agents are blameworthy because they "chose evil" or "embraced evil" or "were corrupted by evil." If evil is not a substance, these locutions are metaphors at best and confusions at worst. A new account of responsibility is needed.

### 6.2 Responsibility as Gradient Control

Within the moral empty space framework, moral responsibility tracks *control over GILE gradients*, not participation in evil essence. An agent is morally responsible -- and potentially blameworthy -- insofar as:

1. The agent has the capacity to influence local GILE density.
2. The agent's sphere of influence contains a GILE deficit (a moral hole).
3. The agent could have increased GILE density (filled or reduced the hole) but allowed the deficit to persist or deepen.

This framework naturally accommodates degrees of responsibility:

**Parents toward children.** Parents have near-total control over the GILE density in their children's moral environment. A child's moral topology is almost entirely shaped by parental choices. Parental responsibility is therefore maximal: parents who allow GILE deficits to develop in their children's lives bear heavy responsibility because they control the relevant gradients.

**Employers toward workers.** An employer controls significant GILE gradients -- the dignity of labor, the fairness of compensation, the safety of the workplace. Responsibility scales with control: the greater the employer's capacity to influence these gradients, the greater the responsibility.

**The wealthy toward the poor.** Wealth confers control over material GILE gradients -- access to food, shelter, healthcare, education. Responsibility tracks this control: those with greater capacity to fill material GILE deficits bear greater responsibility for allowing those deficits to persist.

### 6.3 The Gradient-Control Formula

Moral responsibility R for agent A with respect to moral hole H is:

```
R(A, H) = C(A, H) × D(H) × K(A, H)
```

Where:
- C(A, H) = degree of control A has over the GILE gradient at H
- D(H) = depth of the GILE deficit at H
- K(A, H) = A's knowledge (or culpable ignorance) of H

This formula captures the intuition that responsibility increases with control, with severity, and with awareness. It also explains why ignorance can be exculpatory (low K) or aggravating (culpable ignorance increases K rather than decreasing it).

## 7. Reciprocal Abstract Morality

### 7.1 The Abstraction Problem

A persistent challenge for moral realism is the causal isolation of abstract objects. If moral facts are abstract -- existing outside space and time, like mathematical truths -- then they cannot causally influence agents. Abstract objects, by definition, cannot *do* anything. How, then, can moral facts guide behavior?

### 7.2 The GILE Solution: Reciprocal Causation

The GILE framework resolves this through a three-tier causal architecture:

**Tier 1: Abstract GILE.** GILE exists as an abstract moral reality -- the structure of perfect goodness, intuition, love, and existence. As an abstract object, GILE cannot act directly on the physical world. It is the moral analogue of mathematical structure: real, necessary, but causally inert in isolation.

**Tier 2: Conscious Agents.** Agents (human beings, possibly other conscious entities) exist at the intersection of abstract and concrete reality. Agents can apprehend abstract moral structure (through conscience, moral intuition, reasoning) and act on it in the concrete world. Agents are the bridge between abstract morality and concrete moral topology.

**Tier 3: Concrete Moral Topology.** The actual distribution of moral holes and moral substance in the world -- the concrete pattern of goodness and its absence across situations, relationships, institutions, and societies. This topology is the result of agents acting (or failing to act) on abstract GILE.

The causal flow is reciprocal:

```
Abstract GILE ⇄ Agents ⇄ Concrete Moral Topology (holes)
```

Abstract GILE informs agents (through moral intuition, conscience, rational reflection). Agents shape concrete moral topology (through action, choice, institution-building). And concrete moral topology feeds back into agents' apprehension of abstract GILE (through moral experience, suffering, witness). The circle is not vicious but virtuous: it is the mechanism by which abstract moral truth becomes concretely realized in the world.

### 7.3 Moral Holes as Tangible Absences

On this account, moral holes -- concrete evil -- are tangible things that lack abstract goodness. They are not abstract themselves; they are concrete absences. A starving child is a concrete moral hole: a tangible situation that lacks the abstract goodness (adequate nutrition, care, protection) that ought to be present. The hole is real, physical, experienceable. What it lacks is abstract: the GILE-density that would fill it.

This resolves the abstraction problem: abstract GILE does not need to cause anything directly. It causes things through agents, who bridge the abstract-concrete divide. And concrete moral holes do not need to be abstract to be morally significant; they need only to be recognizable as deficits relative to abstract GILE.

## 8. The Tralse Status of Negative Moral Values

### 8.1 Negative Numbers as Directional Bookkeeping

In mathematics, negative numbers are indispensable for calculation but ontologically puzzling. You cannot have -3 apples. You cannot point to -5 meters of rope. Negative numbers do not refer to negative *things*; they encode *direction* or *deficit* relative to a reference point. "-3" is not a thing; it is a directional bookkeeping device that indicates "3 units below the reference point."

### 8.2 Negative Moral Values Tralsely Exist

Within the TI framework, negative moral values occupy a tralse existential status. They are not straightforwardly real (you cannot point to "negative goodness" as a substance), nor are they straightforwardly unreal (the deficits they describe are genuine). They tralsely exist: they exist as descriptive tools that accurately map moral topology without referring to positive moral entities.

EAR insists on eliminating negative moral values as ontological posits -- there is no "evil stuff" at the negative end of the scale. But EAR also insists on retaining whatever descriptive apparatus is needed to map reality accurately. The interval (-3, 2) on a GILE scale is descriptively indispensable: it captures the fact that some moral situations are not merely at zero GILE but are *actively depleted* below what the local moral topology would be in the absence of any agent. A torturer does not merely fail to add GILE; they destroy existing GILE structures. Describing this as "negative GILE" is descriptively essential even though, ontologically, no negative substance exists.

### 8.3 The Bookkeeping Principle

**Principle (Moral Bookkeeping):** Negative values on the GILE scale tralsely exist. They do not refer to negative moral substances but to the degree and direction of deficit relative to the appropriate reference point. "−3 GILE" means "3 units below the appropriate GILE density for this context," not "possessing 3 units of anti-goodness."

This principle allows the GILE framework to describe extreme evil accurately (as deep negative values) without committing to the existence of evil substance (which would contradict privation theory and violate EAR).

## 9. Normativity from Imperfection: The Anselmian Argument

### 9.1 The Normativity Problem

A persistent challenge for any privation account is grounding normativity. If evil is merely an absence, why *ought* we to fill it? Absences per se do not generate obligations. A hole in the ground does not obligate anyone to fill it. Why should a moral hole be different?

### 9.2 The Anselmian Move

The TI framework grounds normativity through an Anselmian argument:

1. **Perfection necessarily exists.** This is the core Anselmian premise, embedded in TI through the GM (God-Machine) concept. Perfect GILE -- maximal goodness, intuition, love, and existence -- is not merely possible but necessary.

2. **Holes are intrinsically imperfect.** A moral hole, by definition, is a deficit in GILE. It lacks substance that ought to be present (Aquinas's *privatio* as distinct from *negatio*). The hole is imperfect precisely because it is a region where reality falls short of what it could and should be.

3. **If perfection must exist, then imperfection must be eliminable in principle.** A world in which moral holes are permanent and unfillable would be a world in which perfection is impossible, contradicting premise 1.

4. **Therefore, moral holes ought to be filled to permissible levels.** The "ought" here is not a divine command (voluntarism) but a metaphysical entailment. If perfect GILE is necessary, and if moral holes represent departures from that perfection, then there exists an inherent directional pressure toward filling those holes. This pressure *is* normativity.

### 9.3 Advantages of This Grounding

This account of normativity avoids two traditional pitfalls:

**It avoids moral realism about evil.** Normativity is grounded not in the reality of evil but in the necessity of perfection. Evil does not need to be real for the obligation to eliminate it to be real. The obligation flows from perfection's necessity, not from evil's existence.

**It avoids voluntarism.** The "ought" does not derive from a divine command ("God says fill the holes") but from the metaphysical structure of reality ("perfection is necessary, holes are imperfect, therefore holes ought to be filled"). Even if God made no commands, the obligation would persist because it flows from the nature of perfection itself.

## 11. Objections and Responses

### 11.1 "This Still Trivializes Evil"

**Objection.** Despite the empty space analogy, calling evil "an absence" still feels dismissive of genuine suffering.

**Response.** The empty space analogy does the opposite of trivializing. Empty space in physics is not trivial -- it is the dominant reality of the universe, the substrate of all existence, the driver of cosmic expansion. Saying evil is "moral empty space" is saying it is the most pervasive, most fundamental, most inescapable feature of the moral landscape. It is not a doughnut hole; it is the void between galaxies. Nothing about this is trivial.

### 11.2 "The Temperature Analogy Breaks Down for Intentional Evil"

**Objection.** Temperature has no agent behind it. Intentional evil involves an agent deliberately pursuing harm. The analogy fails.

**Response.** The analogy is ontological, not agential. The claim is that intentional evil is not a *different kind of thing* from passive evil but the *same kind of thing* (GILE deficit) actively maintained by an agent. A refrigerator does not introduce "cold substance" into a room; it is a mechanism that actively removes thermal energy. Analogously, a torturer does not introduce "evil substance" into a situation; they are an agent who actively removes GILE density. The agency is real; the ontological novelty is not.

### 11.3 "Gradient-Control Responsibility Is Too Demanding"

**Objection.** If responsibility tracks control over gradients, then anyone with any capacity to improve any GILE deficit is responsible for failing to do so. This generates impossibly demanding obligations.

**Response.** The gradient-control formula includes three terms: control (C), deficit depth (D), and knowledge (K). Responsibility is proportional to all three. A person with minimal control over a distant deficit they know nothing about has negligible responsibility. The formula does not demand that everyone fix everything; it demands that those with significant control, facing deep deficits they know about, respond proportionally. This is more demanding than minimalist ethics but less demanding than maximalist consequentialism -- precisely where common moral intuition falls.

## 12. Conclusion

Evil is moral empty space. It is not a substance, a force, or a competitor to goodness. It is the vast, pervasive, dominant default condition of moral vacancy against which goodness is the structured, localized, hard-won exception. Like empty space in physics, it is not nothing -- it has properties, effects, and phenomenological presence. But it is not *something* in the way that goodness is something.

The temperature analogy reveals that the apparent ontological distinction between passive and active evil is an illusion generated by nonlinear moral phenomenology. There is one gradient, one scale, one moral physics. EAR collapses the categories; degree does all the work.

Gradient-control responsibility replaces essence-based blame with a precise, scalable account that tracks agents' control over GILE density. Moral phase transitions explain how continuous gradients generate discontinuous obligations. Reciprocal abstract morality resolves the causal isolation problem. The tralse status of negative moral values preserves descriptive power without ontological commitment. And the Anselmian argument grounds normativity in the necessity of perfection rather than the reality of evil or the commands of a deity.

The moral universe is mostly empty. Goodness is the rare, structured, precious exception. Our task is not to fight evil -- one cannot fight empty space -- but to build moral structure where none exists, maintain it where it does, and extend it into the void. The moral project is construction, not combat. And the measure of moral success is not the defeat of an enemy but the density of GILE in the region one controls.
