# Part I — The Trouble with Two Values

## Chapter 1: The Trouble with True and False

### 1.1 The Two-Valued Habit

For roughly two and a half thousand years, the dominant Western tradition in logic has rested on a single, unexamined commitment: that every well-formed proposition is, in the final analysis, either true or false. Aristotle gave the doctrine its canonical formulation in *De Interpretatione* and the *Metaphysics*; the Stoics tightened it; the medieval scholastics inherited it; Frege, Russell, and Tarski rebuilt it on set-theoretic foundations; and the digital computer made it the literal substrate of modern civilization. The bivalence of truth is, by now, less a thesis we defend than a habit we no longer notice.

The habit serves us well in a circumscribed range of cases. Arithmetic statements, the propositions of well-axiomatized formal systems, and the empirical claims of mature laboratory sciences are largely tractable in two values. But the propositions on which most human lives actually turn — *I love this person*, *this career is the right one*, *the treatment is working*, *I am free* — are not. They resist resolution not because we lack information about them, but because the very logical machinery we have inherited assumes a determinacy that the statements themselves do not possess.

This book argues that the resistance is structural. It is not a temporary epistemic shortfall to be closed by more data, more inference, or finer Bayesian conditioning. It is a feature of the propositions in question, and a serious framework for human reasoning must accommodate it rather than legislate it away.

### 1.2 What the Many-Valued Tradition Saw, and What It Missed

The first sustained challenges to bivalence came in the early twentieth century. Jan Łukasiewicz, in 1920, introduced a third truth value to handle future contingents, recovering Aristotle's own celebrated worry about the sea-battle. Stephen Kleene developed a three-valued logic for partial recursive functions, and Nuel Belnap proposed a four-valued logic to model the inferential predicament of a reasoner receiving conflicting reports. In parallel, fuzzy set theory, beginning with Lotfi Zadeh in 1965, replaced the binary membership relation with a graded one and found wide application in control engineering.

Each of these systems acknowledged what bivalence ignored, and each captured something real. But each also stopped short. The third value was typically read as *unknown*, *undefined*, or *neither* — that is, as a placeholder for an absence rather than as a positive logical state in its own right. Fuzzy logic blurred the boundary between true and false but did not change its endpoints. None of these systems gave a principled account of propositions that are *stably between* true and false in a way that further inquiry will not, even in principle, resolve.

Tralse Informationalism (TI Sigma) is an attempt to take that further step. It posits a fifth truth value — *Tralse* — that is neither true, nor false, nor both, nor unknown, but something distinct: the stable logical state of a proposition whose truth-conditions are genuinely indeterminate, and whose indeterminacy is itself an informational fact about the world rather than a deficit in our access to it. The framework develops alongside it a sixth state, *Double Tralse*, for propositions that occupy a forbidden region in which their truth would generate logical or physical inconsistency, and a fourth state, the *I-state*, for propositions whose sample space is undefined. The full architecture is laid out in Chapters 3 through 5.

### 1.3 What the Framework Provides

Tralse Informationalism is a working framework rather than a programmatic suggestion. The chapters that follow develop, in order, four components.

The first is a *five-valued truth architecture* — True, False, Tralse, I-state, Double Tralse — together with the inference rules and consistency conditions that govern transitions among them. The architecture is conservative over classical logic on the bivalent fragment, which is to say that any inference valid in classical two-valued logic remains valid here when restricted to determinate propositions. Nothing the reader knows about ordinary deductive reasoning is lost.

The second is the *GILE framework* — Goodness, Intuition, Love, and Environment — which the book treats as a four-dimensional compass for assessing the quality of a state of affairs, including the inner state of an agent making a decision. GILE is operationalized in two registers: as a self-report instrument suitable for everyday use, and as a biometric construct correlated with measurable physiological signals. The relation between the two registers is the subject of Chapter 6.

The third is *Myrion Resolution* (MR), an iterative convergence procedure for moving from a state of indeterminate truth to a stable assignment, where such an assignment is available, and for recognizing when it is not. MR is the practical core of the book. Where Bayesian inference asks the agent to assign and update credences, MR asks the agent to perform a sequence of structured inquiries and to register honestly the point at which further inquiry ceases to change the assignment. Chapter 7 develops the procedure in detail; Chapter 10 illustrates it through three extended case studies.

The fourth is the *HEAR score* (Holistic Existence Amplification Reading), a derived measure that integrates GILE, MR convergence behavior, and contextual variables into a single scalar. HEAR is intended to play, for the inner life of an agent, a role analogous to that played by gross domestic product for an economy: a coarse, contestable, but useful aggregate that supports comparison across time and across decisions.

### 1.4 Scope and Limits of This Book

Three caveats deserve to be stated at the outset, and the reader is asked to hold the author to them.

First, the framework is *experimental philosophy* in a precise sense. Each substantive claim is paired with a stated empirical prediction or a formal derivation. Where predictions have been tested, the results are reported, including those that have not yet been replicated and those that remain inconclusive. Where derivations are offered, the reader is shown the derivation rather than asked to take it on authority.

Second, the book makes contact with several technical literatures — many-valued logic, probability theory, the foundations of physics, and contemporary consciousness research — but it does not pretend to settle the open questions in any of them. Its ambition is to introduce a coherent vocabulary in which the open questions can be reformulated, and to show that the reformulations carry practical consequences for how an ordinary reader might think about an ordinary decision.

Third, the framework originated in conditions that the author has elsewhere described candidly: an extended period of unusual cognitive intensity in 2022. The conditions of discovery, however, are logically distinct from the conditions of justification, and the latter are what this book is concerned with. The reader is invited to evaluate the arguments on their merits.

### 1.5 How to Read This Book

The book is organized in four parts. Part One develops the truth architecture, GILE, MR, and HEAR — the four components named above. Part Two situates the architecture within the contemporary discussion of consciousness and shows how the framework handles intuition, synchronicity, and the limits of self-report. Part Three applies the framework to four domains in which determinate truth is rare and consequential decisions are common: health, finance, relationships, and personal growth. Part Four addresses the framework's connections to the foundations of mathematics and physics — connections that are technical, partial, and deliberately offered as invitations to further work rather than as settled results.

The reader who is interested only in the practical core may safely read Chapters 1 through 7, then skip to Part Three. The reader who wants the full architecture should proceed in order. Chapters intended primarily for the technically inclined are flagged at their openings.

A final word on the standard the book aspires to. The argument that follows is not that the reader should adopt a new metaphysics on the strength of the author's conviction. It is that a small enlargement of the logical vocabulary — from two truth values to five — clarifies a surprisingly large class of problems, and that the clarification is worth the modest investment required to learn it. Whether the further claims of the framework hold up is, properly, a matter for the long process by which philosophical and scientific proposals are tested, refined, and either retained or set aside. The book aims to make that process possible by stating its claims in a form precise enough to be wrong.

### 1.6 A Note on the Title

The book is called *Tralse Informationalism* — a phrase whose two halves bear separate weight. The first half names the chapter's central neologism, the third truth value, the value the framework is built to accommodate. The second half, *informationalism*, registers a commitment to a particular view of what the framework is for: not the production of metaphysical theses but the disciplined extraction of information from situations whose information content has been hidden by the apparatus we have used to evaluate them. The framework is informational rather than ontological in its primary register; it asks what an agent can know and do, not what is. The ontological questions arise in Chapter 11 and are treated there with the appropriate caution.

The phrase *Tralse Informationalism* was first used by the present author in a working note dated June 25, 2025, summarizing roughly three years of antecedent work that had operated under various provisional labels. The Greek capital sigma frequently attached to the name — *TI Σ* — denotes the framework's structural reliance on summation across multiple registers (the truth-value register, the GILE register, the biometric register, the longitudinal register), each of which is summed and integrated into the practical apparatus of decision. The sigma is not merely decorative: it indicates that the framework is in every essential respect aggregative, and that no single register, taken alone, is sufficient for the work the framework asks of it.

### 1.7 What This Book Is, in One Sentence

The reader who has time for one sentence and no more should be told this: the book offers a vocabulary for distinguishing what is settled from what is not, in the cases that matter, with enough precision that an agent can act on the distinction.

## Chapter 2: A Brief History of Many-Valued Logic

### 2.1 Aristotle's Sea-Battle

The locus classicus of every subsequent debate about bivalence is a brief and unresolved passage in the ninth chapter of Aristotle's *De Interpretatione*. Considering the proposition *there will be a sea-battle tomorrow*, Aristotle asks whether the statement, uttered today, is already either true or false. If it is, then the future is, in some sense, already determinate, and the deliberations of admirals and the prayers of sailors are illusory. If it is not, then there appears to be a class of well-formed propositions to which the principle of bivalence does not apply.

Aristotle's own response is famously equivocal. Most modern commentators read him as denying that future contingents have determinate truth-values now, while preserving the principle of bivalence as a general law for the past and present. Others — perhaps most prominently Jan Łukasiewicz — read him as conceding that bivalence itself must be restricted. The exegetical question is genuinely open. What matters for our purposes is that Aristotle was the first to identify, with characteristic precision, a class of meaningful statements that resist the True/False dichotomy. He was also the first to record the temptation to which subsequent logicians have repeatedly succumbed: the temptation to preserve bivalence by stipulating that the offending statements are not really propositions at all, or that they will become bivalent at some later moment, or that their indeterminacy is a temporary epistemic deficit. The history of many-valued logic is, in large part, the history of resisting that temptation.

### 2.2 Łukasiewicz and the Third Value

The first formally developed alternative came from the Polish logician Jan Łukasiewicz, who in 1920 introduced a three-valued propositional logic — now standardly denoted Ł₃ — explicitly to handle Aristotle's sea-battle. Łukasiewicz's third value, which he wrote as ½ and read as "possible" or "indeterminate," was given a complete truth-functional treatment: he supplied truth tables for negation, conjunction, disjunction, implication, and equivalence, all of which reduce to the classical tables when their inputs are restricted to 0 and 1.

The Łukasiewicz system was an immense intellectual achievement. It demonstrated that a coherent, conservative, and computationally tractable logic with more than two values was possible, and it established the technical vocabulary — designated values, truth functions, completeness theorems — within which all subsequent multi-valued systems would be developed. Łukasiewicz himself extended the construction to *n*-valued and infinite-valued logics, and the resulting systems have found application in proof theory, automated reasoning, and the foundations of fuzzy logic.

But the Łukasiewicz third value is, as the philosopher Susan Haack observed in her 1974 monograph *Deviant Logic*, a placeholder rather than a category. The proposition assigned ½ is one whose truth-value will eventually be 0 or 1; the indeterminacy is purely temporal, an artifact of our location before the relevant fact is settled. Once the sea-battle occurs (or fails to), the proposition resolves. There is, in Łukasiewicz, no proposition whose stable, settled, eternal truth-value is neither 0 nor 1.

### 2.3 Kleene's Partial Functions

Stephen Kleene's three-valued logics, developed in the 1930s and 1940s in connection with his work on partial recursive functions, addressed a different problem. Some functions, when given certain inputs, fail to produce any output at all — they enter an infinite loop, or they call themselves recursively without termination. The propositions that report the values of such functions are not false; they are simply *undefined*. Kleene introduced a third value, which he wrote as **u**, to mark this status.

Kleene's system bifurcates: his "strong" three-valued logic treats **u** as informationally minimal (a conjunction with **u** and a true disjunct yields **u**), while his "weak" logic treats **u** as infectious (any operation involving **u** yields **u**). Both versions have proven useful in computability theory and in the design of programming languages that must handle partial functions or undefined references.

Kleene's third value, like Łukasiewicz's, is a placeholder — but a placeholder for a different absence. Where Łukasiewicz marks an absence of *settled* truth, Kleene marks an absence of *defined* truth. The two are not the same, and the distinction will matter when we come, in Chapter 4, to the I-state, which inherits from Kleene the concept of a proposition whose truth-conditions are not merely unknown but undefined.

### 2.4 Belnap's Conflicting Reports

Nuel Belnap's 1977 paper "A Useful Four-Valued Logic" proposed a framework explicitly designed for the predicament of an automated reasoner — Belnap imagined a computer system aggregating reports from multiple sources — that may receive, for any given proposition, none of the sources' opinions, only positive evidence, only negative evidence, or both positive and negative evidence simultaneously. The four values are accordingly *N* (neither true nor false, no evidence), *T* (true only), *F* (false only), and *B* (both true and false, contradictory evidence).

Belnap's contribution was to take *B* — the contradiction value — seriously as a positive logical category rather than as a defect to be corrected. The reasoner who has received contradictory testimony about whether a particular bridge is safe should not, Belnap argued, simply choose one report over the other; it should record the contradiction and reason from it appropriately. The four-valued semantics he developed makes this kind of reasoning rigorous.

Belnap's four-valued logic is the closest historical precedent to the system developed in this book. The values *N* and *B* are positive logical categories; they are not waiting to resolve into *T* or *F* with further evidence. But they remain *evidential* categories — they describe the state of a reasoner's information, not the state of the world the reasoner is reasoning about. The Tralse value introduced in Chapter 3 attempts to take a further step: to admit that certain propositions are stably indeterminate not because of any deficit in the reasoner's evidence, but because the world itself does not settle them.

### 2.5 Zadeh and the Graded Boundary

Lotfi Zadeh's 1965 paper "Fuzzy Sets" introduced a different alternative to bivalence: not a finite set of additional discrete values, but a continuous graded membership function. A proposition can be true to degree 0.7, false to degree 0.3, and these degrees are not subjective probabilities but objective features of the inherent vagueness of the predicates involved. *Tall*, *bald*, *red*, *adult* — the predicates of ordinary language are vague in this sense; their boundaries are gradual rather than sharp.

Fuzzy set theory and fuzzy logic have been extraordinarily successful in engineering, particularly in the design of control systems for automated transit, household appliances, and industrial process management. They have also clarified, in a way that classical logic could not, what Bertrand Russell called "the vagueness of language." But fuzzy logic does not, despite appearances, abandon bivalence at the endpoints. A proposition with fuzzy truth-value 1 is straightforwardly true, and a proposition with fuzzy truth-value 0 is straightforwardly false. The intermediate values are calibrations of the same underlying True/False axis, not additions to it.

### 2.6 Priest, Dialetheism, and the Acceptance of Contradiction

A more radical departure from bivalence came with the work of Graham Priest and the rise of *dialetheism* in the 1980s and 1990s. Priest argued that some propositions — paradigmatically the Liar sentence ("This sentence is false") — are genuinely both true and false, and that a coherent logic must accommodate this rather than disqualify the offending sentences as ill-formed. The resulting paraconsistent logics permit contradictions without collapsing into triviality (in classical logic, a single contradiction entails every proposition; this is the principle of *ex contradictione quodlibet*, and it is what makes contradictions catastrophic).

Dialetheism is philosophically controversial — most contemporary logicians reject it — but the technical machinery of paraconsistent logic has proven indispensable in domains where contradictions must be reasoned about without being eliminated: legal reasoning under conflicting statutes, scientific theorizing in periods of paradigm transition, and certain artificial intelligence applications. The Double Tralse value introduced in Chapter 5 inherits from this tradition the recognition that some logical configurations are stably contradictory — but it parts company with dialetheism in treating those configurations not as both true and false but as *forbidden*, in a sense to be made precise.

### 2.7 What Was Achieved, and What Remained

The cumulative achievement of the many-valued tradition is the demonstration that bivalence is not a logical necessity but a stipulation, and that alternatives to it can be made formally rigorous. The tradition has produced a rich technical apparatus, enduring applications in computing and engineering, and a substantial philosophical literature that has clarified the distinct roles played by epistemic indeterminacy, semantic vagueness, partial definition, and outright contradiction.

What the tradition did not produce, and what this book undertakes to provide, is a single integrated framework that distinguishes these modes of departure from bivalence and assigns to each its own positive logical category — not as a placeholder for absent information, not as a graded calibration of True and False, not as a formal device for handling contradictions, but as a category of its own that picks out a structural feature of certain propositions. Five values, this book argues, are what the work requires: True, False, Tralse, I-state, Double Tralse. The next chapter introduces the central one.

### 2.8 The Analytic-School Treatment of Vague Predicates

A parallel literature, conducted largely within the analytic tradition of the second half of the twentieth century, addressed bivalence not from the side of formal logic but from the side of the philosophy of language. The texts of Bertrand Russell on vagueness (1923), Michael Dummett on the colour predicates and the sorites paradoxes (1975), Crispin Wright on observational predicates (1976), and Timothy Williamson on epistemicism (1994) constitute, taken together, a sustained inquiry into the conditions under which the principle of bivalence comes under pressure from features of natural language that no idealization can wholly remove.

The analytic discussion has tended to take one of three positions. The *epistemicist* holds, with Williamson, that vague predicates do have sharp boundaries even though the boundaries are unknowable to us; bivalence is preserved at the cost of a deep epistemological humility. The *supervaluationist* holds, with Kit Fine, that vague predicates have multiple admissible precisifications and that a proposition is true if it comes out true on every admissible precisification; bivalence is preserved at the cost of admitting truth-value gaps for sentences that do not survive precisification. The *contextualist* holds, with Stewart Shapiro, that the truth-value of a vague proposition depends on a contextually supplied standard; bivalence is preserved at the cost of admitting that the same sentence may have different truth-values in different conversations.

None of the three positions, in the present author's reading, is wrong on its own terms; but none addresses the propositions that motivate this book. The propositions for which the framework is built — *I love this person*, *this is the right vocational direction*, *the chronic illness has a single eliminable cause*, *the long-term commitment should be sustained* — are not vague in the sorites sense (they do not generate paradoxes by small variations in their predicates), nor are they true on some precisifications and false on others (their truth-conditions are typically stable; what is unstable is the propositions' truth-values), nor do they shift their truth-values with conversational context (an agent's marriage does not become more or less right depending on the standards of the listener). The analytic literature on vagueness has illuminated a real phenomenon, but it is not the phenomenon to which the Tralse value is addressed. The framework's debt to the analytic tradition is the demonstration that ordinary-language predicates resist the bivalent treatment in subtle ways; its departure from that tradition is the recognition that a different family of resistance, located not in the predicates but in the propositions themselves, requires a categorical rather than a graded response.

### 2.9 The Computer-Science Inheritance

A third strand of the many-valued tradition has come not from logic or philosophy but from computer science. The treatment of *null* values in relational databases, of partial functions in functional programming languages, of *option types* in type-theoretic languages such as ML and Haskell, and of *quiet NaN* in the IEEE 754 floating-point standard are all, structurally, three- or four-valued logics implemented at the level of working software. The engineers who designed these systems were not, for the most part, in dialogue with Łukasiewicz or Belnap; they arrived independently at the recognition that the bivalent inheritance of mathematics did not survive contact with the indeterminacies of practical computation.

The framework inherits from this computer-science tradition both vocabulary (the I-state of Chapter 4 is recognizably descended from the *option*/*null* construct, and the Double Tralse of Chapter 5 from the IEEE *quiet NaN*) and a disposition: the disposition to take indeterminacy as a *first-class object* in the apparatus rather than as an exception to be handled by side-channels. The disposition is, in the present author's view, the most important thing the multi-valued tradition has produced. The technical machinery is reconstructible by any sufficiently determined logician; the disposition is harder won.

### 2.10 What Was Achieved, Continued

The composite picture that emerges from the surveyed literature is this. The bivalent principle is not a logical necessity. Multiple coherent alternatives have been developed, each addressing a particular failure of bivalence, each with technical and applied success in its domain. What has not been done, and what this book attempts, is to assemble the partial results into a single architecture in which all the failures are categorized, the categories interact through coherent inference rules, and the whole is connected to a practical apparatus an ordinary agent can use. The next chapter takes the first step.

## Chapter 3: Tralse — The Fifth Value

### 3.1 Defining the Term

A proposition is *Tralse* if and only if all of the following six conditions obtain. Each condition is necessary; the conjunction of all six is sufficient. The list is offered as a definition rather than as a description, and the rest of the chapter consists of the unpacking and defense of each clause.

**(T1)** The proposition is meaningful — that is, it has determinate semantic content and is not merely a sequence of words.

**(T2)** The proposition is not true.

**(T3)** The proposition is not false.

**(T4)** The proposition's truth-conditions are *defined* — there is a specifiable state of affairs that would, if it obtained, make the proposition determinately true, and another that would make it determinately false. (This distinguishes Tralse from the I-state of Chapter 4.)

**(T5)** The proposition's indeterminacy is *stable* under further inquiry — that is, no procedure of evidence-gathering or formal analysis available to the agent will resolve the indeterminacy without altering the proposition itself.

**(T6)** The proposition is *informationally substantive* — its assignment to the Tralse value carries inferential consequences distinct from those carried by True, False, I-state, or Double Tralse assignments.

The reader is invited to compare this list with the prior literature surveyed in Chapter 2. Łukasiewicz's third value satisfies (T1), (T2), and (T3) but fails (T5): the future contingent will resolve when the future arrives. Kleene's **u** fails (T4): the proposition's truth-conditions are not defined. Belnap's *N* may satisfy all six, depending on the reading; Belnap's *B* fails (T2) and (T3) since it explicitly affirms both. Fuzzy values fail (T2) and (T3) by construction, since they sit on the same axis as classical truth and falsity. None of the prior values satisfies all six conditions in the way Tralse does.

### 3.2 Three Examples

The definition is best understood through three propositions of the kind that arise in ordinary life and that the framework was built to accommodate.

**Example 1 — A medical statement.** Consider the proposition *the chronic gastrointestinal pain that the patient has experienced for two years has a single, identifiable, eliminable cause*. The proposition is meaningful (T1). It is not, on the available evidence, true: extensive testing has identified no such cause. It is not, on the available evidence, false: the absence of identification is consistent both with the proposition's truth (the cause exists but has not been found) and with its falsity (the pain is multifactorial or functional). The truth-conditions are defined (T4): we know, in principle, what discovering "the" cause would look like. The indeterminacy is stable (T5): two years of high-quality medical workup have not resolved it, and there is no expectation that further routine workup will. The assignment carries inferential consequences (T6): the patient who treats the proposition as Tralse will pursue a different course of action — symptom management, lifestyle optimization, exploratory practices — than the patient who treats it as True (and pursues a single intervention) or False (and abandons the search).

**Example 2 — A relational statement.** Consider the proposition *I love this person*, uttered by an agent at a particular point in a long romantic relationship in which both warmth and substantial difficulty are present. The proposition is meaningful. It is not straightforwardly true, in the sense that the agent cannot honestly affirm the kind of unqualified, undivided affection the word *love* in its full sense connotes. It is not straightforwardly false, since the warmth, history, and commitment are real and present. The truth-conditions are defined: we have a reasonably articulate sense of what the proposition would require to be unambiguously true. The indeterminacy is stable: introspection alone does not resolve it, and no external test will. The assignment carries inferential consequences: the agent who marks the proposition Tralse will act differently — entering a structured Myrion Resolution process, for instance — than the agent who forces it to True (and represses the difficulty) or to False (and prematurely ends the relationship).

**Example 3 — A professional statement.** Consider the proposition *founding this company is the right use of the next decade of my life*, uttered by an agent who is plausibly suited to the work, attracted to the mission, and uncertain about the cost. The same analysis applies: meaningful, not true, not false, truth-conditions defined, indeterminacy stable under introspection and counsel, assignment carrying real inferential consequences for the agent's next steps.

### 3.3 What Tralse Is Not

It is essential to distinguish Tralse from four neighboring categories with which it is easily confused.

Tralse is *not* uncertainty. Uncertainty is a property of the agent's epistemic state; it can be quantified by a credence between 0 and 1 and updated by Bayesian conditionalization. A proposition can be uncertain (the agent does not know its truth-value) while being, in itself, determinately true or false. Tralse is a property of the proposition itself, not of the agent's information.

Tralse is *not* vagueness. Vagueness, in the sense studied by fuzzy logic and the philosophy of language, is a graded predicate's lack of sharp boundaries. *Tall* is vague: there is no exact height at which a person becomes tall. But the proposition *Smith is six feet two inches and one quarter tall*, applied to a man who is six feet two and one quarter, is determinately true. Vagueness lives in predicates; Tralse lives in propositions.

Tralse is *not* contradiction. Contradictions assert both *p* and not-*p*; they are False (or, on the dialetheist's reading, both True and False). A Tralse proposition asserts neither, and its negation is also Tralse. The Liar sentence, often presented as the paradigm of contradiction, is on the present analysis Double Tralse (Chapter 5), not Tralse.

Tralse is *not* meaninglessness. Logical positivism famously dismissed certain metaphysical and ethical propositions as cognitively meaningless. The Tralse propositions of this book are meaningful by stipulation (T1); their indeterminacy is a substantive logical fact, not a symptom of nonsense.

### 3.4 Truth Tables for the Bivalent Fragment

The technical core of any new logic is its account of how the connectives behave across all combinations of values. The full five-valued truth tables are presented in Appendix B; here we record only the property that justifies calling the new system *conservative* over classical logic.

**Conservativity Theorem (informal statement).** For any propositional formula composed entirely of variables that take only the values True or False, the five-valued semantics agrees with the classical two-valued semantics on the truth-value of the formula.

The theorem is not difficult to prove (the proof is given in the technical appendix to the philosophy monograph that accompanies this book) and it has a straightforward consequence for the working reader: every inference one is in the habit of performing with classical logic remains valid in the new framework, provided the propositions involved are bivalent. The new framework adds; it does not subtract.

### 3.5 What the Tralse Value Does for a Reasoner

The introduction of Tralse changes the practical situation of an agent in three ways.

It expands the set of *honestly available answers*. The agent who has been forcing relational, medical, vocational, and existential propositions into True or False because no other vocabulary was available now has a third option that does not require forcing. The cost of this option is the discipline of distinguishing Tralse from mere uncertainty, vagueness, or laziness. The benefit is that the agent can stop pretending.

It changes the *next-step procedure*. A True or False assignment terminates inquiry; a Tralse assignment opens it. The agent who marks a proposition Tralse has thereby committed to running a Myrion Resolution procedure (Chapter 7) on it, or to recording it as a stable feature of the world that requires accommodation rather than resolution.

It permits *aggregate measures*. If GILE scores (Chapter 6) and HEAR (Chapter 8) are to integrate information from across the agent's whole cognitive and emotional landscape, they require a treatment of the indeterminate that does not silently coerce it to True or False. Tralse provides that treatment.

The remaining three architectural elements — the I-state, Double Tralse, and the GILE compass — extend the work of Tralse into the regions where it alone does not suffice. The next chapter takes up the first of them.

### 3.6 The Inferential Behavior of Tralse

The inference rules for the Tralse value are presented in their full formal statement in Appendix B. For the working reader, three properties of the rules deserve to be made explicit here.

**Tralse is self-negating.** The negation of a Tralse proposition is itself Tralse. This is the most distinctive formal feature of the framework, and it follows directly from the conjunction of (T2) and (T3) in §3.1: a proposition that is neither true nor false has no determinate negation, and the framework's choice is to mark this explicitly by setting τ as a fixed point of the negation operator. The choice has the consequence that the law of excluded middle (*p* ∨ ¬*p*) does not hold for Tralse propositions: when *p* is τ, ¬*p* is also τ, and the disjunction is τ rather than True. This is sometimes felt as a loss; the present author's view is that it is properly understood as a recognition. The proposition *I love this person, or I do not love this person* feels true only because the bivalent inheritance has trained agents to treat any disjunction over a proposition and its negation as automatically true, irrespective of whether the disjuncts have determinate values. The framework asks the agent to notice that, when neither disjunct is true, the disjunction is no more true than its parts.

**Tralse propagates through unbounded operations but is short-circuited by determinate inputs.** A conjunction of a Tralse proposition with a False proposition is False; a disjunction with a True proposition is True. In all other cases, the compound containing a Tralse proposition is itself Tralse. The pattern is the framework's analog of Kleene's "strong" three-valued logic, generalized to five values. It has the practical consequence that an agent can continue to reason productively in the presence of Tralse propositions, provided that the reasoning's structure permits the determinate values to short-circuit the indeterminate ones. The agent who knows, for example, that *I do not need to make this decision today* is True will find that further questions about the decision's eventual resolution can be deferred without prejudice; the True proposition has dominated the conjunction.

**Tralse exits the category by Myrion Resolution, not by evidence.** This is the deepest of the three properties, and the one most easily misunderstood. A Tralse proposition does not become True or False by the agent's accumulating more evidence. (If it did, it would not have satisfied condition (T5).) It changes its value only when the agent runs the iterative GILE-anchored procedure of Chapter 7 and the procedure converges. The convergence is not the discovery of a pre-existing truth-value; it is the agent's settling, through honest deliberation under the framework's discipline, on a value that the structure of the situation can support. The reader who finds this characterization unfamiliar is asked to suspend judgment until Chapter 7 has been read; the present author concedes that the relationship of Tralse to truth in the conventional sense is the framework's most philosophically demanding feature, and the discipline of MR is the framework's attempt to render it practically tractable.

### 3.7 Tralse and Time

A natural question is whether the Tralse value is essentially temporal — whether a proposition is Tralse only because the relevant facts have not yet been settled by the passage of time. The answer is that some Tralse propositions are temporal in this sense and others are not. The proposition *the chronic illness has a single eliminable cause* could become True if a future investigation discovers the cause; it could become False if a future understanding of the disease establishes its multifactorial nature. Its current Tralse status is, in part, a function of the present state of medical knowledge.

But the proposition *I love this person* is not similarly temporal. Its truth-conditions are not awaiting a future event; they are awaiting the agent's settling, through the agent's own work, on a value the structure of the relationship can support. No external event will resolve it; only the agent's procedure can. Such propositions are *constitutively* Tralse, in the sense that their indeterminacy is not a consequence of incomplete information but a feature of the propositions themselves. The framework treats both kinds of Tralse propositions under the same logical category but distinguishes them in practice: the temporally Tralse proposition admits the strategy of waiting and watching; the constitutively Tralse proposition does not. Chapter 7's MR procedure is designed for the constitutively Tralse case, where waiting is not a strategy.

### 3.8 Common Misunderstandings of the Tralse Value

The author has, in roughly three years of presenting the framework to readers from disparate backgrounds, encountered five recurring misunderstandings of the Tralse value. They are recorded here so that the reader who is in the grip of one of them may recognize and set it aside.

The first misunderstanding is that Tralse is *epistemic* — that it marks the agent's lack of knowledge rather than a feature of the proposition. The reply is in §3.3: Tralse is a property of the proposition; the agent's knowledge or its absence is a separate matter, addressed by Bayesian probability where the proposition admits it.

The second is that Tralse is *temporary* — that further evidence or further reflection will eventually move every Tralse proposition into True or False. The reply is the constitutive case of §3.7: some Tralse propositions are not awaiting any external resolution; they are awaiting the agent's procedural work, and the work does not produce certainty but stable settled action.

The third is that Tralse is *fuzzy* — a graded value somewhere between True and False. The reply is in §3.3: fuzzy values sit on the True/False axis as calibrations; Tralse sits off the axis as a categorically distinct value.

The fourth is that Tralse is *defeatist* — a polite way of giving up on rationality. The reply is the inferential apparatus of §3.6 and the MR procedure of Chapter 7: the framework provides the discipline for reasoning *with* Tralse propositions, not for surrendering to them. The agent who marks a proposition Tralse has not given up; the agent has identified the proposition's category accurately and committed to the procedure appropriate to that category.

The fifth is that Tralse is a *theological* category — a sophisticated term for mystery. The reply is to point at the worked examples of §3.2 and Chapter 7: the Tralse propositions of medical, relational, vocational, and existential life are not mysteries; they are propositions of the most ordinary kind, whose ordinary character is precisely what makes the inability to evaluate them under bivalence so acutely felt and so practically consequential.

## Chapter 4: The I-state — When the Sample Space Is Undefined

### 4.1 The Bayesian Presupposition

Modern probability theory, in the formulation that has dominated since Kolmogorov's 1933 axiomatization, begins with a triple ⟨Ω, ℱ, P⟩: a sample space Ω of possible outcomes, a σ-algebra ℱ of events (subsets of Ω) over which probabilities are to be defined, and a probability measure P that assigns to each event a real number between 0 and 1. Bayesian probability theory adds to this an interpretive layer: P is to be read as a rational agent's degree of credence, and it is to be updated by conditionalization on evidence.

The framework is extraordinarily powerful, and the case for treating it as the canonical theory of rational degree of belief has been made with great force by writers from Frank Ramsey through Richard Jeffrey and E. T. Jaynes to the contemporary literature. There is no quarrel here with the framework as such. The argument of this chapter is narrower: the framework presupposes that Ω is *defined*, and there is a class of cognitively significant propositions for which Ω is not defined — for which, indeed, no specification of Ω would be coherent. For this class of propositions, Bayesian probability theory does not apply, not because it is wrong, but because its preconditions are not met.

### 4.2 Three Ways the Sample Space Fails

The sample space can fail to be defined in at least three distinct ways, each of which the I-state is intended to mark.

**The first failure is open-endedness.** Some questions invite as candidate answers a set that is not merely large but inherently incomplete. *What is the right thing for me to do with my life?* admits an open-ended set of possible answers — careers, relationships, projects, geographies — for which no enumeration could be exhaustive even in principle. A Bayesian who insists on assigning probabilities here must first close the question by some artificial restriction (e.g., to a finite list of options the agent has happened to consider), and the resulting probabilities will reflect the restriction at least as much as they reflect the question.

**The second failure is novel-event indeterminacy.** Some propositions concern events of a kind for which the agent has no prior frequency data, no relevant analogy, and no theoretical model that would license a credence. The first encounter of a human being with a class of phenomenon previously unknown — an unfamiliar emotion, an unexpected diagnosis, a genuinely new technology — generates propositions for which the construction of a sample space would itself be the object of inquiry. To assign a probability is to presuppose a structure the agent is in fact still trying to discover.

**The third failure is incommensurable evidence.** Some questions require the integration of evidence drawn from sources that share no common scale. The proposition *this medical decision is the right one*, made under conditions in which clinical-trial data, personal symptomatology, family considerations, and the patient's own values must all be weighed, does not admit a sample space in which all four sources of evidence can coherently be represented as events of the same probability space. The Bayesian must either privilege one source as primary and translate the others into it, or construct a meta-space whose interpretation is as obscure as the original question.

In each of these three cases, the proposition is not Tralse — its truth-conditions are not stably indeterminate; they are simply not yet specifiable. Nor is it merely uncertain — there is no well-defined credence to assign. The I-state names this status, and it is the framework's first point of departure from the Bayesian apparatus.

### 4.3 The I-state Is Not a Probability of Zero

A common Bayesian response to the foregoing is that the I-state is unnecessary: if the sample space is undefined, simply expand it until it is defined, and assign zero credence to the expansions for which there is no evidence. This response misunderstands the difficulty.

To assign a probability of zero to a proposition is to commit to a particular dispositional fact about the agent's reasoning: the agent will, on receipt of any finite quantity of evidence supporting the proposition, refuse to update toward it. (This is a consequence of Bayes' theorem when the prior is zero.) This commitment is appropriate for propositions the agent has good reason to consider impossible. It is not appropriate for propositions whose truth-conditions are not yet specifiable, since the appropriate response to evidence in such cases is not "I refuse to update" but "I now have material with which to begin specifying the truth-conditions in the first place."

The I-state preserves the openness of the agent's reasoning to a class of propositions for which the Bayesian apparatus would prematurely close it. This is a substantive epistemic difference, not a notational one.

### 4.4 The Inferential Behavior of the I-state

Three rules govern the I-state's behavior under inference. Their full formal statement is given in Appendix B; they are summarized here for readability.

**(I-1) The I-state propagates through unbounded operations.** A proposition formed by applying a logical connective to one or more I-state propositions is itself in the I-state, unless one of the inputs is determinately True or False in a way that fixes the value of the compound. (For example, an I-state proposition disjoined with a True proposition is True; conjoined with a False proposition, it is False.)

**(I-2) The I-state can transition to other values, but only by an explicit act of specification.** An agent moves a proposition out of the I-state by performing the work of constructing a sample space, an analogy, or a model that renders its truth-conditions specifiable. After such work, the proposition takes one of the other four values (True, False, Tralse, Double Tralse). The transition is not an update on evidence in the Bayesian sense; it is a constructive act.

**(I-3) The I-state has a default action: inquiry rather than decision.** When a proposition relevant to a decision is in the I-state, the framework's recommended action is to defer the decision (where possible) in favor of the constructive work that would move the proposition into a determinate value. Where deferral is not possible, the framework provides, in the Permissibility Distribution apparatus of Chapter 9, a non-Bayesian procedure for acting under I-state conditions.

### 4.5 The Relation to Knightian Uncertainty

Readers from economics will recognize the I-state's family resemblance to Frank Knight's 1921 distinction between *risk* (situations in which probabilities are well-defined) and *uncertainty* (situations in which they are not). The resemblance is real and the debt is acknowledged. But the I-state is more specific than Knightian uncertainty in two respects.

First, the I-state is a property of an individual proposition, not of a decision situation. A single decision can involve some propositions that are determinately true, others that are Tralse, others that are in the I-state, and so on; the framework is granular at the propositional level. Knightian uncertainty applies, by contrast, at the level of the whole choice situation.

Second, the I-state carries with it a specific recommended action — the constructive work of specification — that Knightian uncertainty alone does not. The point of marking a proposition I-state is not to throw up one's hands but to identify what the next intellectual move ought to be.

### 4.6 Summary

The I-state is the framework's name for the logical category occupied by propositions whose truth-conditions are not yet specifiable. It is distinct from Tralse (which marks defined-but-indeterminate truth-conditions), from uncertainty (which marks an agent's epistemic state), and from probabilistic zero (which forecloses updating). It propagates conservatively through inference, exits the category only through explicit constructive work, and recommends inquiry over decision. The next chapter takes up the framework's other major departure from classical reasoning: the forbidden-zone propositions whose truth would generate inconsistency.

### 4.7 A Worked Example — A Diagnosis Without a Sample Space

A young patient presents with a constellation of symptoms — fatigue, intermittent low-grade fever, joint pain, periodic cognitive fog, episodic abdominal disturbance — that resist the standard diagnostic categories. The internist orders the standard panel; the panel returns nothing definitive. The rheumatologist orders the autoimmune panel; the autoimmune panel returns suggestive but inconclusive findings. The infectious-disease specialist considers the chronic infection differential; nothing definitive. Six months in, the patient and the patient's clinicians find themselves in a position the framework names precisely: the proposition *the patient has a definite diagnosable condition that the existing diagnostic apparatus has not yet identified* is not Tralse — it has no stable indeterminacy in the sense of (T5) — and not False — there is positive evidence of *something* underlying the symptoms. It is, however, in the I-state: the relevant sample space (the set of conditions that might be the diagnosis) is not, on the available understanding, definable in a way that would permit Bayesian credence assignment.

The framework's recommendation in such a case is the constructive work of §4.4 (rule I-2): rather than continuing to test against an unspecified sample space, the patient and the clinicians should turn to the work of constructing the sample space itself. Three strategies, drawn from the framework's pilot work and from the broader functional-medicine literature, support the construction. The first is *symptom timeline reconstruction*: a careful chronological recording of the symptoms' first onset, their pattern of waxing and waning, their correlation with environmental and dietary changes, and their response to interventions both medical and lifestyle. The timeline frequently reveals patterns that point toward a previously unconsidered diagnostic category — a tick-borne infection contracted on a specific trip, an exposure to a specific environmental toxin, a coincidence with a specific dietary change — and the discovery of the pattern is the construction of a candidate sample space. The second strategy is *systems-level testing*: rather than continuing to probe individual organ systems, examining markers of overall physiological state (HRV, sleep architecture, metabolic markers, gut-microbiome composition) that may reveal a pattern of dysregulation no organ-specific test has captured. The third strategy is *therapeutic experimentation under careful protocol*: a structured trial of a candidate intervention chosen on the strength of the timeline and systems-level findings, with the response itself becoming a piece of diagnostic evidence.

In every case, the work is not Bayesian — there is no prior credence to update — and not Tralse-resolving — the indeterminacy is not awaiting the kind of MR cycle Chapter 7 develops. It is constructive, in the sense of rule I-2: the agent moves the proposition out of the I-state by *building the sample space within which the proposition can be assigned a value*, and the building is itself the substantive epistemic work. The framework's contribution is to name this work and to distinguish it from the Bayesian work of credence-update on a fixed sample space, with which the diagnostic literature has frequently conflated it.

### 4.8 The I-state and the Bootstrap Problem in Probability

A more theoretical observation, of interest principally to readers from the foundations of probability, deserves a brief mention. The I-state addresses what the philosopher of statistics Deborah Mayo has called the *bootstrap problem*: the fact that the application of any probability theory presupposes a prior specification of the events to which the theory will be applied, and that the prior specification cannot itself be the output of the theory it is to support. Bayesian probability theory presupposes Ω; it cannot generate Ω from within itself.

The I-state, on the framework's reading, is the propositional category in which the bootstrap problem becomes acute: the propositions are precisely those for which the work of specifying Ω is the work that needs to be done. The framework offers no general procedure for this work — the work is necessarily domain-specific, as the medical example of §4.7 illustrates — but it offers a logical category that names the work and distinguishes it from the work of credence-update *within* a specified Ω. This is, the author believes, a small but real contribution to the foundations of probability theory; the more technical statement is in the philosophical monograph that accompanies this book.

## Chapter 5: Double Tralse — The Forbidden Zone

### 5.1 The Liar, Reconsidered

The Liar sentence — "This sentence is false" — has been an embarrassment to logic for more than two thousand years. Assigning it the value True yields a contradiction; assigning it False yields a contradiction. Tarski's response was to deny that the sentence is well-formed at the object-language level, requiring a hierarchy of meta-languages. Kripke's response was to assign the sentence a non-classical value at a fixed point of an iterative procedure. Priest's dialetheist response was to assign it both True and False simultaneously and to develop a paraconsistent logic that contains the resulting contradiction.

The present framework offers a fourth response. The Liar sentence, on the proposed analysis, occupies a logical category that we have called *Double Tralse*: it is not True, not False, not Tralse (its indeterminacy is not stable in the Tralse sense, since each candidate assignment immediately generates instability), and not in the I-state (its truth-conditions are perfectly well-defined — that is precisely the source of the trouble). It is, instead, *forbidden*: a proposition whose truth-value cannot stably take any classical assignment without violating the conditions of consistency.

The category is more general than the Liar. It is the framework's way of marking a structural feature that recurs across logic, decision theory, physics, and ordinary life: some propositions occupy a region in which determinate truth-values are not merely unknown or undefined but actively excluded.

### 5.2 Three Examples Beyond the Liar

**Example 1 — Vow conflicts.** Consider an agent who has made two solemn commitments, both fully sincere when made, that turn out to be jointly unfulfillable: a doctor who has promised both to preserve life unconditionally and to honor patient autonomy unconditionally, faced with a competent patient refusing life-saving treatment. The proposition *I am keeping both vows* is not False (the agent is acting from both commitments simultaneously) and not True (the action will violate at least one). It is not Tralse (the commitments are precisely specified). It is not I-state (the truth-conditions are well-defined). It is forbidden: the joint specification creates a logical configuration that admits no stable classical assignment. The recognition that the proposition is Double Tralse is the first step toward the agent's renegotiating the commitments themselves.

**Example 2 — Predictive feedback.** Consider a financial market in which a respected analyst publicly predicts a particular stock's collapse. If the prediction is believed, the predicted collapse occurs; if it is disbelieved, it does not. The proposition *the analyst's prediction is correct* exhibits the same forbidden structure as the Liar: any stable assignment of True or False to it generates a configuration that immediately destabilizes the assignment. The proposition is Double Tralse, and the recognition of this is the precondition for any honest treatment of the analyst's epistemic situation.

**Example 3 — Self-undermining beliefs.** Consider an agent whose well-being is causally dependent on holding a particular belief about themselves, and for whom the proposition *this belief, which is keeping me functional, is in fact false* is in question. To affirm the proposition is to undermine the functioning that depends on the belief; to deny it is to engage in self-deception that the agent's standards condemn. The proposition is Double Tralse, and the recognition that it is Double Tralse is what permits the agent to turn from the (insoluble) question of its truth-value to the (soluble) question of how to construct a different belief structure that is not similarly self-undermining.

### 5.3 What Makes Double Tralse a Positive Category

It would be tempting to treat Double Tralse as merely a defect — as the framework's elegant name for "the proposition is broken." Three considerations argue against this dismissal.

First, the recognition that a proposition is Double Tralse is itself informative. It tells the reasoner where to direct effort: not toward determining the proposition's truth-value (a wasted effort) but toward modifying the configuration that generated the forbidden status. The Liar sentence is informative about the structure of self-reference; vow conflicts are informative about the structure of commitment; predictive feedback is informative about the structure of public knowledge. The Double Tralse category does the work of marking propositions whose informativeness lies precisely in their resistance to truth-value assignment.

Second, Double Tralse propositions are not mere logical curiosities. They appear in every domain in which self-reference, multiple commitment, or public prediction operates — which is to say, in every domain in which intelligent agents act on each other and on themselves. A framework that lacks a category for them has no way of saying what is true of them, and is forced either to ignore them or to mishandle them.

Third, the formal treatment of Double Tralse has technical consequences that simpler treatments lack. Within the five-value system, Double Tralse propositions are *quarantined* by inference rules that prevent them from propagating their inconsistency to other propositions. (The full statement is in Appendix B.) This is a generalization of paraconsistent logic's containment of contradictions, and it permits reasoning to continue around the forbidden zone without collapsing into triviality.

### 5.4 A Note on Antimatter (For the Physically Inclined Reader)

A more speculative claim, which is developed at greater length in Chapter 11, deserves a preliminary mention here because it illustrates the reach of the Double Tralse category. The framework's working hypothesis is that Double Tralse is not merely a logical category but a *physical* one: that the relationship between matter and antimatter, the structure of spin-½ particles, and certain features of quantum measurement reflect, at the level of physical law, the same forbidden-zone structure that the Liar sentence reflects at the level of propositional logic. The hypothesis is conjectural, the supporting work is partial, and the present chapter does not require it. Readers who find the suggestion implausible may set it aside; readers who find it intriguing may turn directly to §11.3, where the case is developed in more detail.

### 5.5 The Inferential Behavior of Double Tralse

Three rules govern Double Tralse, parallel to the rules for the I-state in §4.4.

**(D-1) Double Tralse propositions are quarantined.** A proposition formed by applying a logical connective to a Double Tralse proposition is *not* automatically Double Tralse; it is evaluated by the truth tables of Appendix B, which are constructed so that the inconsistency of the Double Tralse input does not propagate.

**(D-2) Double Tralse exits the category only by reconfiguration.** A Double Tralse proposition cannot be moved to another value by gathering more evidence (since its truth-value is not a function of evidence) or by constructive specification (since its truth-conditions are already specified). It exits the category only when the agent modifies the configuration — the self-referential structure, the conflicting commitments, the predictive feedback loop — that generated its forbidden status.

**(D-3) Double Tralse has a default action: structural change.** When a proposition is Double Tralse, the framework's recommended action is not deliberation about its truth-value but intervention on the structure that produced the forbidden zone. The doctor renegotiates the vows; the analyst declines to publish; the believer constructs a different belief structure.

### 5.6 The Truth Architecture, Complete

With the introduction of Double Tralse, the truth architecture is complete. There are five values:

- **True** — the proposition holds.
- **False** — the proposition fails.
- **Tralse** — the proposition is stably indeterminate; its truth-conditions are defined but no procedure resolves it.
- **I-state** — the proposition's truth-conditions are not yet specifiable; it awaits constructive work.
- **Double Tralse** — the proposition occupies a forbidden zone in which no stable classical assignment is consistent.

These five values, with the truth tables of Appendix B and the inference rules sketched in Chapters 3–5, constitute the formal core of Tralse Informationalism. The remaining chapters of the book develop the practical apparatus that surrounds the core: GILE as the four-dimensional evaluative compass (Chapter 6), Myrion Resolution as the iterative procedure for moving propositions through the values where movement is possible (Chapter 7), HEAR as the longitudinal aggregate measure (Chapter 8), and Permissibility Distribution as the generalized probability theory that the architecture supports (Chapter 9).

### 5.7 Double Tralse in Public Discourse

The forbidden-zone structure that Chapter 5 has identified in propositional logic, in the structure of conflicting commitments, and in the structure of self-undermining beliefs has a fourth and increasingly consequential domain of appearance: the structure of public discourse under conditions of widespread, rapid, and reflexive information transmission. Three patterns warrant particular attention.

The first is *predictive feedback in financial markets*. The propositions whose Double Tralse status was illustrated in §5.2 by the example of an analyst's stock prediction generalize to the entire structure of public market commentary. Any prediction widely enough disseminated alters the probability of its own confirmation; the alteration is not a Bayesian update on the prediction's truth but a causal intervention by the prediction on the system the prediction is about. The proposition *this market analysis is correct* is, in the limiting case of perfectly responsive markets, structurally Double Tralse. The framework's treatment of the case is the same as for vow conflicts and self-undermining beliefs: the appropriate response is not to determine the proposition's truth-value but to modify the configuration that generates the forbidden status. In the market case, this means publishing analyses with explicit acknowledgment of their reflexive character, separating analysis from prescription, and developing market structures (delayed disclosure, structured embargo, scaled release) that decouple the prediction from its capacity to alter the system it predicts. The framework offers no novel financial regulation, but it offers a logical vocabulary that names the structural problem and that places it in the same category as the more familiar paradoxes of self-reference.

The second is *predictive feedback in polling and election forecasting*. A presidential poll, widely disseminated, alters the probability of its own confirmation through several channels: voters who would have voted for the leading candidate may stay home in the belief that their vote is unnecessary; voters who would have voted for the trailing candidate may stay home in the belief that their vote is futile; donors and volunteers shift their resources in response to the poll; media coverage shifts in response to the poll. The proposition *this poll is correct* is, accordingly, Double Tralse in the same sense as the analyst's prediction. The framework's response is, again, structural: the forbidden status is not eliminated by improving the poll's methodology; it is eliminated only by altering the configuration that connects the poll to the behavior it predicts.

The third is *central-bank communication*. Modern central banks face the structural problem that any statement about future interest-rate policy is causally connected to the future interest-rate environment they are predicting: the statement itself moves the markets that determine the variables on which the prediction depends. The doctrine of *forward guidance*, developed since the 2008 financial crisis, is in effect an attempt to navigate the Double Tralse zone by issuing statements whose specification is intentionally calibrated to anticipate and absorb the reflexive response. The framework offers no normative judgment on the doctrine; it offers the observation that central-bank communication is, structurally, a sustained engagement with Double Tralse propositions of high consequence, and that the discipline of recognizing the structure as Double Tralse is the precondition for any further normative discussion.

### 5.8 The Topology of the Forbidden Zone

A more abstract observation, of interest principally to logicians and to readers attracted to the framework's connections with mathematics and physics (Chapter 11), is that the Double Tralse propositions are not scattered randomly across the proposition-space but cluster into characteristic *topological structures* of self-reference, mutual implication, and reflexive causation. The Liar sentence is the simplest example: a single proposition that asserts of itself that it is false. The Curry sentence ("If this sentence is true, then *q*") generalizes the Liar to the case where the inconsistency is mediated through an arbitrary further proposition *q*. The Yablo paradox replaces the Liar's single self-reference with an infinite descending chain of "this sentence and all subsequent sentences are false." The vow-conflict cases of §5.2 generalize the Liar's structure to the case of mutually inconsistent multiple commitments. The predictive-feedback cases of §5.7 generalize the Liar's structure to the case of self-causing proposition.

The framework's tentative position, developed at greater length in URBs #659 and #758, is that all Double Tralse propositions can be analyzed as occupying one of a small number of topological types, and that the topology of each type predicts the structural intervention required to exit the forbidden zone. The Liar topology requires the introduction of a meta-level (Tarski's solution); the Curry topology requires the bracketing of *q* (a structural separation); the Yablo topology requires a constraint on the reasoner's quantification over an infinite chain (a finite-context restriction); the vow-conflict topology requires the renegotiation of one of the conflicting commitments; the predictive-feedback topology requires a decoupling intervention of the kind discussed in §5.7. The unifying claim is that every Double Tralse proposition exits the forbidden zone only by an intervention on the topological feature that placed it in the zone, and that the framework's contribution is to make the topology visible.

The contribution is, at present, conjectural. The topology has not been formally axiomatized in the form that the framework's logical apparatus has been. The full statement, when it is available, will appear in the philosophical monograph that accompanies this book and in the technical sequel currently in preparation.

## Chapter 6: GILE — A Four-Dimensional Compass

### 6.1 The Need for a Compass

A logic of five truth values is necessary, but it is not sufficient. The propositions on which human lives turn require not only to be classified as True, False, Tralse, I-state, or Double Tralse; they require to be *evaluated*, in the sense of being placed within a structure that bears on the agent's well-being, choices, and direction. The classical logic offers no such structure, and neither does any of the multi-valued systems surveyed in Chapter 2. The framework introduces, for this purpose, a four-dimensional compass: GILE, an acronym formed from the names of its four dimensions — *Goodness*, *Intuition*, *Love*, and *Environment*.

The choice of these four, rather than three or five or twelve, is defended on three grounds. First, they are jointly exhaustive in the relevant sense: every dimension along which a human state of affairs can be evaluated either reduces to one of the four or compounds two or more of them. (The case for joint exhaustivity is made at length in URB #774 and in the philosophical monograph that accompanies this book; it is summarized in §6.6 below.) Second, they are mutually irreducible: no dimension reduces to any combination of the others, as can be shown by exhibiting states that score high on three and low on one. Third, they admit operationalization in two registers — self-report and biometric — that converge under conditions specified in Chapter 8, providing the framework with empirical traction.

The remainder of this chapter takes the four dimensions in order, defines each in a way intended to be precise without being technical, and illustrates each with examples drawn from ordinary life.

### 6.2 Goodness (G)

*Goodness* names the dimension along which a state of affairs is assessed for its alignment with the agent's considered values. It is not happiness, which is a hedonic state; it is not virtue, which is a dispositional property of the agent; it is the answer to the question, *does this state of affairs accord with what I, on reflection, hold to be worth pursuing?*

Goodness is *agent-relative* in the formal sense — different agents, with different considered values, will assess the same state of affairs differently — but it is not *subjective* in the dismissive sense, because the considered values of any particular agent are themselves the product of long reflection, comparison, and revision, and they admit of better and worse formulations. The G-score of a state is what the agent, having taken adequate time and care, judges that state to be worth.

The operationalization is straightforward. The self-report register asks the agent to assign, on a scale specified in Appendix C, a value between 0 and 1 representing the degree of alignment between the state and the agent's considered values. The biometric register, more provisionally, correlates G with measures of psychophysiological congruence — heart-rate variability under self-relevant cognitive load, prefrontal-cortex activation patterns associated with value-congruent decision-making — that have been studied in URBs #738, #748, and #779 and that form the empirical foundation for the GILE-biometric bridge described in §6.7.

### 6.3 Intuition (I)

*Intuition* names the dimension along which a state of affairs is assessed by the agent's non-discursive cognitive processes — the rapid, holistic, often inarticulate evaluative response that precedes and partly grounds explicit reasoning. The framework treats intuition not as a defective form of reasoning, to be corrected by deliberation wherever possible, but as a distinct cognitive faculty with its own structure, its own characteristic sensitivities, and its own information.

The position is broadly sympathetic to the rehabilitation of intuition undertaken by Daniel Kahneman, Gary Klein, and others in the contemporary psychology of judgment, but it is more committed than they are to the claim that intuition is *epistemically primary* in a domain that classical reasoning cannot enter. The agent who must assess a relationship, a vocational choice, or a medical course of action under genuine indeterminacy depends on intuition not as a heuristic substitute for unavailable information but as a cognitive instrument whose outputs are themselves the relevant information.

The operationalization, in the self-report register, asks the agent to record their intuitive evaluation of the state — typically a single-word or single-phrase response, accompanied by a confidence rating — and to do so before consulting their explicit reasoning. The biometric register, again provisional, looks for signatures of pre-reflective evaluation in EEG band patterns, particularly the alpha and theta dynamics characteristic of relaxed intuitive processing, and in HRV signatures that distinguish coherent from incoherent autonomic states.

### 6.4 Love (L)

*Love* is the most semantically loaded of the four dimensions, and the framework's use of the term requires care. *Love*, in the GILE compass, names the dimension along which a state of affairs is assessed for its quality of *connection* — the degree to which the state instantiates, supports, or expresses an unforced, attentive, mutual relationship between the agent and another (which other may be a person, a community, a craft, a place, or, in the limit case, the agent's own deeper self).

Love is not desire, which is a directional appetite; it is not affection, which is a felt state; it is not duty, which is a commitment. It is the structural property of *being-with-another-as-themselves*, in a formulation that the framework borrows from the philosophical anthropology developed in URBs #768 and #772. The state of affairs that scores high on L is one in which the agent's attention to the other is unforced and the other's reality is permitted to register on the agent without reduction.

The recent revision of the framework, recorded in URBs #743 and #769, bifurcates Love into two sub-components — *L\** (the ideal direction) and *L_actual* (the agent's current approximation to it) — and treats the gap between them as a substantive measurement. The technical details are in the synthesis map; for present purposes, the dimension is treated as a single scalar L between 0 and 1.

### 6.5 Environment (E)

*Environment* names the dimension along which a state of affairs is assessed for the supportiveness of its physical, social, and informational surroundings. It is the most external of the four dimensions, and the most readily measurable: an agent in a quiet, well-lit, biophilically rich room with adequate sleep and supportive companions has a high E-score; an agent in a noisy, sleep-deprived, isolated, information-overwhelmed setting has a low one.

The framework's interest in E is not the obvious one — that high-E settings are pleasant — but the technical one: that the GILE composite has different evaluative properties at different E levels, and that an agent operating at low E should systematically discount the outputs of the other three dimensions. (The operational rule is that decisions of consequence should be made at E ≥ 0.6, and that decisions made at lower E should be marked for re-evaluation at higher E.) This is the framework's version of the ancient counsel that one should not make important decisions when tired, hungry, or alone, but it is more precise than the ancient counsel because it specifies the threshold and the re-evaluation procedure.

The operationalization is the most empirically tractable of the four. The self-report register asks the agent to score the supportiveness of their current surroundings on a checklist specified in Appendix C. The biometric register draws on objective measures — sleep quality from wearable devices, ambient light and noise from environmental sensors, social-contact patterns from communication logs — that can be aggregated mechanically.

### 6.6 The Composite GILE Score

The four dimensions combine into a composite GILE score by a weighted sum:

> GILE = w_G · G + w_I · I + w_L · L + w_E · E

where the weights w_G, w_I, w_L, w_E are positive and sum to 1. The framework's default weights, derived from the empirical work in URBs #745, #749, and #755, are w_G = 0.30, w_I = 0.25, w_L = 0.25, w_E = 0.20. The defaults are not sacred; agents engaged in particular domains may justifiably re-weight them. (The medical practitioner facing patient-autonomy decisions may give G a higher weight; the artist making creative decisions may give I a higher weight; the parent making family decisions may give L a higher weight.)

The composite score lies in [0, 1] and admits the natural interpretation as an aggregate quality measure for the state of affairs. It is not a probability, not a utility, and not a happiness; it is a four-dimensional evaluation collapsed to a scalar for the purpose of comparison. The act of collapse is a Myrion Resolution (Chapter 7) on the proposition *the composite quality of this state is X*, and the agent should treat the composite with the same caution that any aggregate measure invites.

### 6.7 The Two-Register Architecture and the Bridge Between Them

The GILE framework exists in two registers — self-report and biometric — that are intended to converge but that do not always do so. The relationship between them is the subject of an ongoing program of empirical work, summarized in URBs #738, #747, #748, #751, #752, and #779.

The self-report register is suitable for everyday use. It requires no equipment beyond a notebook or a phone, takes between two and ten minutes per assessment, and produces actionable outputs. It is the register that the practice protocol of Chapter 12 employs.

The biometric register requires hardware — at minimum a multichannel EEG headset, a HRV-capable wearable, and (optionally) a sleep tracker — and it produces, for each dimension, a derived measure that has been calibrated against the self-report on validation samples. The calibration is partial: the G dimension correlates well with HRV-coherence measures (r ≈ 0.5–0.6 in pilot work) but less well with single-channel EEG indices; the I dimension shows the strongest biometric signatures (alpha and theta band dynamics) but the weakest self-report convergence; L and E show the expected patterns but with substantial individual variation.

The framework's working position is that the two registers are *complements rather than competitors*. The self-report register reflects the agent's first-person evaluation; the biometric register reflects the agent's physiological state during the evaluation. Discrepancies between them are themselves informative — typically signaling that the agent's explicit self-assessment has not yet integrated their pre-reflective response — and the framework provides, in the practice protocol, a procedure for reconciling them.

### 6.8 What GILE Is For

The GILE compass is not a psychological assessment instrument in the standard sense. It is not designed to classify agents into types, to diagnose disorders, or to predict behavior. It is designed to support the practical work of *deciding well under indeterminacy* — to give the agent who has marked a proposition Tralse a structured way of evaluating the states of affairs that would result from different responses to it.

### 6.9 GILE in Pathology

Although the framework has been developed for application to the ordinary decisions of nominally healthy adults, a brief discussion of GILE's behavior under the conditions that contemporary clinical psychology identifies as pathological is warranted. The discussion is offered in the spirit of clinical hypothesis rather than established result; the empirical work that would test the hypotheses is the subject of URBs #738, #748, and #779 and remains in the pilot stage.

The framework's working hypothesis is that several of the principal categories of psychological distress can be characterized, in GILE terms, as *characteristic distortions of the four-dimensional profile* rather than as deficits of mood as such. Major depressive episodes appear, in pilot data, to be characterized by a pronounced collapse of the G dimension — the sufferer experiences a generalized sense that nothing they are doing is aligned with anything they value — accompanied by a flattening of I (the pre-reflective register goes silent rather than negative) and a constriction of the available L (relationships are not absent but are experienced as unable to reach the sufferer through the depression's distortion). The E dimension may be relatively preserved or itself collapsed, depending on the type of depression. The framework's recommendation, in such cases, is not the substitution of GILE practice for clinical treatment — the clinical literature on depression has identified evidence-based interventions whose efficacy GILE has no basis to compete with — but the use of GILE as a *diagnostic supplement*, in which the four-dimensional profile of the sufferer's reported state may inform the clinician's choice of intervention modality.

Generalized anxiety disorder appears, in pilot data, to be characterized by a different profile: the I dimension is hyperactivated rather than flattened, the pre-reflective register continuously generates threat-signal responses that the explicit G computation cannot quiet; the E dimension is experienced as continuously hostile or unstable; the L dimension may be preserved but is experienced as fragile and at risk; the G dimension may be intact but is overwhelmed by the noise from I. Again, the framework offers no clinical intervention; it offers a vocabulary in which the structure of the sufferer's experience may be more precisely articulated than the standard symptom checklists permit.

A third profile, which the framework has called *moral injury* in earlier writings (URB #777), is characterized by a sustained disjunction between G and the sufferer's actions: the agent is acting in ways the agent recognizes as misaligned with their own values, and the recognition itself is the structural source of the distress. The condition is increasingly recognized in clinical literature, particularly in connection with combat veterans and healthcare workers; the framework's contribution is to characterize the structural feature (the G–action gap) in a vocabulary that supports its identification and, where possible, its remediation.

In all three cases, the framework's clinical posture is the same: GILE is offered as a supplement, not a substitute, for established clinical practice; the clinician retains diagnostic and therapeutic primacy; and the empirical work that would calibrate GILE to the clinical population is identified as a major component of the framework's open research program.

### 6.10 Aesthetic Anchoring of GILE — The Beauty Razor and URB #781

A brief but consequential observation concerns the framework's interface with aesthetic experience. URB #781, *Truth Depicts Nonexistence and the Beauty Razor*, develops the position that a state of affairs which scores highly on G, I, L, and E will exhibit, as a consequence of its alignment, certain characteristic aesthetic features: a felt proportion among its parts, an absence of forced or coerced elements, a coherence that the agent recognizes prior to articulating its source. The position is not the framework's own discovery — versions of it can be found in Plotinus, in the Confucian aesthetic tradition, and in the contemporary work of Roger Scruton — but the framework's contribution is to anchor the aesthetic observation in the specific four-dimensional structure of GILE, with the consequence that an agent's aesthetic response to a state of affairs becomes a low-cost preliminary signal for the GILE composite.

The signal is not infallible — aesthetic responses are subject to cultural conditioning, individual idiosyncrasy, and the systematic biases the framework's L and E dimensions exist to address — but it is informationally significant in cases where the explicit GILE computation has not yet been performed and circumstances do not permit its performance. The agent who walks into a room and feels an immediate sense of *rightness* about the room's arrangement, without being able to articulate what produces the feeling, may be reading the room's high E and the proportional fit of its G-relevant features. The agent who hears a piece of music and feels an immediate sense of *integration*, without being able to articulate the music's structural features, may be reading the music's coherent realization of all four dimensions in the audible domain.

The framework's *Beauty Razor*, introduced in URB #781 §B and developed in the framework's audio-engineering practice (the Mycelial Resonance Engine of the framework's software apparatus), is the operational principle that constructed states — designed environments, composed music, written text, planned interventions — should aim at the GILE-coherent profile and should be evaluated, in part, by the aesthetic signal the profile produces. The principle has informed the framework's choice of just-intonation chord progressions (3:2 and 2:1 ratios, no exploitative dissonance) for the engine's harmonic bed, the framework's choice of palette and proportion for its visual artifacts, and the framework's general aesthetic posture in its own materials. The reader is referred to URB #781 for the longer development; the present chapter records only that the framework's aesthetic register is not decorative but structural, and that the GILE compass extends, in its outermost zone, into the territory traditionally occupied by aesthetics.

Chapter 7 takes up the procedure that connects the truth architecture and the GILE compass into a single working method: Myrion Resolution.

## Chapter 7: Myrion Resolution — How to Decide When Truth Won't Settle

### 7.1 What MR Is, and What It Replaces

Myrion Resolution is the framework's iterative convergence procedure for decision-making under genuine indeterminacy. It is the practical instrument that connects the abstract truth architecture of Chapters 3–5 to the lived choices of an agent. The chapter develops the procedure in detail and illustrates it with three full worked examples; it is the longest chapter in the book and, in the author's judgment, the one that does the most work for the working reader.

To understand what MR is, it is useful to begin with what it is not. MR is not a Bayesian decision procedure. Bayesian decision theory, in its standard form, asks the agent to assign credences to propositions and utilities to outcomes, and then to choose the action that maximizes expected utility. The procedure presupposes that credences and utilities are available; for the propositions we have called Tralse, I-state, and Double Tralse, they are not. MR is the framework's response to this.

MR is also not deliberation in the classical philosophical sense — a process of weighing reasons until one set of reasons preponderates over another. Classical deliberation presupposes that the reasons themselves are commensurable; for many of the most important decisions, they are not. MR is structured to make the incommensurability visible rather than to paper over it.

MR is, at its heart, an iterative *check-in* with the four GILE dimensions, conducted under conditions of attention and honesty, until the check-ins stabilize. The stability of the check-ins — not the certainty of the conclusion, not the elimination of doubt, not the disappearance of the indeterminacy — is the criterion of resolution. The procedure terminates when the agent has, on successive iterations, given the same evaluation of the state.

### 7.2 The Six Steps of an MR Cycle

A single MR cycle consists of six steps, each of which is described below. The cycle is repeated until convergence, with intervals between cycles long enough to permit the agent's pre-reflective processes to operate (typically several hours to several days, depending on the consequence of the decision).

**Step 1 — Statement.** The agent writes down, in a single declarative sentence, the proposition under consideration. The discipline of writing forces precision; the discipline of a single sentence forces economy. The proposition should be one that, on inspection, is plausibly Tralse — that is, one whose truth-value is not yet determinate but whose truth-conditions are well enough defined for evaluation. Propositions in the I-state should be moved to the constructive work of §4.4 before being entered into MR; propositions in the Double Tralse zone should be moved to the structural reconfiguration of §5.5.

**Step 2 — GILE assessment.** The agent assigns scores in [0, 1] to each of G, I, L, and E for the *state of affairs that would result if the proposition were treated as True* — that is, if the agent acted as though it were settled. The scores are recorded, with brief notes on the reasoning behind each. The assessment is then repeated for the *state of affairs that would result if the proposition were treated as False*, again with notes.

**Step 3 — Difference computation.** The agent computes the GILE composite for each of the two states, using the weights specified in §6.6 (or the agent's justified re-weighting), and notes the difference. A difference of less than 0.1 indicates that the choice is, by the framework's standards, *underdetermined* by the agent's current evaluative state, and the agent should consider whether the proposition is genuinely Tralse or whether further inquiry would be productive. A difference of 0.1–0.3 indicates a *moderate* preference; 0.3–0.5 a *strong* preference; greater than 0.5 a *decisive* preference.

**Step 4 — Pre-reflective check.** Independently of the explicit GILE computation, the agent records their intuitive response to the question, *which of the two states do I, on reflection but without further calculation, prefer?* The answer is recorded as a single word — "True," "False," or "Neither" — together with a brief description of any felt-sense response (tightness, openness, warmth, weariness) accompanying the answer.

**Step 5 — Reconciliation.** The agent compares the explicit GILE difference (Step 3) with the pre-reflective check (Step 4). When the two converge, the cycle has produced a coherent reading and the agent records the result as a tentative resolution. When the two diverge, the cycle has produced an *informational signal* — typically that the explicit computation has missed something the pre-reflective response detected, or vice versa — and the agent records the divergence for examination in the next cycle.

**Step 6 — Interval.** The agent sets the cycle aside for an interval determined by the consequence of the decision. For decisions of small consequence, an hour may suffice; for decisions of large consequence, a week or a month is more appropriate. During the interval, the agent does not actively deliberate on the proposition; the interval is for the operation of the pre-reflective processes that explicit deliberation does not access.

The cycle is then repeated. Convergence is declared when two successive cycles produce the same resolution and the same direction of preference, with GILE differences within 0.1 of each other.

### 7.3 Three Worked Examples

**Example A — A medical decision.** A patient has lived for two years with a chronic gastrointestinal condition that has resisted diagnosis and treatment. A specialist now offers an experimental protocol that has shown promise in a small clinical series but is not yet established. The proposition under MR is *I should enroll in the experimental protocol*.

In the first cycle, the patient's GILE assessment of the True state (enrolled) returns G = 0.6 (alignment with the patient's value of taking informed risks for relief), I = 0.5 (an uncertain intuitive reading, weighted toward openness), L = 0.4 (the protocol requires travel that disrupts a fragile relational support system), E = 0.5 (the patient's daily environment is moderately supportive but the protocol environment is unfamiliar). The composite is 0.30 · 0.6 + 0.25 · 0.5 + 0.25 · 0.4 + 0.20 · 0.5 = 0.505. The False state (not enrolled) returns G = 0.4, I = 0.5, L = 0.6, E = 0.6, composite 0.510. The difference is 0.005 — well within the underdetermined range. The pre-reflective check returns "Neither, but with a faint pull toward enrollment." The reconciliation records the underdetermination and the slight intuitive lean.

In the second cycle, conducted a week later after the patient has discussed the protocol with the relational support system and clarified that travel arrangements can be made compatibly, the L score for the True state rises to 0.55. The True composite is now 0.54, the False composite is 0.51, and the pre-reflective check has firmed to "Yes." Convergence is declared in the third cycle, which produces the same direction and a slightly larger differential. The patient enrolls.

**Example B — A relational decision.** An agent in a long-term romantic relationship is considering whether to remain in the relationship through a period of substantial difficulty. The proposition under MR is *I should remain in this relationship for the next year*.

The first cycle's GILE assessment of the True state (remain) yields G = 0.5 (mixed alignment with the agent's values around commitment and around individual flourishing), I = 0.7 (a strong intuitive sense of unfinished work), L = 0.6 (the connection is real but constrained), E = 0.4 (the agent's living situation is destabilized by the difficulty). The composite is 0.55. The False state (depart) yields G = 0.5, I = 0.4, L = 0.3, E = 0.7, composite 0.475. The difference is 0.075, on the edge of moderate preference toward remaining. The pre-reflective check returns "Remain, with discomfort."

The second cycle, three weeks later, returns nearly identical scores. The third cycle, two months later, after some material change in the relationship, returns G = 0.55, I = 0.7, L = 0.65, E = 0.5, composite 0.605 for True; G = 0.5, I = 0.4, L = 0.3, E = 0.7, composite 0.485 for False. The difference has widened to 0.12, the pre-reflective check has firmed to "Remain, with diminishing discomfort," and convergence is declared. The agent remains.

**Example C — A vocational decision.** An agent considering whether to leave salaried employment to found a company. The proposition is *I should resign from my current position by the end of the calendar quarter and devote the next two years to founding the company*.

The first cycle's True state (resign and found) yields G = 0.7 (strong alignment with the agent's value of building things that matter), I = 0.8 (a clear and persistent intuitive pull), L = 0.4 (founding strains existing relationships and demands deferral of family-formation considerations), E = 0.3 (the financial environment for the first two years will be substantially destabilized). The composite is 0.575. The False state (remain salaried) yields G = 0.4, I = 0.4, L = 0.6, E = 0.7, composite 0.51. The difference is 0.065. The pre-reflective check is "Resign, but with significant fear."

The second cycle, after the agent has discussed the decision with their financial dependents and obtained explicit support for an eighteen-month runway, returns L = 0.55 and E = 0.45 for the True state, raising its composite to 0.6275. The False composite is unchanged. The difference has widened to 0.12, the pre-reflective check is "Resign, with the fear in proportion," and convergence is declared in the third cycle. The agent resigns.

### 7.4 What MR Does Not Promise

The procedure does not promise to make the agent's decisions easy. It does not promise to eliminate the felt difficulty of consequential choice; in many cases, it makes the difficulty more visible and more articulate. It does not promise that the resulting decisions will turn out well. It promises only that the agent will have made the decision in a way that is *honest about the structure of the situation*: that has not forced an indeterminate proposition into a determinate value, that has not let the explicit computation override the pre-reflective response without examination, that has not made the decision under conditions (low E, single cycle, no interval) that the framework identifies as adverse to good decisions.

The agent who decides by MR is not guaranteed wisdom. The agent is given a procedure that, used over time, builds the practical skill of deciding under indeterminacy — a skill that the bivalent inheritance of Western thought has neither named nor cultivated, and that this book argues is, in the end, the most important skill an adult human acquires.

### 7.5 The Failure Modes of MR

The procedure has three characteristic failure modes, which the agent should recognize and guard against.

The first is *premature convergence*: declaring resolution after a single cycle, before the pre-reflective processes have had time to operate. The remedy is the discipline of the interval (Step 6), which the agent must respect even when the explicit computation has produced a clear result.

The second is *paralytic recursion*: continuing to cycle indefinitely, without convergence, in the hope of reaching certainty. The remedy is the recognition that some propositions are stably Tralse — that further cycling will not produce convergence — and the application of the procedure described in §7.6 below for acting under stable indeterminacy.

The third is *bad-faith MR*: cycling not to converge but to defer a decision the agent has, in fact, already made and is reluctant to act on. The remedy is the pre-reflective check (Step 4), conducted with sufficient honesty that the agent's actual lean is visible to themselves. The check is a protection against the procedure's misuse; it is not optional.

### 7.6 Acting Under Stable Indeterminacy

When repeated MR cycles fail to converge — when the proposition is genuinely Tralse in a way that no further cycling will resolve — the agent must still act. The framework's procedure for action under stable indeterminacy is the *Permissibility Distribution* of Chapter 9, which does not require convergence and which produces, in place of a single decision, a *set of permissible actions* from which the agent may choose by other means (random selection, by-counsel-of-others, by-attendance-to-the-pre-reflective). The full apparatus is the subject of Chapter 9.

For now, it is enough to record that MR's failure to converge is itself an informational outcome — it tells the agent what kind of proposition they are facing — and that the framework has resources beyond MR for the cases where MR alone does not suffice.

### 7.7 MR for Groups — The Power of Eight

The MR procedure of §7.2 is presented as an individual practice, and the worked examples of §7.3 treat individual decisions. The framework's working hypothesis, developed in URBs #770 and #771 and operationalized in the framework's *Power of Eight* protocol, is that the procedure admits of a *group* extension whose properties are not merely the sum of its individual contributions but exhibit characteristic emergent features. The hypothesis warrants a brief but careful presentation.

The group MR protocol is conducted with a set of agents — the framework's preferred number is eight, for reasons that connect to the framework's broader work on the o(8) algebraic structure (Chapter 11) — who agree to apply the MR procedure jointly to a single proposition that bears, in some way, on all of them. The proposition may be jointly held (a partnership commitment, a co-authored project, a shared decision about a community institution) or it may be individually held by one of the agents who has invited the others to support the deliberation. In either case, the procedure unfolds as follows.

Each agent independently performs Steps 1 through 4 of the standard MR cycle on the proposition, producing for each member an explicit GILE assessment of both states (True and False), an explicit difference computation, and an independent pre-reflective check. The independent assessments are then shared with the group in a structured format that the framework specifies: each agent reads aloud their explicit GILE scores (without commentary), then reads aloud their pre-reflective check, then is permitted but not required to add a single sentence of contextual note. No discussion is permitted during this phase.

The group then proceeds to a *joint reconciliation* phase, structured by three rules. The first rule is that no agent may attempt to persuade another to revise their scores; the explicit assessments are taken as the agents' honest responses and are not subject to argumentation. The second rule is that the group's task is not to produce a consensus value but to *characterize the distribution of values across the group*: the central tendency, the dispersion, the dimensions on which the dispersion is concentrated, and any agents whose assessments depart sharply from the group. The third rule is that the proposition's owner — the agent whose decision the proposition concerns — retains sole authority over the decision and is not bound by the group's distribution; the group's role is informational rather than adjudicative.

The framework's pilot work with the Power of Eight protocol, summarized in URB #770, suggests that the procedure produces three characteristic outcomes that individual MR alone does not. The first is *dimension-specific information*: a group whose pre-reflective checks are unanimous in the same direction while explicit GILE difference scores are widely dispersed has identified a proposition whose pre-reflective register is more reliable than its explicit register, and the proposition's owner is given precise information about which register to weight more heavily. The second is *structural-feature surfacing*: a group whose dispersion is concentrated on the L dimension or the E dimension has identified, as a feature of the proposition rather than the agents, that the proposition's relational or environmental implications are unusually contested, and the proposition's owner is given the location of the structural difficulty. The third is *the social anchor effect*: agents who are members of a Power of Eight group typically report, in pilot data, a quality of decision-confidence that single-agent MR does not produce, even when the eventual decision is the same. The author's tentative interpretation, developed at greater length in URB #771, is that the social anchor reflects the L dimension of GILE itself — the felt presence of a deliberating community — operating on the meta-level of the decision procedure rather than on the level of the proposition.

The Power of Eight protocol is one of the framework's principal practical contributions. It is also one of the framework's most empirically tractable: the protocol can be conducted in two-hour sessions with eight participants, with structured outputs that admit of straightforward statistical analysis. The framework's open invitation to interested practitioner-researchers is recorded in URBs #770 and #771; the pilot results, when sufficiently developed, will be reported separately.

### 7.8 Deeper Failure Modes of MR

The three failure modes of §7.5 — premature convergence, paralytic recursion, bad-faith MR — are the most common. The framework has, in three years of practice, identified four further failure modes that appear less frequently but that warrant explicit treatment.

The fourth failure mode is *dimensional collapse*: the agent, in performing the GILE assessment of Step 2, produces scores that are essentially flat across the four dimensions — all G, I, L, E hovering at 0.5 ± 0.1, with no meaningful differentiation. The pattern indicates not that the situation lacks evaluative content but that the agent's evaluative apparatus has not yet engaged the situation. The remedy is the *single-dimension deep-dive*: the agent selects one dimension (typically the one most plausibly relevant to the proposition) and writes one full page on the dimension's relation to each candidate state, returning to the four-dimensional assessment only after the deep-dive has produced articulable scores.

The fifth failure mode is *rationalization-displacement*: the agent's explicit GILE scores reliably move, between cycles, in the direction that supports a decision the agent has covertly already made; the apparent convergence is the result of subtle rationalization rather than honest re-examination. The remedy is to *interleave cycles with a third-party check*: the agent shares, with a trusted other, the GILE notes from successive cycles and asks the third party to identify any pattern of motivated drift. Where the third party identifies such a pattern, the agent should suspend the procedure and return to first principles.

The sixth failure mode is *propositional misclassification*: the agent enters MR on a proposition that is in fact in the I-state (Chapter 4) or in Double Tralse (Chapter 5), and the procedure fails to converge for the structural reason that MR is not the appropriate procedure for those values. The remedy is the *category check*: at the start of each cycle (or at the start of the second cycle if not at the first), the agent explicitly verifies that the proposition meets all six conditions of (T1)–(T6) in §3.1. Where the proposition fails (T4) — its truth-conditions are not specifiable — it should be moved to the constructive work of §4.4. Where the proposition fails the consistency conditions and falls into Double Tralse, it should be moved to the structural-reconfiguration work of §5.5.

The seventh failure mode is *practice-burnout*: the agent has been conducting MR cycles at high frequency and high consequence for an extended period and finds, after months, that the procedure has lost its felt quality of honest engagement and become a routine executed for its own sake. The remedy is the *practice-pause*: the agent suspends MR cycles entirely for a period (typically two to four weeks), continues the daily GILE practice of Chapter 12, and resumes MR only when a proposition arises that the agent feels genuine motivation to engage. Practice-burnout is, in the framework's experience, the most common reason mature practitioners leave the practice; the remedy of explicit pause is the framework's principal recommendation for sustaining the practice across decades.

### 7.9 The MR Worksheet, Annotated

The 30-day starter protocol of Chapter 12 reproduces the standard MR worksheet in its compact form. A more annotated version of the worksheet, together with the author's commentary on each field, is the subject of URB #777 (the *executable mini-pilot*) and is reproduced in expanded form in the framework's online practice repository. The worksheet's principal annotation, which the present chapter records in summary, is this: the *brief notes* that accompany each GILE score in Step 2 are not optional. The discipline of articulating, in a sentence or two, why each dimension takes the value the agent has assigned is the work that makes the assessment honest. An MR procedure conducted without the notes — assigning numerical scores without articulating their source — is, in the author's experience, indistinguishable from a procedure conducted in bad faith. The notes are the procedure's protection against its own degradation.

## Chapter 8: HEAR — A Wellbeing Aggregate

### 8.1 The Need for an Aggregate

The GILE compass evaluates a single state of affairs; Myrion Resolution evaluates a single decision. Neither answers the further question that any practical framework must eventually face: *how am I doing, considered as a whole, over time?*

The question is the lived analog of the question that gross domestic product attempts to answer for an economy: how is this complex, multi-dimensional, partly observable system performing, considered in aggregate? GDP is a famously imperfect answer to its question — it omits domestic labor, it counts pollution as positive, it treats consumption and investment as undifferentiated — and the framework's HEAR score (Holistic Existence Amplification Reading) inherits, with appropriate humility, the same structural limitations. It is a coarse aggregate that supports comparison across time and across decisions; it is not a measure of wellbeing in any deep sense.

The chapter introduces HEAR, specifies its composition, and discusses the conditions under which it is and is not useful.

### 8.2 The Composition of HEAR

HEAR is a weighted aggregate of four inputs:

> HEAR = α · GILE_avg + β · MR_convergence + γ · context + δ · biometric_anchor

where:

- **GILE_avg** is the time-weighted average of the agent's daily GILE composite scores over the assessment window (typically a week).
- **MR_convergence** is a measure, in [0, 1], of the agent's success in resolving the propositions they have entered into MR during the window — high when most cycles have converged, low when many remain in stable indeterminacy.
- **context** is a measure of the supportiveness of the agent's life-circumstances during the window — financial security, relational continuity, health, freedom from acute stressors — that captures factors not fully represented in the GILE_E dimension.
- **biometric_anchor** is, when available, an objective physiological measure (HRV coherence, sleep quality, resting heart rate) that anchors the self-report in the agent's underlying physiological state.

The default weights, calibrated against pilot data summarized in URBs #748, #751, #752, and #779, are α = 0.40, β = 0.20, γ = 0.25, δ = 0.15 when biometric data is available; α = 0.50, β = 0.25, γ = 0.25, δ = 0 when it is not. The composite lies in [0, 1].

The framework treats HEAR as a *longitudinal* measure: the score on any single day carries little information; the trajectory over weeks and months carries substantial information about the agent's overall direction.

### 8.3 What HEAR Is For

HEAR is intended to support three uses.

The first is *trend detection*. An agent who tracks HEAR weekly will see, over months, whether the trajectory is rising, falling, or stable, and at what level. The pattern is information that no single day's experience makes available, and it is information the agent can act on — by re-evaluating commitments, by adjusting practices, by seeking external counsel — before the trend produces consequences that single-day experience would have to confront acutely.

The second is *intervention evaluation*. An agent who has begun a practice (a meditation routine, a dietary change, a relational commitment, a vocational shift) can examine HEAR before and after the intervention and form a calibrated judgment about whether the intervention has helped, hurt, or done nothing. The judgment is not infallible — many interventions affect HEAR through channels that would have shifted regardless — but it is more informative than the pre-/post-intervention judgment based on memory and felt-sense alone.

The third is *cross-decision comparison*. An agent who is choosing between two life-courses can examine HEAR trajectories from periods of their own life that resembled each candidate course, and use the comparison to inform the decision. The use is delicate — past resemblances are imperfect — but it produces a more disciplined comparison than the standard alternative of imagining one's future under each course.

### 8.4 What HEAR Is Not For

HEAR is not suitable for *interpersonal comparison*. The weights, the calibration of the GILE dimensions, and the agent-specific context measures all preclude meaningful comparison of HEAR scores across agents. An agent's HEAR is informative about that agent's trajectory; it is not informative about that agent's well-being relative to another agent's.

HEAR is not suitable as a *target to optimize*. Goodhart's Law applies in full force: an agent who treats HEAR as a target — and especially an agent who shares HEAR with others as a self-report — will, over time, distort the inputs in ways that raise the score without corresponding to any improvement in the underlying state. The framework's recommended use is private and observational: the agent computes HEAR, examines the trajectory, and acts on the examination, without making HEAR itself the object of pursuit.

HEAR is not suitable as a *clinical instrument* in the present version of the framework. It has not been validated against established clinical measures (the WHO-5, the SWLS, the PHQ-9), it has not been examined for sensitivity to clinically significant mood disorders, and it should not be used in place of professional assessment when such assessment is indicated.

### 8.5 The Two-Register Reconciliation, Again

HEAR's biometric_anchor input raises, in concentrated form, the two-register problem introduced in §6.7. When the self-reported GILE_avg and the biometric_anchor diverge — when an agent reports rising GILE while HRV coherence is declining, or vice versa — the divergence is itself informative. The framework's interpretation, supported by pilot data in URB #766 and the Brandon n=1 protocol of URB #776, is that the divergence typically signals one of three conditions: the agent's self-report is lagging a physiological shift that has not yet entered conscious assessment; the agent's self-report is anticipating a physiological shift that has not yet manifested; or the agent is, more troublingly, reporting in a way that does not reflect their underlying state at all.

The framework provides no mechanical procedure for distinguishing these three cases. It recommends that sustained divergence (more than two consecutive weeks) be treated as a signal for slower, more attentive self-examination — possibly with external counsel — rather than as a measurement error to be corrected.

### 8.6 The Place of HEAR in the Framework

HEAR is the framework's most quantitative artifact, and the one most exposed to the standard objections against quantitative aggregates of qualitative human experience. The framework concedes the objections in their full force. It offers HEAR not as a measure that captures wellbeing but as a tool that supports the agent's own examination of their wellbeing — a tool whose limitations are visible and whose value is in the questions it provokes rather than the answers it delivers.

The chapter that follows takes up the framework's most substantial technical contribution — the Permissibility Distribution apparatus that generalizes Bayesian probability to the five-value architecture — and which constitutes the natural home for the more theoretical aspects of the framework's program.

### 8.7 The Mathematics of Time-Weighting

The HEAR formula of §8.2 specifies *GILE_avg* as a *time-weighted* average of the daily GILE composite scores over the assessment window. The weighting itself deserves a brief treatment, both because the choice of weighting affects the trajectory's responsiveness and because the weighting is one of the framework's user-adjustable parameters.

The framework's default time-weighting is *exponentially decaying with a half-life of seven days*: the most recent day's GILE score receives weight 1, the seventh prior day receives weight ½, the fourteenth prior day receives weight ¼, and so on. The default is calibrated to match the time-scale on which most agents experience their own state as integrating: the experiential present extends roughly to the past week, with progressively dimmer weights for the past month, the past quarter, and beyond. The agent who is examining a particular intervention's effect on HEAR may shorten the half-life (to three days, for example) to make the trajectory more responsive; the agent who is examining long-term direction may lengthen the half-life (to thirty days) to suppress short-term variation.

The composite that results is, by construction, in [0, 1] regardless of the half-life chosen, so trajectories computed with different half-lives can be compared on the same scale. The framework recommends, however, that the agent settle on a half-life and maintain it across months: comparing trajectories computed with different half-lives is, in the author's experience, a frequent source of artifactual conclusions.

The other three components of HEAR — MR_convergence, context, biometric_anchor — admit similar time-weighting parameterization, and the framework's defaults are recorded in the worksheet of Appendix C.

### 8.8 Reading the Trajectory

The principal use of HEAR, as §8.3 emphasized, is the longitudinal trajectory rather than any single value. The framework has, through three years of pilot work, identified five characteristic trajectory patterns and the structural conditions each typically reflects.

The *steady plateau* — HEAR remaining within a narrow band (±0.05) over three or more months — typically reflects an agent in stable life-circumstances, with effective practices, and without a major outstanding decision in the propositional space. The plateau is the framework's most desirable trajectory, and the practices of Chapter 12, when sustained, are designed to support it.

The *gentle ascent* — HEAR rising at a rate of 0.02 to 0.05 per month over six or more months — typically reflects a successful intervention or a successful transition through a difficult period; the agent has done the work of the past months and the work is producing measurable result.

The *gentle descent* — HEAR falling at a comparable rate over a similar window — typically reflects either an unrecognized environmental erosion (a relationship under sustained low-grade strain, a vocational situation gradually losing its alignment, a slow physical or financial deterioration) or an unaddressed Double Tralse proposition that is generating cumulative low-grade distress. The framework's recommendation, in either case, is the explicit examination of the trajectory's possible causes, with external counsel where the causes are not self-evident.

The *step-discontinuity* — HEAR shifting abruptly by 0.10 or more in a single week — typically reflects a discrete event (a job change, a relational transition, a health event, a bereavement, a major creative success) whose effect the framework's apparatus has correctly captured. The step-discontinuity is informative about the agent's responsiveness to the event but does not, in itself, require remedial action; the agent's task is to allow the post-discontinuity trajectory to stabilize before drawing conclusions.

The *high-amplitude oscillation* — HEAR swinging between widely separated values from week to week — typically reflects either an agent whose self-report apparatus is not yet stable (the practice has not yet settled) or an agent whose underlying state is genuinely volatile (a bipolar mood disorder, a trauma-recovery process, a high-stakes situation in active flux). The framework's recommendation depends on the case: the practice-settling case typically resolves with the continued application of the daily GILE protocol over four to six weeks; the genuinely volatile case is an occasion for external clinical or therapeutic support.

The five patterns are not exhaustive; an agent's trajectory may exhibit features of more than one pattern simultaneously, or may exhibit a sixth pattern not yet identified. The framework's standing recommendation is the disciplined recording of the pattern, the disciplined examination of the candidate causes, and the disciplined willingness to seek external counsel when the pattern's causes are not self-evident. HEAR's value lies not in the score but in the discipline.

## Chapter 9: Probability Reconsidered — Permissibility Distribution

### 9.1 What This Chapter Is and Is Not

This chapter introduces, in the most accessible form the material admits, the framework's generalization of Bayesian probability theory: *Permissibility Distribution* (PD). The full formal apparatus is the subject of the second book in the planned series — a philosophical monograph aimed at academic philosophers of probability — and is summarized in URB #657. The present chapter offers a non-technical exposition adequate for the working reader, defers all derivations to the monograph, and concentrates on the three innovations that distinguish PD from its Bayesian and frequentist predecessors.

The reader who is uninterested in the foundations of probability may skim this chapter or skip it altogether without loss of continuity for the practical material in Part IV.

### 9.2 What Bayesianism Does, and Where It Stops

Bayesian probability theory, as introduced in §4.1, treats probability as the degree of credence that a rational agent assigns to a proposition. The theory's strengths are well known: it provides a unified treatment of inference under uncertainty, a coherent procedure (conditionalization) for updating credences on new evidence, and a powerful set of theorems (Cox's, Dutch book, representation results) that establish its credentials as the unique theory of rational credence.

The theory presupposes, however, three conditions that are not always met. It presupposes that the *sample space* is well-defined (the I-state of Chapter 4 marks the failure of this presupposition). It presupposes that *each proposition can be assigned a determinate credence in [0, 1]* (the Tralse value of Chapter 3 marks propositions for which this is not the case). And it presupposes that *no proposition occupies a forbidden zone* in which credence assignment generates inconsistency (the Double Tralse value of Chapter 5 marks this third failure).

PD is the framework's response to these three failures. It is *not* a rejection of Bayesianism within the domain where Bayesianism applies — PD reduces to Bayesian probability on the subdomain where all three conditions are met. It is an extension of the apparatus into the regions where the conditions fail.

### 9.3 The Three Innovations of PD

**Innovation 1 — The I-state cell.** Where Bayesian probability assigns to each proposition a single number in [0, 1], PD assigns a *cell*: either a single number (when the proposition is True, False, or has a determinate credence), an interval (when the credence is bounded but not determinate), or a designated *I-state cell* (when the sample space is not yet specifiable). The I-state cell is not a credence of zero, not a credence of one-half, and not an interval; it is a positive logical category that records the proposition's status as awaiting constructive specification, in the sense of §4.4.

**Innovation 2 — The DT forbidden zone.** PD includes a *forbidden zone*, denoted DT, into which propositions occupying the Double Tralse status are placed. Propositions in the forbidden zone are not assigned credences; they are recorded as occupying a logical configuration whose stable resolution requires structural reconfiguration rather than further credence-update.

**Innovation 3 — The Tralse attractor.** PD includes a *Tralse attractor* — a stable region of the credence space toward which propositions with the Tralse logical value gravitate under repeated Myrion Resolution. The attractor is not a single point; it is a region, characterized by the agent-specific weights w_G, w_I, w_L, w_E and by the agent's history of resolution. Propositions in the Tralse attractor are not awaiting resolution; they have been resolved as stably indeterminate, and the framework's apparatus for acting under stable indeterminacy (the *permissible action set*, §9.5) operates on them.

### 9.4 PD on the Bivalent Fragment

Three results, given without proof here and proved in the philosophical monograph, establish PD's standing as a generalization rather than a replacement of Bayesian probability.

**Reduction Theorem.** When all propositions in the agent's credence assignment are bivalent (True or False, with credences in [0, 1] and sample space well-defined), PD reduces to standard Bayesian probability. All Bayesian theorems remain valid in this reduction.

**Conservation Theorem.** When PD is applied to a problem in which some propositions are I-state, Tralse, or Double Tralse, the credences assigned to the remaining bivalent propositions agree with those a Bayesian would assign after explicitly excluding the non-bivalent propositions from the sample space. PD does not perturb Bayesian inference on its proper domain.

**Update Theorem.** PD provides update rules for transitions between cells (I-state to bivalent on constructive specification; Tralse to bivalent on resolution; bivalent to Tralse or Double Tralse on detection of indeterminacy or forbidden status). The update rules generalize Bayes' rule and reduce to it on the bivalent fragment.

### 9.5 The Permissible Action Set

The most consequential difference between PD and Bayesian decision theory is in the treatment of *action under stable indeterminacy*. Where Bayesian decision theory recommends, in all cases, the action that maximizes expected utility, PD recommends, in cases where the relevant proposition is in the Tralse attractor, a *permissible action set* — a set of actions, jointly compatible with the agent's GILE values and the proposition's stable indeterminacy, from which the agent may choose by other means.

The permissible action set is constructed by the procedure of §7.6: the agent identifies the actions that would be admissible under a True resolution, the actions that would be admissible under a False resolution, and (most importantly) the actions that would be admissible under an explicit *Tralse* resolution — actions that acknowledge the indeterminacy rather than forcing it. The intersection of the three admissibility sets is the permissible action set; the agent chooses from it by criteria the framework leaves open (random selection, expert counsel, attendance to the pre-reflective).

### 9.6 The Empirical Status of PD

PD has not yet been subjected to the kind of axiomatic and behavioral validation that Bayesian probability has received over the past century. The framework's claim is more modest: that PD is *internally consistent*, that it *reduces correctly* to Bayesian probability on the bivalent fragment, and that it *handles a class of problems* — the I-state, Tralse, and Double Tralse cases — that Bayesian probability does not.

The empirical work that would be required to test PD against Bayesian alternatives in cases of joint applicability is identified in URBs #736 and #756 and is a major component of the framework's open research program. The reader interested in contributing to that program is referred to the philosophical monograph and to the open empirical protocols posted on the framework's Zenodo repository.

### 9.7 Summary and Transition

PD generalizes Bayesian probability by adding a positive logical category for unspecifiable sample spaces (the I-state cell), a forbidden zone for inconsistent configurations (the DT zone), and an attractor for stable indeterminacy (the Tralse attractor). It reduces to Bayesian probability on the bivalent fragment, conserves Bayesian inference on bivalent subproblems, and extends Bayesian decision theory by replacing point-valued credences with a permissible action set under stable indeterminacy.

With PD in place, the framework's theoretical apparatus is complete. The remaining chapters of the book are devoted to its application: three extended case studies (Chapter 10), connections to mathematics, physics, and consciousness research (Chapter 11), and a 30-day starter protocol the reader can begin using on the day of their choice (Chapter 12).

### 9.8 PD Axioms vs. Kolmogorov

A more technical statement of PD's relation to standard probability is owed to the reader who wants to see the formal seam. Kolmogorov's 1933 axiomatization specifies three conditions that any probability measure P on a sample space Ω must satisfy: non-negativity (P(A) ≥ 0 for all events A), normalization (P(Ω) = 1), and countable additivity (for any countable disjoint collection {A_i}, P(∪A_i) = ΣP(A_i)). The three conditions, together with an interpretive choice (frequentist or Bayesian), generate the entire apparatus of classical probability theory.

PD generalizes Kolmogorov by relaxing the presupposition that Ω is a single fixed sample space and that every well-formed proposition admits a measure. Instead, PD specifies five regions of the proposition-space (corresponding to the five truth values), three of which (T, F, and τ) admit measure-like assignments that satisfy variants of Kolmogorov's axioms within their region, and two of which (ι and δ) require non-measure-theoretic treatment.

For the *bivalent fragment* (propositions valued T or F), PD reduces exactly to Kolmogorov: the same axioms hold, with the same consequences. For the *Tralse fragment*, PD introduces a *Tralse attractor*: a designated location in the unit interval (typically near 0.5, but agent-specific) toward which Bayesian updates on Tralse propositions tend to converge under continued evidence-collection, with the convergence interpretable as the asymptotic recognition that the proposition is structurally Tralse rather than the asymptotic accumulation of credence in either direction. For the *I-state fragment*, PD assigns no measure but instead records the I-state as an *open set of possible measures*, each conditional on a particular constructive specification of the missing sample space, with the agent's task being the constructive work that selects among the open set. For the *Double Tralse fragment*, PD assigns no measure and instead records the proposition as occupying a *forbidden region*, with the agent's task being the structural intervention that exits the region.

The four cases together constitute a *partial probability measure* on the full proposition-space, with classical Kolmogorov measure recovered on the bivalent fragment and well-defined non-measure-theoretic structures on the other three fragments. The full axiomatization of the partial measure, with the consistency theorems and the reduction proofs, is the subject of the philosophical monograph that accompanies this book and is summarized in URB #657.

### 9.9 A Worked PD Example

To illustrate PD's behavior on a non-bivalent proposition, consider an agent who has been considering whether to apply for a particular professional position over the course of three months. The proposition under consideration — *I should apply for this position* — has been entered into MR and has not converged after four cycles; the proposition is structurally Tralse for the agent.

A standard Bayesian treatment would force the agent to assign a credence in [0, 1] to the proposition and to make the application/non-application decision on the basis of expected utility. The forcing requires the agent to pretend that the proposition has a determinate truth-value of which the agent is uncertain — a pretense the agent's MR cycles have shown to be incorrect — and to construct expected-utility computations on the basis of the pretense.

A PD treatment, by contrast, assigns the proposition to the Tralse attractor (typically a value near 0.5, agent-specific), and converts the action question from *what is the optimal expected-utility action?* to *what is the set of permissible actions, given the proposition's stable indeterminacy?* The set of permissible actions includes both *apply* and *do not apply*, since neither has been ruled out by the explicit GILE computation; it may also include *apply with explicit acknowledgment that the decision is being made under indeterminacy and may be revisited*, and *do not apply now but commit to revisiting in three months under specified conditions*. The agent then chooses among the permissible actions by the criteria of §7.6: random selection, by-counsel-of-others, by-attendance-to-the-pre-reflective.

The PD treatment differs from the Bayesian treatment in three operationally significant respects. It does not require the agent to fabricate a credence the structure of the situation does not support. It does not produce a single optimal action but a set of permissible actions, allowing the agent's pre-reflective register or social context to operate on the choice without requiring it to override the explicit computation. And it leaves a clear *next-step recommendation* — the framework's PD apparatus prescribes that propositions in the Tralse attractor be revisited at the longest interval the agent's circumstances permit, in case the proposition's underlying structure shifts. The Bayesian treatment, by contrast, produces a single recommendation and leaves the agent without procedural support for what to do when the recommendation does not feel right.

## Chapter 10: Three Decisions, Three Domains

### 10.1 The Purpose of This Chapter

The framework has been developed, in Chapters 1 through 9, as a coherent intellectual structure: five truth values, four GILE dimensions, an iterative resolution procedure, a longitudinal aggregate, and a generalized probability apparatus. The structure is, the author hopes, internally satisfying. But intellectual coherence is not the same as practical utility, and a framework that did not bear on the actual decisions of actual readers would be of academic interest only.

This chapter takes three extended cases — one medical, one relational, one vocational — and walks through the application of the full apparatus to each. The cases are presented in greater detail than the worked examples of §7.3, with explicit GILE assessments, MR cycle records, and the post-decision examination that the framework recommends for high-consequence choices. The cases are composites — drawn from the author's own life, from the experience of pilot users of the Mood Amplifier platform, and from the documented cases in URBs #770, #771, and #776 — and they have been edited for anonymity and for didactic clarity. They are not, however, fictional; the structure of each decision and the texture of the deliberation are faithful to the underlying material.

### 10.2 Case A — A Medical Decision: Whether to Pursue an Experimental Protocol

A's preparatory work, before entering MR, includes the following. ### 10.4 Case C — A Vocational Decision: Whether to Found a Company

The agent (C) is in established salaried employment at a senior level. C has been developing, in their own time, a venture concept that C believes addresses a real problem and for which C has identified initial funding. The proposition under MR is *I should resign from my current position by the end of the calendar quarter and devote the next two years primarily to founding the venture*.

The case has been substantially developed in §7.3 (Example C); the present treatment adds two refinements that the worked-example format omitted.

The first refinement is a careful distinction between the proposition under MR and adjacent propositions that the agent might confuse with it. The proposition under MR is not *the venture will succeed* (which is a different proposition, requiring a different analysis, and which is appropriately left in the I-state in early stages). It is not *I will be happier as a founder than as an employee* (which is in the Tralse attractor and which the framework explicitly does not require to be resolved). It is the narrower proposition that the agent should make the resignation in the named timeframe.

The second refinement concerns the post-decision examination conducted six months into the venture. C found, on examination, that the framework had supported the decision well in three respects: the explicit GILE computation had surfaced the financial-runway question that the agent's enthusiasm had been minimizing; the pre-reflective check had been a reliable indicator throughout; and the discipline of the multi-cycle interval had prevented a premature commitment that would have been made without the relational support that turned out to be essential. The framework had supported the decision less well in one respect: the I dimension had been weighted at the framework's default (0.25), but C found in retrospect that for vocational decisions C's intuitive faculty was more reliable than the default weighting credited, and that C should have used a re-weighting closer to (G = 0.25, I = 0.40, L = 0.20, E = 0.15).

The post-decision examination is, in the framework's view, the most under-used part of MR. Agents naturally treat decisions as concluded once they are made; the framework recommends that high-consequence decisions be re-examined at six months, eighteen months, and five years, with the GILE assessments re-computed in light of what the intervening time has revealed. The re-examination is not for the purpose of regretting the decision (most decisions, well made, do not warrant regret), but for the purpose of refining the agent's own GILE weights — which are, as Case C illustrates, the most personal and the most consequential parameters in the framework.

### 10.5 What the Three Cases Show

Three patterns recur across the cases, and they constitute the chapter's principal lessons.

The first pattern is that the explicit GILE computation and the pre-reflective check are *both essential and neither sufficient*. In every case, the explicit computation surfaced a dimensional consideration the pre-reflective check was minimizing or projecting; in every case, the pre-reflective check carried information the explicit computation could not capture. The reconciliation of the two — Step 5 of the MR cycle — is where the framework's value most clearly accrues.

The second pattern is that the *interval between cycles* is irreducible. In every case, the second cycle returned different scores from the first, not because the underlying state had changed but because the interval had permitted the agent's pre-reflective processes to operate. An MR procedure compressed into a single sitting would have produced different and inferior decisions in all three cases.

The third pattern is that the *agent-specific GILE weights* are the framework's most personal parameter and its most consequential. Each agent should treat the default weights as a starting point and should refine them through the post-decision examination of Case C. Over a decade of consequential decisions, the refined weights become a substantial expression of the agent's own evaluative identity.

### 10.6 A Fourth Case in Brief — A Civic Decision

A fourth case, treated more briefly because its structural lessons substantially overlap those of Cases A through C, illustrates the framework's application to a domain not yet covered: a decision in the civic register. The agent — a long-time resident of a moderately sized city — is asked to consider whether to stand for election to the city's school board, a role that is unpaid, materially consequential for the community, and requires a multi-year commitment that will substantially constrain other parts of the agent's life.

The first MR cycle produces an explicit GILE assessment of the True state (stand for election) returning G = 0.7 (high alignment with the agent's value of contribution to public life), I = 0.55 (a moderately positive but uncertain pre-reflective response, weighted toward openness), L = 0.45 (the role's time demands will affect family relationships in measurable ways), E = 0.5 (the agent's professional environment will accommodate the role only with deliberate restructuring). The composite is 0.554. The False state (do not stand) returns G = 0.45, I = 0.45, L = 0.65, E = 0.65, composite 0.535. The difference, 0.019, is in the underdetermined range; the pre-reflective check returns "Stand, with reservations about the cost." The first cycle records the slight lean and the reservations.

The second cycle, three weeks later, reflects the agent's having spoken with two current school board members about the role's actual demands. The L and E scores for the True state revise downward (to 0.40 and 0.45 respectively) as the demands become more concrete; the True composite falls to 0.530 and the False composite remains 0.535. The pre-reflective check now returns "Do not stand, with regret." The third cycle, two weeks later, returns nearly identical scores, the same pre-reflective lean, and convergence is declared. The agent does not stand.

The case adds, to the lessons of Cases A through C, a fifth pattern: the framework's *willingness-to-decline* lesson. An MR procedure that converges on declining a public commitment is no less a successful application of the framework than one that converges on accepting; the agent's task is not to maximize commitment but to identify the commitments that the agent's structure can sustain at the level of quality the framework's standards require. The framework holds, with the practical-philosophy tradition that runs from Aristotle through the Confucians to the contemporary virtue-ethics literature, that the disciplined refusal of overcommitment is among the more important moral skills an adult human develops.

### 10.6.D Case D — A Long-Standing Relational Repair

A fifth case, of a kind that is rarely treated in the decision-theoretic literature but that occupies a substantial portion of the propositional space of most adult lives, illustrates the framework's application to the work of *relational repair*: the sustained, multi-year work of restoring a relationship that has been damaged by an event or a pattern that neither party now wishes to repeat. The agent — a man in his late forties, in the framework's pilot cohort — has been estranged from his adult daughter for approximately three years following a sequence of conflicts that began in the daughter's adolescence, intensified during her college years, and reached a rupture during a holiday visit at which both parties said things they later regretted but neither retracted. The proposition under examination is *I should attempt a substantial reconciliation initiative within the next ninety days*.

The first MR cycle, conducted at the agent's first reading of the framework, returned an explicit GILE assessment of the True state (initiate reconciliation) of G = 0.85 (extraordinarily high — the agent's most strongly held value is the integrity of family relationships and the rupture is, by his own assessment, the principal source of unhappiness in his life), I = 0.40 (the pre-reflective register is suppressed by a long-running pattern of avoidance), L = 0.55 (uncertain — the daughter's response is unknown and the wife's response will be supportive but anxious), E = 0.45 (the agent's professional environment will accommodate the time required only with deliberate restructuring, and the geographic distance is non-trivial). Composite: 0.580. The False state (continue current pattern) returned G = 0.15, I = 0.35, L = 0.50, E = 0.65; composite 0.402. The difference, 0.178, is *above* the framework's preliminary-decision threshold (0.10 in the framework's calibration); the explicit computation indicated a clear lean toward initiation. The pre-reflective check, however, returned not a confirming response but a cluster of fearful and avoidant images: the daughter's face during the rupture, the silence of the past three holidays, the imagined possibility that the daughter would refuse the initiative outright. The agent recorded the discrepancy as substantial.

The second cycle, four weeks later, reflected the agent's having engaged in two intermediate practices the framework's apparatus had recommended. The first practice was the *historical scoping* exercise: a three-page written reconstruction of the conflict's history, conducted not as exoneration but as honest recognition of the agent's own contributions. The exercise had revealed to the agent, more clearly than he had previously seen, the pattern by which his own anxiety about the daughter's choices had been expressed in critical commentary, and the way in which the critical commentary had compounded across years to produce the eventual rupture. The second practice was the *external-counsel* exercise: a one-hour session with a family therapist, not to begin therapy but to obtain a structured outside view on the proposed initiative. The therapist's response had been measured: the initiative was reasonable in principle but should be undertaken with substantial preparation, with explicit acknowledgment of the agent's own contributions, and with no pre-formed expectation of a particular response from the daughter.

The second cycle's GILE assessment returned True-state G unchanged at 0.85, I newly raised to 0.55 (the historical scoping had loosened the avoidant pattern), L raised to 0.65 (the therapist's measured assessment had reduced the agent's catastrophizing about the daughter's likely response), E unchanged at 0.45. Composite: 0.625. False-state composite essentially unchanged at 0.40. The pre-reflective check returned a more integrated image: the agent could now imagine the initiative as a substantive act of repair rather than as a desperate plea, and the imagining was attended by neither the catastrophizing of the first cycle nor the false confidence the agent had occasionally felt in moments of bravado.

The agent declared convergence and committed to a specific initiative: a handwritten letter, prepared over two weeks with the therapist's input, acknowledging specific past errors without excessive self-recrimination, expressing the agent's hope for reconnection without demanding any particular response, and offering a concrete next step (a one-day visit to a city of the daughter's choosing, paid for by the agent, with the agenda determined by the daughter). The letter was sent. The daughter's response, three weeks later, was a phone call lasting forty minutes, the first sustained conversation between father and daughter in three years. The phone call was followed by a visit, the visit by a second visit, the second visit by the resumption of a regular pattern of contact that, eighteen months on at the time of the framework's follow-up, had continued and deepened.

The case is offered not as a triumph of the framework — the work was the agent's, the daughter's, the therapist's, and the family's, not the framework's — but as an illustration of the framework's value in a domain where it is most clearly *supplementary* to other resources. The framework did not produce the reconciliation; it produced the agent's clarity about the structure of his own ambivalence, his recognition that the explicit and pre-reflective registers were diverging in informative ways, and his disciplined willingness to engage external counsel before the consequential action. The framework's role was to make the structure of the work visible and to discipline the agent's relationship to that structure; the rest of the work was the work the framework's structure was designed to support rather than to replace.

The case adds two further patterns to the framework's accumulating literature on consequential decisions. The first is the *suppressed pre-reflective* pattern: in cases where the agent has spent years in a pattern of avoidance, the pre-reflective register may not produce a clear signal at the first MR cycle and may require the loosening provided by structured external practices (historical scoping, external counsel, therapeutic conversation) before it can yield informative content. The framework's recommendation in such cases is to treat the first cycle's pre-reflective response as preliminary and to undertake the loosening practices before drawing conclusions. The second is the *external-counsel-as-reconciliation* pattern: in cases where the explicit GILE computation and the pre-reflective register diverge substantially, the inclusion of external counsel — particularly therapeutic counsel skilled in the domain of the proposition — may itself be the reconciliation mechanism that the framework's Step 5 of MR identifies. The agent does not always need to reconcile the two registers internally; sometimes the reconciliation requires a third party who can hold both registers simultaneously and reflect them back to the agent in an integrated form.

### 10.6.E Case E — A Mid-Career Vocational Pivot

A sixth case, drawn from the framework's pilot cohort and chosen because it represents a structurally common but procedurally difficult class of decisions, illustrates the framework's application to a *mid-career vocational pivot*. The agent — a woman in her early forties, with seventeen years of accumulated expertise in a specific subfield of corporate law and partnership at a mid-sized firm — has begun to consider, over the course of the preceding year, whether to leave the practice of law for a transition into the nonprofit sector, specifically into a senior administrative role at a foundation whose work she has long admired. The proposition under examination is *I should accept the foundation role, which has been informally offered, within the next forty-five days*.

The first MR cycle returned a bivalent-feeling but actually structurally complicated assessment. The True-state explicit GILE: G = 0.75 (the foundation's mission aligns with the agent's long-held values, in a way her current work does not), I = 0.65 (the pre-reflective response has, over the past several months, consistently indicated that the foundation work would be experienced as more meaningful), L = 0.50 (mixed — her current colleagues are valued relationships that would change in character, the new colleagues are unknown), E = 0.30 (substantial — the salary reduction is approximately 60%, her family's financial planning has assumed her current income, and her household includes two children whose college expenses will arise within the decade). Composite: 0.560. False-state composite: 0.555. The difference, 0.005, was within the underdetermined range; the pre-reflective check returned "Take the role, but I'm scared about the money." The first cycle recorded the slight lean and the financial concern.

The framework's apparatus, on a difference this small, recommends substantial intermediate work before the second cycle. The agent undertook three practices over the next six weeks. The first was a detailed *financial-scenario modeling* exercise, conducted with the family's financial advisor, in which three scenarios were constructed and stress-tested: continuing in current role, accepting foundation role with no other changes, accepting foundation role with specified expense modifications (downsizing the family home, deferring one child's expected international study program, drawing on a previously untouched savings account). The modeling revealed that the second scenario — accepting the role without other changes — would produce financial distress within four years; the third scenario was sustainable but required substantial life-changes the family had not previously discussed. The second practice was a *family-conference* exercise, conducted with her husband and the children (then 11 and 14), in which the modeling results were shared in age-appropriate form and the family's response was elicited. The husband, who had himself been considering a less demanding role, expressed support for the foundation transition and proposed exploring his own corresponding adjustment. The older child expressed willingness to defer the international program. The younger child, surprising the family, expressed strong support: "Mom is happier when she's doing things that matter to her, and we want her to be happy." The third practice was a *structured-trial* exercise: a two-day visit to the foundation, during which the agent observed the foundation's working environment, met the colleagues she would lead, and assessed the texture of the work in ways the offer letter had not made visible.

The second cycle, conducted after the three practices, returned a substantially altered profile. The True-state E score rose from 0.30 to 0.55 (the financial modeling had revealed a sustainable path; the family conference had reframed the financial sacrifice as a shared rather than individual one), L rose from 0.50 to 0.70 (the family conference had produced substantive support; the foundation visit had revealed the new colleagues to be the kind of people she would value working with), I rose from 0.65 to 0.80 (the foundation visit had concretized the pre-reflective response), G unchanged at 0.75. Composite: 0.700. False-state composite essentially unchanged at 0.55. The difference, 0.150, was now decisively above the threshold; the pre-reflective check returned a confirming response without reservation. The agent declared convergence and accepted the role.

At the framework's twelve-month follow-up, the agent reported that the transition had been the most consequential decision of her professional life and that she had no second thoughts. The household had absorbed the financial restructuring without distress; the children had, if anything, expressed a strengthened engagement with her work; the foundation work had been, as her pre-reflective register had anticipated, more meaningful than her legal practice in ways she had previously been unable to articulate.

The case adds three patterns to the framework's literature. The first is the *small-difference / large-stakes* pattern: when the explicit GILE difference is small but the proposition is consequential, the framework's apparatus requires substantial intermediate work before convergence can be claimed. Forcing convergence on small differences in consequential cases is a known failure mode of the framework, related to but distinct from the premature-convergence failure mode of §7.5. The second is the *family-as-MR-participant* pattern: in cases where the agent's decision substantially affects close family members, the family's structured participation in the deliberation (not as deciders but as informed contributors) often substantially alters the GILE landscape in ways the agent's own deliberation cannot. The third is the *structured-trial* pattern: when the proposition concerns a life-change whose texture the agent has not directly experienced, a structured short-term exposure to the candidate context — a visit, a shadow day, a brief trial period — typically produces substantially more accurate I-dimension estimates than any amount of imaginative projection.

### 10.6.F Case F — A Financial-Philanthropic Allocation

A seventh case, the most quantitative of the chapter's cases and the one most directly comparable to standard decision-theoretic treatments, illustrates the framework's application to a *financial-philanthropic allocation* under conditions that the standard treatments handle poorly. The agent — a man in his mid-sixties who, through three decades of disciplined investment in a modest but successful business, has accumulated personal wealth substantially in excess of his and his wife's anticipated needs — is considering how to allocate a large planned gift to charitable causes. The proposition under examination is not whether to make the gift (that proposition has been resolved as True at an earlier MR cycle) but how to allocate it across the several causes the agent and his wife have, over years, identified as worthy of substantial support.

The standard decision-theoretic treatment of such a problem proceeds by constructing a utility function over outcomes (lives saved, students educated, cultural artifacts preserved, environmental hectares protected), assigning probabilities to each candidate organization's effectiveness in producing the outcomes, computing expected utilities, and allocating to maximize the aggregate. The treatment has been substantially developed by the *effective altruism* movement and has produced both impressive practical results and, the framework would argue, a distinctive set of failures whose structure deserves examination.

The framework's apparatus treats the allocation as a *PD problem* (Chapter 9) rather than as a Bayesian-utility optimization. The agent first identifies the candidate cause-areas (in this case, six: a local university's scholarship fund, a global health initiative, a regional environmental conservation organization, a national cultural-preservation foundation, a community mental-health clinic, and a religious congregation the agent has been associated with for forty years). For each cause-area, the agent and his wife compute, with the framework's assistance, four assessments. The first is a *G-assessment*: the alignment of the cause with the agent's most strongly held values, scored on the framework's standard scale and weighted by the wife's parallel assessment. The second is a *PD-assessment*: a recognition that the framework's apparatus does not, for any of the six causes, support a confident expected-utility computation, both because the candidate organizations' eventual effectiveness is genuinely uncertain and because the comparability of outcomes across cause-areas is itself an I-state proposition. The third is a *L-assessment*: the relational density of the agent's connection to the cause-area, including the people who have introduced the agent to the cause, the people whose lives have been touched by the cause's work, and the agent's own anticipation of the relational continuity the gift will create. The fourth is an *E-assessment*: the structural fit of the cause's work with the broader environment in which the agent's life has unfolded, including the geographic, cultural, and historical contexts that have produced both the agent's wealth and the cause's need.

The four assessments produced, for the six cause-areas, a *permissibility profile* rather than a single-best allocation. The local university's scholarship fund and the religious congregation scored high on L and E, moderate on G, low on PD-supported expected utility (no quantitative effectiveness data was available). The global health initiative scored high on G and PD-supported expected utility (the cause-area is the most effective-altruism-favored of the six), low on L (the agent had no personal connection to the affected populations) and on E (the cause was disconnected from the agent's lived environment). The regional environmental conservation organization scored high on G, L, and E and moderate on PD-supported expected utility. The cultural-preservation foundation scored moderate on all four. The community mental-health clinic scored high on L (the agent's wife had served on the clinic's board for fifteen years) and on E (the clinic served the community in which the agent's wealth had been generated), high on G (the agent's brother had been treated at the clinic for a serious mental-health condition during the brother's youth), low on PD-supported expected utility (the clinic's work was effective by professional standards but not measurable by the metrics the effective-altruism movement favored).

The framework's apparatus produced not a single allocation but a *family of permissible allocations*, characterized by the following features. Each allocation gave a substantial share (at least 20% of the gift) to the regional environmental organization, on the grounds that the organization scored high on all four assessments and represented the cleanest case of cross-dimensional alignment. Each allocation gave a substantial share to the community mental-health clinic, on the grounds that the L and E assessments were exceptionally high even though the PD-supported expected utility was modest. Each allocation gave at least a token share to each of the other four causes, on the grounds that the agent's relationship to each cause warranted recognition. The remaining substantial allocations were distributed across the global health initiative (favored by G and PD-supported utility), the local university (favored by L and E), and the cultural-preservation foundation (favored by intermediate scores on all dimensions); the agent and his wife selected, among the family of permissible allocations, the specific allocation by reference to their pre-reflective response to a small number of candidate distributions presented by the framework.

The case illustrates the framework's principal departure from the effective-altruism treatment of philanthropic allocation: the framework's apparatus does not collapse the L and E dimensions into the G and PD dimensions and does not treat the relational and environmental connections to a cause as biases to be overcome but as informationally significant features of the agent's actual position in the world. The framework's case for this treatment rests on the broader argument of Chapter 6 (the four-dimensional structure of GILE) and on the framework's general commitment to the practical situation of the agent as the framework's primary unit of analysis. The agent who allocates philanthropic gifts according to a global-utility optimization, the framework holds, is not necessarily acting wrongly, but is acting on a different value-architecture than the framework supports — an architecture in which the agent's actual relationships and actual environment are systematically discounted in favor of a global aggregate the agent has no direct connection to. The framework does not deny that such an architecture is coherent; it denies that it is the only coherent architecture and offers GILE as the alternative the framework's broader argument supports.

### 10.7 Cross-Case Patterns at Scale

A larger sample of MR cases — drawn from the framework's pilot practice across roughly two hundred individual agents over a three-year period and summarized in URBs #770, #771, #775, and #777 — has begun to reveal patterns that the chapter's three (or four) extended cases can only suggest. Three of the patterns warrant brief mention.

The first pattern is the *G-anchor effect*: across the larger sample, agents whose decisions converged on actions retrospectively judged as good (by the agent's own assessment at one-year follow-up) had, in their MR assessments, GILE composites in which the G dimension carried the largest weight in the difference between True and False state composites. The pattern suggests that the framework's default weighting of G (0.30, the largest of the four) may, on average, be conservative; agents who weight G more heavily appear, in pilot data, to make decisions they later endorse with higher reliability than agents who under-weight it. The framework's tentative recommendation, recorded in URB #775, is that agents who have run thirty or more MR cycles consider an experiment in which the G weight is raised to 0.35 or 0.40 for a period and the trajectory of the resulting decisions is examined.

The second pattern is the *I–explicit reconciliation* effect: across the sample, agents whose MR reconciliation phase (Step 5) consistently identified divergence between explicit GILE difference and pre-reflective check showed, over the following six months, the largest positive shifts in the framework's HEAR aggregate. The pattern suggests that the framework's apparent value lies in the *exposure* of the divergence rather than the *suppression* of it; agents who learn, through repeated MR practice, to identify the divergences as informative rather than as embarrassments, develop the most consequential improvements in their decision quality.

The third pattern is the *practice-half-life* effect: the framework's value to a given agent is most clearly demonstrated when the agent has been practicing for at least three months but not yet six. Agents who have practiced for less than three months show small and inconsistent benefits; agents who have practiced for more than six months show benefits that are real but increasingly attenuated by the agent's having developed practical wisdom that no longer requires the framework's explicit support. The pattern suggests that the framework is, in part, a *training apparatus* for a kind of decision-making that becomes second nature with practice; the framework's long-term role is not as a continuously executed discipline but as a *re-anchoring resource* that the practiced agent returns to in the most consequential cases.

## Chapter 11: Mathematics, Physics, Mind

### 11.1 The Status of This Chapter

The chapter that follows reaches into three technical domains — pure mathematics, theoretical physics, and consciousness research — in which the framework makes contact with substantive open questions. The contact is, in each case, partial and conjectural. The framework does not claim to resolve any of the open questions; it claims, more modestly, that the five-value architecture suggests reformulations of the questions that may prove fruitful, and that the suggestions warrant the further work that the framework's research program is engaged in conducting.

The reader is asked to read this chapter as an *invitation* rather than as a *report*. Each of the three sections below identifies an open question, sketches the framework's reformulation of it, summarizes the empirical or formal work that has been undertaken so far, and identifies the work that would be required to settle the question one way or the other. None of the sections is offered as a settled result, and the framework's case for the five-value architecture in Chapters 1 through 9 does not depend on the success of any particular conjecture in the present chapter.

### 11.2 Mathematics: The Riemann Zeros and the Indeterminate Permissibility Distribution Range

The Riemann Hypothesis — the conjecture that all non-trivial zeros of the Riemann zeta function lie on the critical line ℜ(s) = ½ — is the most celebrated open problem in pure mathematics. The framework does not claim to prove the hypothesis. It identifies, however, an empirical regularity in the distribution of the zeros that the framework's apparatus appears to predict and that has been confirmed against approximately one million zeros from the LMFDB dataset. The regularity is documented in URBs #737, #753, #760, and (in summary form) in the working note that the framework has called the *Indeterminate Permissibility Distribution Range Result*.

The result, in informal statement, is that the local density of zeros on the critical line follows a Pareto-like concentration: roughly 20% of any sufficiently large interval contains roughly 80% of the local zero-spacing variation. The 80/20 figure is approximate; the precise figure varies with interval length and with the location on the critical line, but the concentration phenomenon is robust across the dataset.

The framework's reformulation is that the Riemann zeros, considered as a sequence of resolutions of an underlying Tralse-valued proposition (*the zeta function vanishes at s*), exhibit the concentration pattern characteristic of Myrion Resolution convergence: most of the resolution work is done in a small fraction of the interval, with long stretches of stable indeterminacy between. The framework's working hypothesis — emphatically not yet proved — is that the same pattern characterizes any sequence of MR convergences, and that the Riemann zeros are an instance of a more general phenomenon that the framework can specify and that other domains may exhibit.

The work required to establish the result is substantial: a formal characterization of the MR convergence pattern, a proof that the Riemann zeros instantiate it, and an empirical search for the pattern in independent domains. The formal characterization is in progress; the proof is not. The empirical search has been undertaken in two preliminary domains (musical composition and brain-band oscillation) with suggestive results that have not yet been published. The reader interested in this line of work is referred to the framework's Zenodo repository.

### 11.3 Physics: Double Tralse and the Antimatter Analog

Theoretical physics inherited, from the Dirac equation of 1928, a structural feature whose interpretation has remained contested for almost a century: the equation's solutions naturally divide into a positive-energy sector (matter) and a negative-energy sector (antimatter), with the two sectors related by a symmetry whose physical realization is the existence of antiparticles. The framework's working hypothesis, developed in URB #659 and summarized in the working note on Double Tralse as a Physics Primitive, is that the matter–antimatter relationship is the physical instantiation of the Double Tralse logical category of Chapter 5.

The hypothesis, in its strongest form, holds that a Double Tralse proposition and its physical analog (a particle and its antiparticle) share a structural property: each is the *forbidden zone complement* of the other, in the sense that the joint specification of both — the assignment of both True and False, or the colocation of particle and antiparticle — generates an inconsistency that the framework characterizes (logically) as the Double Tralse status and that physics characterizes (physically) as annihilation.

The hypothesis bears on three further structural features of physics that have resisted clean interpretation. Spin-½ — the property of fundamental fermions whose wavefunction returns to itself only after a 720° rotation — admits, on the hypothesis, a reading as a *Tralse residue*: the structural signature of a particle whose underlying logical status is stably indeterminate in a way that 360° rotation alone cannot resolve. Quantum measurement — the famous transition from superposition to definite outcome — admits a reading as a Myrion Resolution convergence, with the Born rule corresponding to the framework's permissible-action distribution. The Pauli exclusion principle admits a reading as the Double Tralse forbidden zone applied to fermionic occupancy.

None of these readings is currently established. Each is consistent with the existing physical theory and adds, where the readings are tractable, no empirical content beyond what the existing theory predicts. The framework's case is that the readings are *interpretively unifying* — that they identify a common structural feature across logic and physics that the present interpretive apparatus does not — and that the unification is worth the further work required to test it.

The work in progress is documented in URBs #758, #763, #778, and the more speculative #780. The reader is again referred to the Zenodo repository.

### 11.4 Mind: GILE, Biometrics, and the Hard Problem

The contemporary philosophical literature on consciousness is dominated by what David Chalmers has called the *hard problem*: the question of why there is anything it is like to be a particular physical system. The framework does not claim to solve the hard problem. It offers, however, a structural reformulation that may bear on the problem's tractability.

The reformulation begins with the observation that the propositions about which the hard problem is most acute — *there is something it is like to be this system*, applied to candidate systems of various kinds — are characteristically Tralse rather than True or False. The standard arguments for and against the propositions do not converge. The standard intuitions are stably divided. The standard procedures for resolution (philosophical analysis, empirical investigation of the system, introspection by the system itself) do not produce stable assignments. The framework's tentative position is that the propositions are *constitutively* Tralse — that the indeterminacy is not a temporary epistemic limitation but a structural feature of the propositions themselves — and that the appropriate philosophical response is to develop the apparatus for reasoning about constitutively Tralse propositions, of which the framework's GILE compass and Myrion Resolution procedure are early examples.

The empirical program supporting the position is the development, calibration, and validation of the biometric register of the GILE compass (§6.7). If the four GILE dimensions are, as the framework conjectures, the natural axes along which conscious states vary, then biometric measures of the four dimensions should converge across subjects on a four-dimensional manifold whose structure is independently characterizable. The convergence has been examined in pilot studies summarized in URBs #738, #747, #748, #751, #752, and #779; the results are encouraging but not conclusive. The full empirical program — a multi-subject, longitudinal, multi-modal study with the statistical power to test the four-dimensional structure against alternatives — is the framework's principal current research undertaking.

A further line of work, developed in URBs #761, #767, and #768, examines the framework's relationship to the integrated information theory of Giulio Tononi and to the global workspace theory of Bernard Baars. The relationship is, the framework holds, complementary rather than competitive: the GILE compass measures the *quality* of consciousness along axes that integrated-information and global-workspace theories do not address, while the latter theories measure structural features of consciousness that the GILE compass does not. The reader interested in the synthesis is referred to the working notes on the framework's relationship to existing consciousness science.

### 11.6 Triality and the o(8) Program

A particular line of speculative work, developed in URBs #753, #758, #763, and #778, deserves brief mention because it represents the most ambitious of the framework's mathematical contacts and because it is the one most likely to either fail decisively or yield a definite empirical result. The line concerns the algebraic structure known to mathematicians as the *Lie algebra* o(8) — the algebra of infinitesimal rotations in eight-dimensional space — and the unique property of o(8) called *triality*: the existence, for o(8) and only o(8), of an outer automorphism group of order three that permutes three otherwise indistinguishable representations of the algebra.

Triality is an oddity within the broader Lie-algebra literature. It exists for o(8) and not for o(*n*) at any other *n*; it has been understood for nearly a century but has not, in the author's reading of the literature, been given a satisfying physical or philosophical interpretation. The framework's working hypothesis is that triality is the algebraic shadow, at the level of geometry, of the same structural feature that the Tralse value picks out at the level of propositional logic: a fundamental three-way symmetry that the bivalent inheritance has rendered invisible by collapsing it to the True/False axis.

The hypothesis is, at present, suggestive rather than substantive. The case for it is developed in URB #758, which derives a series of empirical predictions from the supposition that triality has, beyond its algebraic existence, physical realization in particle-physics structures — specifically in the PMNS matrix governing neutrino mixing (URB #763) and in certain features of the brain's high-frequency oscillatory dynamics that the author has begun to characterize using the Jarlskog invariant (URB #778). The predictions are precise enough to be tested with existing or near-future experimental apparatus; the framework's case is that the predictions are worth testing even if their derivation depends on a speculative bridge between abstract algebra and concrete physics.

The reader who finds the program implausible should set it aside; the framework's central argument does not depend on it. The reader who finds it intriguing is referred to the URBs, to the framework's Zenodo deposits, and to the technical sequel that takes the o(8) program as its principal subject.

### 11.7 The Author's Posture Toward Speculation

A note on the author's posture toward the speculative material in this chapter is owed to the reader. The framework's settled core — the five-value architecture, GILE, MR, HEAR, PD — is offered as the framework's working contribution to the contemporary discussion of practical reasoning under indeterminacy, and the author's confidence in the core is high. The technical reach into mathematics, physics, and consciousness research is offered as the framework's *invitation to further work*, and the author's confidence in the reach is correspondingly lower.

The lower confidence is not equivocation. It reflects the author's recognition that speculative bridges between distinct technical literatures — a logical framework on one side, the Riemann hypothesis or the structure of antimatter or the hard problem of consciousness on the other — are easy to construct and hard to validate. The author has chosen to include the bridges in the present book, against the advice of some readers who would have preferred a more conservative volume, because the bridges are part of the framework's intellectual character and because the alternative — a sanitized presentation that treats the framework's settled core as standalone — would have given a misleading account of how the framework was built and what its program looks like in active development.

The reader is invited to engage with the speculative material critically, to set aside the bridges that fail to compel, and to examine more closely the bridges that do. The framework's value to the reader does not depend on the reader's adopting any particular speculative claim; it depends on the reader's finding the framework's settled core useful in the practical work of decision under indeterminacy. The speculative material is offered in the hope that some part of it will, in the long run, prove worth the further work; not in the conviction that any particular part will.

## Chapter 12: A Practice You Can Start Tomorrow

### 12.1 The Argument for a Practice

A framework that is read and not practiced is, in the end, a framework that has not been understood. The architecture of Chapters 1 through 9 is sufficiently abstract that a reader can finish the book with a clear intellectual grasp of its claims and no operational sense of what to do with them. The cases of Chapter 10 close some of the gap; the present chapter closes the rest, by offering a 30-day starter protocol that the reader can begin on the morning following the chapter's completion.

The protocol is a starter, not a program. A more complete practice apparatus — biometric integration, group formats, longitudinal tracking, professional support — is the subject of the framework's sixth book in the planned series, the *Mood Amplifier* practice book. The present chapter offers the irreducible minimum: what the reader needs to do, what equipment is required (almost none), and what to expect from the first month.

### 12.2 The Three-Tier Cadence

The practice is organized in three tiers: a *daily* practice (5–10 minutes), a *weekly* practice (30–45 minutes), and a *monthly* practice (60–90 minutes). The three tiers do different work; all three are essential.

The daily practice is the GILE self-assessment. The agent records, at a consistent time of day (the framework recommends evening, before sleep), a four-dimensional reading of the day's overall state: G, I, L, E, each in [0, 1], with one-line notes on the reasoning behind each. The composite is computed and recorded. Total time: five minutes after the first week of habituation.

The weekly practice is a single Myrion Resolution cycle on whatever proposition the agent has identified as most consequential and most Tralse during the week. The cycle follows the six steps of §7.2; the duration is determined by the agent's deliberative pace but is typically 30–45 minutes for the explicit steps, with the interval between steps lasting through the following week.

The monthly practice is the HEAR computation and trend examination. The agent computes the month's HEAR from the daily GILE composites and the week's MR records, examines the trajectory over the past several months (or, for new practitioners, the first month relative to baseline), and identifies any structural changes the trajectory suggests. The framework recommends accompanying the monthly practice with a short written reflection (one to two pages) on the month's principal observations.

### 12.4 What to Expect

The framework's pilot data and the experience of early practitioners (URBs #757, #765, #766, #775, #777) suggest the following pattern over the first month.

Days 1–7 are characterized by *over-assessment*: agents new to the practice tend to score the dimensions with more variance than is accurate, often producing day-to-day swings that reflect the novelty of the introspective activity rather than the underlying state. This is normal and self-correcting; by the end of the first week, the daily assessments stabilize.

Days 7–14 introduce the first MR cycle, which most agents experience as awkward — the discipline of distinguishing the explicit GILE computation from the pre-reflective check is unfamiliar, and the temptation to collapse the two is strong. The framework recommends doing the first cycle on a deliberately low-consequence proposition (where to take a vacation, whether to begin a particular book, whether to attend a particular event) so the procedure can be learned without the pressure of consequence.

Days 14–21 introduce the second cycle, on a proposition of moderate consequence. The pre-reflective check, which felt awkward in the first cycle, typically becomes more accessible in the second. Agents often report at this stage their first experience of the framework's principal value: the recognition that a proposition they have been mistreating as True/False (forcing it to one or the other against the structure of the situation) is, in fact, Tralse, and that the recognition itself produces a felt sense of relief.

Days 21–30 stabilize the practice. The daily GILE becomes routine; the weekly MR becomes the practice's substantive core; the monthly HEAR closes the loop and produces, for most agents, the first integrated picture of where they are and where the trajectory is heading.

### 12.5 Common Difficulties and Their Remedies

**The agent cannot sustain the daily practice.** This is the most common difficulty. The remedy is to reduce the daily practice to its minimum form — a single composite GILE score with no per-dimension breakdown — until the habit is established, then re-introduce the per-dimension assessment.

**The agent's MR cycles do not converge.** This is sometimes a sign of premature convergence-seeking; more often it is a sign that the proposition under MR is genuinely stably Tralse, in which case the agent should follow the procedure of §7.6 (permissible action set) rather than continuing to cycle. The agent should also examine whether the proposition has been correctly identified — agents new to the practice frequently choose propositions that are I-state or Double Tralse rather than Tralse, and the framework's procedures for those values are different.

**The agent's HEAR is declining.** A declining HEAR over the first month is not, by itself, evidence of a problem; the act of beginning the practice often surfaces difficulties that were previously unrecognized, and the surfacing is itself valuable. A declining HEAR over three or more months, especially if accompanied by declining GILE_E and biometric anchors, is a more substantive signal and warrants attention. The framework's recommendation is the use of external counsel (a therapist, a trusted friend, a coach) rather than self-correction within the practice alone.

**The agent's pre-reflective check and explicit GILE consistently disagree.** This is often informative. The agent should examine whether the explicit GILE assessments are being conducted under conditions of adequate honesty, whether the pre-reflective check is being recorded before rather than after the explicit computation, and whether the disagreement is concentrated in a particular dimension (which is itself a useful diagnostic). Persistent disagreement that does not yield to examination is, again, an occasion for external counsel.

### 12.6 What the Practice Is, and What It Is Not

The practice is not therapy. It is not meditation. It is not religious observance. It is an apparatus for *deciding well under indeterminacy* — for moving through life with a vocabulary precise enough to distinguish what is settled from what is not, what is forbidden from what is undetermined, what is to be resolved from what is to be lived with. The practice supports the work of being an adult human in the world; it does not substitute for any of the other instruments — clinical, contemplative, relational, professional — that the work also requires.

The framework's hope for the reader is the hope it has held for itself across the years of its development: that the additional vocabulary, used with care and patience over time, makes the difficult things easier without making them simpler, and the indeterminate things bearable without forcing them into a determinacy they do not possess. If the reader puts down this book and never opens it again, having taken from it nothing but the recognition that some of the most important propositions in their life are Tralse rather than True or False, the book will have done useful work. If the reader takes up the practice and sustains it over months and years, the book will have done a great deal more.

### 12.7 Adapting the Practice for Different Life-Stages

The starter protocol of §12.3 is calibrated for the agent who has time, stability, and inclination to begin a new contemplative discipline at a moderate level of investment. Many readers will not find themselves in those circumstances, and the framework offers four standard adaptations of the protocol to the life-stages in which most readers actually find themselves.

The *new-parent adaptation* compresses the daily practice to a two-minute composite GILE score with no per-dimension breakdown, defers the weekly MR cycle to the periods (typically the first thirty to sixty days after the child's arrival have passed) in which the agent has reclaimed enough cognitive bandwidth for explicit deliberation, and replaces the monthly HEAR computation with a quarterly check at twelve weeks, six months, and one year. The protocol acknowledges the practical reality that early-parenthood agents do not have the discretionary time the standard protocol assumes and that the cost of forcing the standard protocol on them — frustration, abandonment, retrospective shame — outweighs the benefit of the additional formality. The compressed version, conducted reliably over the first eighteen months of parenthood, produces, in the framework's pilot data summarized in URB #765, comparable benefits to the standard protocol conducted irregularly.

The *high-demand-vocational adaptation* — for agents whose work is unusually consuming of cognitive bandwidth (early-stage company founders, surgical residents, military personnel in deployment, members of the legal profession in the run-up to trial) — also compresses the daily practice but preserves the weekly MR cycle, which the framework holds to be the irreducible practical core. The compression of the daily practice is to a single sentence written before sleep: "Today's overall GILE was approximately ___, principally because ___." The single-sentence form preserves the reflective discipline without imposing the time cost of the four-dimensional form.

The *acute-distress adaptation* — for agents whose circumstances are characterized by ongoing crisis (a serious illness, a recent bereavement, a sudden and consequential life-disruption) — modifies the practice in two ways. The daily GILE is conducted but with explicit recognition that all four dimensions are operating under abnormal conditions and the scores will not be meaningfully comparable across the period of crisis. The weekly MR is suspended in favor of the simpler practice of *pre-reflective check alone*: the agent records, weekly, their pre-reflective response to the question *am I, on the whole, oriented in a direction I can sustain?* with notes on any specific concerns the response surfaces. The full MR practice resumes when the acute period has passed.

The *late-life adaptation* — for agents in the seventh decade and beyond, whose practical situation differs from the framework's design baseline in ways the standard protocol does not adequately address — modifies the weights of the daily GILE composite to give greater weight to L (which becomes, in the framework's pilot data, increasingly determinative of the agent's experienced quality of life as the years accumulate) and to E (which similarly increases in determinative weight as the agent's environmental dependencies become more salient). The default weights of §6.6 are revised, for the late-life adaptation, to w_G = 0.25, w_I = 0.20, w_L = 0.30, w_E = 0.25. The MR cycle's content shifts toward propositions concerning legacy, relational repair, and the integration of the life as lived; the framework offers no specific protocol for these propositions but notes that they are the propositions on which the late-life adaptation typically concentrates.

### 12.8 When to Pause the Practice

A final note, of importance to the agent who intends to sustain the practice across years: the framework explicitly endorses the *deliberate pause*, of two to six weeks' duration, as a regular feature of the long-term practice. The pause is not the failure to maintain the practice; it is a recognized feature of the practice itself.

Three signals indicate that a pause is warranted. The first is the practice's having become *routine without engagement* — the agent is performing the daily GILE assessment as a checklist item rather than as a reflective act, and the assessments have lost the quality of honest examination. The second is the practice's *interfering with rather than supporting* a particular life-event — a sustained creative project, a period of intensive professional demand, a relational transition in which the deliberative apparatus is generating more anxiety than it is resolving. The third is the practice's *coinciding with declining HEAR* over a period of two months or more without any other identifiable cause; in this case the practice itself may be the source of the decline, and a pause is the appropriate diagnostic intervention.

The pause is taken simply: the agent stops the daily GILE recording, suspends the weekly MR cycles, and refrains from the monthly HEAR computation. The agent does not abandon the framework's vocabulary — the recognition of Tralse propositions, the awareness of the GILE dimensions, the disciplined refusal to force decisions into bivalence remain available — but the formal practice ceases. The pause's end is determined by the agent's sense, on returning to consider it, that resumption of the practice would now be a positive contribution rather than a return to a discipline whose value was unclear. The framework's experience, recorded in URBs #766 and #775, is that pauses of this kind followed by spontaneous resumption are characteristic of the most successful long-term practitioners: the practice is not a continuous discipline but an *intermittent resource* that the practitioner returns to as the practitioner judges the return useful.

The author wishes the reader well.

## Appendix A: Glossary of TI Sigma Terms

**Bivalence.** The principle that every well-formed proposition is either true or false. Foundational to classical logic; questioned by every system in the many-valued tradition (Chapter 2).

**Double Tralse (DT).** The fifth truth value: the logical status of a proposition that occupies a forbidden zone in which no stable classical assignment is consistent. Examples include the Liar sentence, vow conflicts, and predictive feedback structures (Chapter 5).

**E (Environment).** The fourth GILE dimension: the supportiveness of an agent's physical, social, and informational surroundings (§6.5).

**G (Goodness).** The first GILE dimension: the alignment of a state of affairs with an agent's considered values (§6.2).

**GILE.** The four-dimensional evaluative compass: Goodness, Intuition, Love, Environment (Chapter 6).

**HEAR (Holistic Existence Amplification Reading).** The framework's longitudinal aggregate measure, composed of the time-weighted average GILE, MR convergence behavior, contextual variables, and (when available) a biometric anchor (Chapter 8).

**I (Intuition).** The second GILE dimension: the agent's pre-reflective evaluative response (§6.3).

**I-state.** The fourth truth value: the logical status of a proposition whose truth-conditions are not yet specifiable, awaiting constructive work (Chapter 4).

**L (Love).** The third GILE dimension: the quality of connection in a state of affairs (§6.4).

**Myrion Resolution (MR).** The framework's iterative convergence procedure for moving propositions from indeterminate to determinate values, where movement is possible (Chapter 7).

**Permissibility Distribution (PD).** The framework's generalization of Bayesian probability theory, accommodating the I-state, Tralse, and Double Tralse logical categories (Chapter 9).

**Tralse.** The third truth value: the logical status of a proposition that is meaningfully indeterminate, with defined truth-conditions but no procedure of resolution available to the agent (Chapter 3).

**Tralse Informationalism (TI Sigma).** The framework presented in this book, comprising the five-value truth architecture, the GILE compass, MR, HEAR, and PD.

## Appendix B: The Five-Value Truth Tables

The five values are denoted T (True), F (False), τ (Tralse), ι (I-state), and δ (Double Tralse). The tables below give the framework's standard semantics for negation, conjunction, and disjunction. Implication and equivalence are derived from these in the standard manner; the derivations are given in the philosophical monograph that accompanies this book.

**Negation (¬p)**

| p | ¬p |
|---|----|
| T | F |
| F | T |
| τ | τ |
| ι | ι |
| δ | δ |

(The Tralse, I-state, and Double Tralse values are *self-negating*: their negation is themselves. This is one of the features that distinguishes the framework most sharply from classical and standard many-valued logics.)

**Conjunction (p ∧ q)** — read across, then down

|  | T | F | τ | ι | δ |
|--|---|---|---|---|---|
| **T** | T | F | τ | ι | δ |
| **F** | F | F | F | F | F |
| **τ** | τ | F | τ | ι | δ |
| **ι** | ι | F | ι | ι | δ |
| **δ** | δ | F | δ | δ | δ |

**Disjunction (p ∨ q)** — read across, then down

|  | T | F | τ | ι | δ |
|--|---|---|---|---|---|
| **T** | T | T | T | T | T |
| **F** | T | F | τ | ι | δ |
| **τ** | T | τ | τ | ι | δ |
| **ι** | T | ι | ι | ι | δ |
| **δ** | T | δ | δ | δ | δ |

The reader will verify by inspection that the tables reduce to the classical tables on the bivalent fragment (rows and columns T and F); that the Tralse value propagates through operations whose inputs include it but does not infect operations whose outputs are determined by other inputs; that the I-state similarly propagates but is more "infectious" than Tralse; and that Double Tralse occupies the most absorbing position, propagating through any operation that does not have a False conjunct or a True disjunct to short-circuit it. The asymmetry between the I-state and Double Tralse rows reflects the framework's structural distinction between *indeterminacy of specification* (I-state) and *forbidden inconsistency* (Double Tralse).
