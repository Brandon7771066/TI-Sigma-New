Ever since I was 10 years old, I felt called to a special mission to bring harmony to the world. It started as a desire to connect everyone around the globe through the internet so they could be friends and have pleasant in-person occasions. I knew that I was destined to make a significant impact on the world, but I have always been extremely curious and introspective as well. I became fascinated by philosophy and cognitive science when I was 12 and endeavored to be a scientist. Thus, I studied Cognitive Science and Business Administration and graduated from UConn. As the only undergraduate speaker of the year 2019 featured at UConn's TEDx chapter, I presented "How The Science of Curiosity Can Crush Your Comfort Zone," which was published on YouTube in 2020. My special interests in the Free Energy Principle, Self-Determination Theory, philosophy, and psychology perfectly prepared me for the kind of deep interdisciplinary work I do today across AI, formal reasoning, and healthcare innovation.

During my college years, I took a tremendous interest in mental health — partly out of fascination with the mind, and partly because of my own mental health struggles. It was quite plain to me that modern medicine, philosophy, religion, and culture have failed to address what I consider the central problem of our species: suffering. Chronic pain alone affects up to half of the world's population. Anxiety and depression are rampant at a time when there should be tremendous prosperity and happiness. When I was 19, I learned about a Scottish woman named Jo Cameron who never experienced anxiety, sadness, or pain across her entire 77-78 year life. I knew immediately that her special mutations on the understudied FAAH and FAAH-OUT genes were key to dissolving the omnipresent suffering epidemic. I had no degree at the time and no rationale to start a company just yet — but the seed had been planted.

I started my career in solar at age 20 to make an impact on the world while preparing my mind for what was to come. My overall conclusion from being a solar marketer, salesman, scientist, and philosopher is that humans are tremendously irrational — even on basic, common-sense issues like solar energy. Skepticism toward climate change and imaginarily high costs abounded. I was amongst the top salespeople at arranging meetings and pitching people in stores, yet sales were arduous. By 24, I firmly decided to take matters into my own hands and found a company explicitly centered on eliminating suffering and promoting wellbeing. My original plan was to get my Doctorate first for the credentials. However, I opted to move forward with just my Bachelor's and hire 7 PhD scientists through Kolabtree. I realized that assembling the right talent and actually getting started was better than waiting up to a decade. Thus, BlissGene Therapeutics was born — grounded in my utilitarian convictions that we ought to promote wellbeing and eliminate suffering over the long term as much as possible. Our work focuses on translating the Jo Cameron mutations into a gene therapy that can dramatically reduce chronic pain, anxiety, and depression in the general population.

In June 2025, while still developing BlissGene and securing funding, I launched my signature field of philosophy: Tralse Informationalism / Transcendent Intelligence Sigma (TIΣ). TIΣ refutes the notion that there are only two truth states. The idea that everything can be captured by a 0 or a 1 is utterly ridiculous, even though modern computing, math, and science are built on it. Truth is not bivalent; it is a rich, multidimensional spectrum I call Tralse. Tralse Informationalism, which I, Brandon Charles Emerick, invented, formally identifies five truth values — True, False, Indeterminate, Meta-Indeterminate, and No Answer — along with a family of Meta-Truths such as "Moot," which are an agent's reactions to any of the four base values.

I have empirical proof of this. Using Fleiss Kappa — the gold-standard interrater-reliability metric — I have demonstrated that my five-tiered system achieves k ≈ 0.9 in AI classification, versus 0.4-0.6 for binary. In interrater reliability, 0.8 is considered exemplary; 0.9 is phenomenal, dwarfing measures used for diagnosing severe psychiatric conditions. The most common misconception about AI in healthcare and scientific research is that current AI is "intelligent enough" to evaluate claims reliably. The truth is that with today's architecture, AI cannot even grade most statements as either true or false to begin with — because truth itself is a spectrum, which binary cannot accommodate. Responsible innovation in AI begins with admitting this.

My methodology insists on machine-checked proofs, pre-registered hypotheses, and competent-rater empirical validation rather than glossy claims. Every major TIΣ conjecture is pre-registered with falsification criteria *before* I run it, and I publish my errors as loudly as my successes. In an AI landscape rife with overclaiming and hype, that kind of accountability is not optional; it is the price of admission for anyone serious about truth.
