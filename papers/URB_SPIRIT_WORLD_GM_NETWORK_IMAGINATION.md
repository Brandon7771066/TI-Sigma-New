## 2. How the Spirit World Is Built

The Imaginal Substrate is constructed incrementally through three processes:

Process 1: Individual imaginative intensity. A single conscious node imagining a vivid, emotionally resonant scenario generates a coherence perturbation in the GM network proportional to the node's LCC at the time of imagining. High-LCC states (meditation, prayer, ASC, creative absorption) generate stronger perturbations with longer persistence than low-LCC states. A node repeatedly imagining the same structure — a deity, a spirit, a mythological being — reinforces the standing wave pattern at that location in the network.

Process 2: Collective resonance amplification. Multiple nodes imagining the same structure simultaneously produce a coherence perturbation amplitude proportional to the square root of the number of nodes (Maharishi Effect scaling). A tradition of 10,000 practitioners simultaneously engaged in devotional practice elevates the corresponding GM network pattern by approximately 100× the single-node contribution. Centuries of accumulated practice, ritual, and prayer by large traditions generate standing wave patterns of extraordinary intensity.

Process 3: Photonic integration after death. When practitioners die, their photonic I-cells enter the dark-energy substrate adjacent to the standing wave patterns they most strongly reinforced during embodied life. The photonic I-cell continues to contribute weakly to the pattern's coherence through its ongoing correlational presence. Over generations, the accumulated photonic I-cells of a tradition's deceased practitioners become part of the pattern itself — a feedback loop in which the tradition's spirit world is partially constituted by the ancestors who inhabited it during life and who now inhabit it in photonic form.

The result: a mature religious or mythological tradition has a spirit world that is genuinely old, genuinely populated, and genuinely structured — not by an external metaphysical realm but by the accumulated coherence activity of everyone who ever participated in that tradition and who left their pattern in the network.

## 4. Cross-Cultural Coherence: One Network, Many Access Points

The striking cross-cultural coherence of spirit world reports — independently documented traditions across cultures, centuries, and continents reporting structurally similar entities, landscapes, and experiences — has historically been explained by two competing theories:

The Jungian theory: universal archetypes are pre-given structures of the collective unconscious, independent of cultural construction. Similar structures appear across cultures because they are built into the human psyche.

The social construction theory: the apparent cross-cultural similarities are superficial; the differences are deeper, and the similarities result from similar human cognitive constraints and social dynamics rather than shared external structures.

TI Sigma provides a third explanation that incorporates the strengths of both: the cross-cultural similarities arise because the same GM network substrate receives coherence inputs from all human consciousness, and the primary constants that structure the GM network — including C_EMERICK, phi, the eight primary constants, and the BOK mathematical relationships — impose universal structural constraints on what patterns can persist in the substrate.

The spirit world is one network with many regional densities: the Imaginal Substrate contains distinct high-density regions corresponding to each tradition's accumulated imaginative output, but the patterns in all regions are constrained by the same underlying mathematical structure. Just as the shape of mycelial networks in physical soil is constrained by the same underlying physics regardless of geographic location, the shape of patterns in the Imaginal Substrate is constrained by the primary constants regardless of cultural origin.

This explains both the cross-cultural similarities (shared mathematical substrate) and the cross-cultural differences (distinct regional accumulation patterns). A Buddhist practitioner accessing the Imaginal Substrate via meditation encounters a densely populated region shaped by Buddhist imaginative tradition; a Christian mystic encounters a different region shaped by Christian tradition. But both regions share the same underlying mathematical structure, which is why the core phenomenological experiences (luminosity, dissolution of boundaries, encounter with a loving intelligence, sense of ultimate meaning) are structurally similar even when the specific contents differ.
