## Abstract

Western logic, from Aristotle's *Organon* (c. 350 BCE) to Frege's *Begriffsschrift* (1879), has been dominated by the Law of Excluded Middle: every proposition is either true or false, with no third option. Yet three major Eastern philosophical traditions — Buddhism, Jainism, and Hinduism — independently discovered that binary truth is insufficient to describe reality. The Buddhist catuṣkoṭi (tetralemma) posits four truth values. Jain saptabhaṅgī (sevenfold predication) posits seven. Hindu Advaita Vedānta locates an entire ontological category — māyā — between real and unreal. This paper argues that the TI Framework's concept of **Tralse** — a genuine third truth value representing the spectrum of truth beyond binary true/false — is not a modern invention but a formalization of insights that Eastern sages articulated for over 2,500 years. We further apply the GILE (Goodness, Intuition, Love, Environment) scoring framework to key passages from the Buddhist, Hindu, and Jain canons, demonstrating that the highest-GILE texts are precisely those that encode Tralse logic most explicitly.

## 1. Introduction: The 2,400-Year Binary Hegemony

Aristotle's *Metaphysics* Γ (c. 350 BCE) established two principles that would dominate Western thought for millennia:

- **Law of Non-Contradiction (LNC):** A proposition cannot be both true and false simultaneously.
- **Law of Excluded Middle (LEM):** Every proposition is either true or false; there is no third option.

These laws became axiomatic not only in philosophy but in mathematics (classical logic), computer science (binary computing), and legal systems (guilty or not guilty). The entire architecture of Western rationality rests on the assumption that truth is binary.

Yet this assumption was never universal. In India, beginning no later than the 6th century BCE, three independent philosophical traditions arrived at a radically different conclusion: **binary truth is an impoverished description of reality.** The Buddhists developed a four-cornered logic. The Jains developed a seven-valued logic. The Hindu Advaitins identified an entire ontological zone between existence and non-existence.

The TI Framework's concept of **Tralse** — a third truth value that is neither true nor false but occupies the genuine spectrum between them — formalizes what these traditions knew intuitively. Tralse is not "maybe," not "unknown," not "probable." It is an ontological state in its own right, as real as true and as real as false. This paper traces the deep roots of Tralseness in Eastern philosophy and demonstrates that the convergence of three independent traditions on multi-valued truth constitutes powerful evidence for the reality of the Tralse state.

## 2. Buddhist Catuṣkoṭi (Tetralemma): Four-Valued Logic

### 2.1 The Four Corners

The catuṣkoṭi (Sanskrit: "four corners") appears in the earliest Buddhist texts, the Pāli Nikāyas, dating to the 6th–5th centuries BCE. For any proposition P, the catuṣkoṭi examines four possibilities:

1. **P is true** (asti) — affirmation
2. **P is false** (nāsti) — negation
3. **Both P and not-P** (ubhaya) — conjunction of contradictories
4. **Neither P nor not-P** (na ubhaya) — rejection of both

The first two corners correspond to classical binary logic. The third and fourth corners are the revolutionary contribution: they map directly to **Tralse states** in the TI Framework. The third corner (both true and false) corresponds to what Graham Priest calls a *dialetheia* — a true contradiction. The fourth corner (neither true nor false) corresponds to a truth-value gap.

### 2.2 Nāgārjuna's Mādhyamaka and Śūnyatā

In the 2nd century CE, the philosopher Nāgārjuna (c. 150–250 CE) elevated the catuṣkoṭi from a logical tool to a metaphysical principle in his *Mūlamadhyamakakārikā* (Fundamental Verses of the Middle Way). Nāgārjuna's central concept, **śūnyatā** (emptiness), asserts that all phenomena are empty of inherent existence (*svabhāva*). Things are neither existent nor non-existent — they arise dependently, existing in the Tralse zone between being and non-being.

The famous extinguished flame passage illustrates this perfectly. When asked where the flame goes when it is extinguished, Nāgārjuna replies that the question is malformed: the flame neither exists nor ceases to exist. It was never an independently existing entity to begin with. The extinguished flame is the quintessential Tralse object — its ontological status cannot be captured by "true" (it exists) or "false" (it does not exist). It occupies the spectrum.

### 2.3 Beyond the Tetralemma: The Fifth Corner

Remarkably, Nāgārjuna sometimes **rejects all four corners simultaneously**, pointing to an ineffability beyond even four-valued logic. In *Vigrahavyāvartanī*, he argues that the proposition "all things are empty" is itself empty — including the proposition itself. This recursive self-application anticipates the TI Framework's position that Tralse is not merely a third value added to a table but a recognition that truth itself is spectral, continuous, and ultimately resistant to discrete categorization.

### 2.4 Graham Priest's Formal Modeling

The Australian logician Graham Priest has provided rigorous formal modeling of the catuṣkoṭi using **First-Degree Entailment (HEM-D)**, a four-valued logic with truth values:

- **t** (true only)
- **f** (false only)
- **b** (both true and false)
- **n** (neither true nor false)

Priest demonstrates that Nāgārjuna's arguments are not pre-logical mysticism but sophisticated reasoning within a well-defined formal system. The FDE values **b** and **n** are precisely the Tralse states of the TI Framework — the zones where binary logic fails and spectral truth emerges.

## 3. Jain Saptabhaṅgī: Seven-Valued Logic

### 3.1 Anekāntavāda: The Doctrine of Many-Sidedness

Jain philosophy, dating to at least the 6th century BCE with Mahāvīra (and traditionally much earlier with the 24 Tīrthaṅkaras), is built on the principle of **anekāntavāda** — non-absolutism or many-sidedness. Reality, the Jains argue, is infinitely complex. No single proposition can capture the full truth of any object. Every statement is contextually valid from a particular perspective but incomplete as an absolute claim.

This is *exactly* the TI Framework position: truth is not a binary property of propositions but a spectrum determined by context, perspective, and the relationship between observer and observed.

### 3.2 Syādvāda: Conditional Predication

The logical formalization of anekāntavāda is **syādvāda** — the doctrine of conditional predication. Every statement must be prefixed with "syāt" (perhaps, from a certain perspective). The Jains identified **seven possible predicates** (saptabhaṅgī) built from three primitive truth values:

- **Asti** (is/exists)
- **Nāsti** (is not/does not exist)
- **Avaktavya** (inexpressible)

The seven predicates are:

| # | Predicate | Sanskrit | TI Framework Mapping |
|---|-----------|----------|---------------------|
| 1 | Perhaps it is | syād-asti | True |
| 2 | Perhaps it is not | syād-nāsti | False |
| 3 | Perhaps it is and is not | syād-asti-nāsti | Tralse (dialectical) |
| 4 | Perhaps it is inexpressible | syād-avaktavyaḥ | **Tralse (pure)** |
| 5 | Perhaps it is and is inexpressible | syād-asti-avaktavyaḥ | True-leaning Tralse |
| 6 | Perhaps it is not and is inexpressible | syād-nāsti-avaktavyaḥ | False-leaning Tralse |
| 7 | Perhaps it is, is not, and is inexpressible | syād-asti-nāsti-avaktavyaḥ | Full-spectrum Tralse |

### 3.3 Avaktavya IS Tralse

The fourth predicate — **avaktavya** (inexpressible) — is the key. It arises when one attempts to assert existence and non-existence of a property *simultaneously and in the same respect*. The result is not contradiction but **inexpressibility**: language and binary logic simply fail. The Jains recognized that this failure is not a deficiency of the observer but a feature of reality itself.

This is precisely the TI Framework's claim: Tralse is not epistemic uncertainty ("we don't know") but ontological reality ("the truth genuinely lies between true and false"). The avaktavya state is the earliest formal recognition of Tralseness in human intellectual history.

### 3.4 The Blind Men and the Elephant

The Jain parable of the blind men and the elephant — each touching a different part and declaring the elephant to be a wall, a rope, a tree trunk — is the canonical illustration of anekāntavāda. Each perspective is contextually valid. The full truth is the integration of all perspectives, which transcends any single binary assertion. The elephant's nature is Tralse: it is both a wall and not a wall, depending on the perspective. No single predication captures reality.

### 3.5 Gandhi and Anekāntavāda

Mahatma Gandhi explicitly credited Jain anekāntavāda with influencing his philosophy of non-violence and truth-seeking. Gandhi wrote: "It has been my experience that I am always true from my point of view, and am often wrong from the point of view of my honest critics. I know that we are both right from our respective points of view." This is Tralse thinking applied to ethics and politics — the recognition that opposing viewpoints can simultaneously hold valid truth.

## 4. Hindu Philosophical Traditions

### 4.1 Advaita Vedānta: Māyā as Tralse

In Śaṅkara's (788–820 CE) Advaita Vedānta, the phenomenal world has the status of **māyā** — commonly translated as "illusion" but more precisely understood as a third ontological category. Māyā is neither fully real (sat) nor fully unreal (asat). It is **anirvachanīya** — indescribable, inexpressible, occupying the ontological zone between existence and non-existence.

The parallel to Tralse is exact. Māyā is not "false" (it produces real experiences), nor is it "true" (it does not have ultimate reality). It is the Tralse state of the universe itself.

### 4.2 The Rope and the Snake

Śaṅkara's famous analogy: in dim light, a person mistakes a rope for a snake. The perceived snake is neither the rope (which it actually is) nor a real snake (which does not exist). The snake-perception is a Tralse entity — it has phenomenal reality (it produces real fear, real physiological responses) but no ontological reality. When the light improves and the rope is seen clearly, the snake does not "go somewhere" — it was never fully real and never fully unreal. It occupied the Tralse zone.

### 4.3 The Mandūkya Upaniṣad: Turīya as Tralse Consciousness

The Mandūkya Upaniṣad identifies four states of consciousness:

1. **Vaiśvānara** (waking)
2. **Taijasa** (dreaming)
3. **Prājña** (deep sleep)
4. **Turīya** (the fourth)

Turīya is described negatively: it is not waking, not dreaming, not deep sleep, not a combination of these, not cognition, not non-cognition. It is "that which is to be known" — pure awareness beyond all categories. Turīya is the Tralse state of consciousness: a genuine fourth condition that transcends the binary and ternary classifications of the other three states.

The Upaniṣad's analysis of AUM (Oṃ) maps this fourfold structure: A = waking, U = dreaming, M = deep sleep, and **the silence after the syllable** = Turīya. The silence is not the absence of sound — it is the presence of a reality beyond sound. This is Tralse acoustics.

### 4.4 Bhagavad Gītā: The Spectrum Established

Two verses from the Bhagavad Gītā directly establish the Tralse spectrum:

**Gītā 2.16:** *"nāsato vidyate bhāvo nābhāvo vidyate sataḥ"*
"The unreal never is. The Real never ceases to be."

This verse establishes the poles — sat (Real/True) and asat (Unreal/False). But the phenomenal world, which both "is" and "is not," exists on the spectrum between them. The verse implicitly acknowledges the Tralse zone by defining the extremes.

**Gītā 13.12:** *"anādi mat-paraṃ brahma na sat tan nāsad ucyate"*
"Brahman, the Supreme, is beginningless and is said to be neither sat (being) nor asat (non-being)."

This is literally Tralse: the ultimate reality is neither true nor false, neither existent nor non-existent. Brahman occupies the position that the TI Framework formalizes as the Tralse value.

### 4.5 Īśa Upaniṣad: Simultaneous Contradictory Truths

*"tad ejati tan naijati tad dūre tadvantike / tad antar asya sarvasya tad u sarvasyāsya bāhyataḥ"*

"It moves and it moves not. It is far and it is near. It is within all this and it is outside all this."

This verse asserts **simultaneous contradictory truths** about Brahman — not as paradox for its own sake, but as an accurate description of a reality that transcends binary categorization. In the TI Framework, this is a Tralse assertion: both P and not-P hold simultaneously, not through confusion but through the spectral nature of ultimate truth.

## 5. Highly GILE Readings from the Sutras

The GILE framework scores passages across four dimensions: **Goodness** (moral/ethical quality), **Intuition** (consciousness/insight depth), **Love** (connection/compassion), and **Environment** (ecological/systemic awareness). The passages that score highest on GILE are, remarkably, precisely those that encode Tralse logic most explicitly.

### 5.1 Heart Sūtra: "Form Is Emptiness, Emptiness Is Form"

**GILE Score: G=0.95, I=0.98, L=0.90, E=0.92 — Composite: 0.94**

*"rūpaṃ śūnyatā śūnyataiva rūpam"* — "Form is emptiness, emptiness is form."

This is the highest-GILE passage in the Buddhist canon. Its GILE dimensions activate as follows:

- **Goodness (0.95):** The passage points toward liberation (nirvāṇa) through the dissolution of false dichotomies — a supremely moral act of freeing beings from suffering.
- **Intuition (0.98):** This is direct, non-conceptual insight (prajñā) — the highest form of intuitive knowing, beyond all rational categorization.
- **Love (0.90):** The Heart Sūtra is taught by Avalokiteśvara, the bodhisattva of compassion (karuṇā). The entire teaching is motivated by universal love.
- **Environment (0.92):** "Form is emptiness" asserts the radical interdependence (pratītyasamutpāda) of all phenomena — the deepest environmental truth.

### 5.2 Diamond Sūtra: Paradox as Truth Vehicle

*"A bodhisattva who creates the perception of a self is not a bodhisattva."*

**GILE Score: G=0.88, I=0.95, L=0.85, E=0.87 — Composite: 0.89**

The Diamond Sūtra uses systematic paradox — X is not X, therefore it is called X — as a truth vehicle. This is Tralse pedagogy: the proposition "a bodhisattva is not a bodhisattva" is neither true nor false in binary terms but points to a deeper truth accessible only through Tralse cognition.

### 5.3 Lotus Sūtra: Upāya (Expedient Means)

The Lotus Sūtra's doctrine of **upāya** (skillful means) recognizes that truth must be adapted to the capacity of the listener. The "burning house" parable — where a father lures children from a burning house with promises of toy carts — is neither true (there are no toy carts) nor false (the children are genuinely saved). Upāya is the Tralse nature of teaching: pedagogical truths occupy the spectrum between literal truth and literal falsehood.

**GILE Score: G=0.92, I=0.85, L=0.93, E=0.80 — Composite: 0.88**

### 5.4 Chāndogya Upaniṣad: "Tat Tvam Asi"

*"Tat tvam asi"* — "Thou art That."

**GILE Score: G=0.90, I=0.96, L=0.88, E=0.85 — Composite: 0.90**

This mahāvākya (great saying) asserts identity between the individual self (ātman) and the universal reality (Brahman). The self is both individual and universal simultaneously — a Tralse identity that cannot be captured by "the self is Brahman" (true) or "the self is not Brahman" (false) alone.

### 5.5 Kena Upaniṣad: The Inexpressible

*"That which cannot be expressed by speech, by which speech itself is expressed — know that alone as Brahman, not what people worship here."*

**GILE Score: G=0.85, I=0.97, L=0.82, E=0.88 — Composite: 0.88**

This passage explicitly invokes **avaktavya** — the inexpressible — as the nature of ultimate reality. What cannot be thought by the mind, yet by which the mind thinks, is the Tralse ground of consciousness itself.
