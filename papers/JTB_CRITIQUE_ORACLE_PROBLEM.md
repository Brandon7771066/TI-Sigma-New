# The Oracle Problem: A Systematic Critique of Justified True Belief and the Unargued Metaphysics of Epistemology

**Brandon Emerick**
**February 2026**

---

## Abstract

The Justified True Belief (JTB) framework has dominated Western epistemology since Plato's *Theaetetus*. Despite Gettier's famous counterexamples in 1963, philosophers continue patching JTB with increasingly elaborate conditions rather than questioning its foundations. This paper argues that JTB suffers from a fatal structural flaw: it demands simultaneous justification AND truth, which requires either trivial knowledge (tautologies), oracle access (impossible by assumption), or temporal collapse (incoherent). Furthermore, the assumption that precognition is impossible -- embedded in nearly all epistemological frameworks -- is itself an unargued metaphysical claim that constitutes what I call "metaphysical policing in a lab coat." The paper exposes this hidden assumption as the **Oracle Problem** and proposes replacing JTB with a temporal, process-oriented epistemology rooted in ternary (tralse) logic, where knowledge is not a static possession but a dynamic trajectory through epistemic space.

**Keywords:** Justified True Belief, Gettier Problem, Oracle Problem, precognition, temporal epistemology, tralse logic, epistemic integrity, metaphysical neutrality

## 1. JTB: "Just Total Bull****" (The Mom Test)

When I first explained the Justified True Belief framework to my mother -- that philosophers define knowledge as a belief that is both justified and true -- she paused for exactly one second and said: "That's Just Total Bull****."

Sometimes common sense cuts through philosophical fog with a precision that tenure cannot match.

The professional version of her critique, stripped of its refreshing profanity, is this: JTB conflates two fundamentally incompatible requirements and then pretends the conflation is a definition. It demands that an agent simultaneously satisfy a perspectival condition (justification) and an absolute condition (truth), as though these two requirements inhabit the same epistemic universe. They do not.

**Justification is perspectival.** It is evaluated from within the agent's epistemic situation. What counts as justified depends on what evidence is available, what cognitive resources the agent possesses, what inferential standards are operative in the context, and what background beliefs are in play. Justification is a property of the agent's relationship to her belief -- it is inherently first-personal, situated, and local.

**Truth is absolute.** It is evaluated from a god's-eye view that no finite agent occupies. Whether a proposition is true depends not on what anyone believes, perceives, or infers, but on how reality actually is -- the full, unabridged, mind-independent state of affairs. Truth is a property of the proposition's relationship to the world -- it is inherently third-personal, unsituated, and global.

JTB pretends these two conditions can be "cleanly glued together" into a single, coherent definition of knowledge. They cannot. The glue has never held. Gettier (1963) did not break JTB -- he merely exposed the category error that was hiding inside it from the beginning. Every Gettier case is a demonstration that justified belief and true belief can come apart precisely because they are answerable to different masters: one to the agent's epistemic position, the other to the structure of reality itself.

My mother saw this instantly. Professional epistemologists have spent sixty years not seeing it.

## 2. The Cake-and-Eat-It Problem

JTB wants epistemic hygiene AND metaphysical certainty simultaneously. It wants to eat the cake of justification and have the cake of truth, at the same time, on the same plate. But no finite agent has access to both at once.

**Justification is LOCAL.** It depends on available evidence, cognitive resources, inferential capacities, and context. An agent's justification for believing P is a function of her epistemic situation at time t -- what she has observed, what she can infer, what she has reason to trust. Justification is bounded by the agent's horizon.

**Truth is GLOBAL.** It depends on how reality actually is, which may be unknowable from any local perspective. Whether P is true is not a function of anyone's epistemic situation; it is a function of the total state of the world, including aspects of the world that may be permanently inaccessible to the agent.

Demanding both simultaneously is like demanding that a map BE the territory. A map is useful precisely because it is a local, compressed, perspectival representation of something too large and complex to apprehend directly. The value of a map is its fidelity to the territory, but fidelity is a matter of degree, and no map achieves identity with what it represents. JTB demands identity: the agent's epistemic map must perfectly coincide with the metaphysical territory, and the agent must know that it does. This is not a definition of knowledge. It is a definition of omniscience.

The "J" and the "T" in JTB pull in opposite directions:

- If you emphasize **J**, you get warranted belief that might be false. You get epistemic responsibility without metaphysical guarantee. You get the best an honest agent can do -- which, by JTB's own lights, might not be knowledge at all.

- If you emphasize **T**, you get truth that might be unjustified. You get metaphysical accuracy without epistemic warrant. You get lucky guesses, accidental correctness, and beliefs that happen to be true for reasons the agent does not and cannot grasp.

- JTB claims to **unite** them, but Gettier showed the union is unstable. The conditions can be independently satisfied while the conjunction fails to constitute knowledge. This is not an accident. It is a structural feature of any framework that tries to bridge an unbridgeable gap between the local and the global.

The Cake-and-Eat-It Problem is not a puzzle to be solved by adding more conditions. It is a symptom of a fundamental architectural flaw.

## 3. Why Patches Don't Work

For sixty years since Gettier's three-page demolition, epistemologists have proposed increasingly elaborate patches to save JTB. Each patch attempts to add a fourth condition -- JTB+X -- that will exclude Gettier cases while preserving the spirit of the original framework. Every one of them fails, and they all fail for the same reason: they try to bridge the local/global gap from within a snapshot framework that cannot accommodate temporal, process-oriented knowledge.

**The No-False-Lemma Condition (Clark, 1963).** Clark proposed that knowledge requires that the agent's justification not depend on any false lemma. The idea is elegant: Gettier cases involve reasoning through a false intermediate belief, so exclude those cases. But this condition fails because there are Gettier cases that involve no lemmas at all -- cases where the agent's belief is justified by direct perception that happens, by cosmic coincidence, to be veridical for the wrong reasons. If I see what looks exactly like a barn, and it is a barn, but I am in Fake Barn Country where most barn-shaped objects are facades, I have knowledge on the no-false-lemma view (no false lemma was involved) but I manifestly do not have knowledge. The patch is too narrow.

**Defeasibility Theory (Lehrer and Paxson, 1969).** Lehrer and Paxson proposed that knowledge is undefeated justified true belief: there must be no true proposition such that, if the agent were to learn it, her justification would be defeated. But this creates a regress of potential defeaters. For any true proposition D that might defeat my justification, there might be a further true proposition D' that would restore it, and a further D'' that would defeat it again. The theory requires surveying the entire space of true propositions and their defeater/restorer relationships -- which is precisely the kind of god's-eye access that finite agents lack. The cure presupposes what the disease makes impossible.

**The Causal Theory of Knowledge (Goldman, 1967).** Goldman proposed that knowledge requires an appropriate causal connection between the fact that makes P true and the agent's belief that P. This handles many Gettier cases elegantly but founders on mathematical and logical knowledge. What is the "appropriate causal connection" between the fact that 2+2=4 and my belief that 2+2=4? Mathematical truths do not cause anything in the physical sense. Nor do logical truths, modal truths, or moral truths (if there are any). The causal theory works for perceptual knowledge and fails for everything else.

**Relevant Alternatives (Dretske, 1970).** Dretske proposed that knowledge requires the ability to discriminate the actual state of affairs from relevant alternatives. You know that P if you can rule out the relevant scenarios in which P is false. But who decides what is "relevant"? The theory pushes the problem back one level: from "what is knowledge?" to "what is relevance?" -- and the latter question is at least as hard as the former. In practice, "relevant" tends to mean "whatever makes the theory give the right verdicts," which is circular.

**The Safety Condition (Sosa, 1999; Williamson, 2000).** Sosa and Williamson proposed that knowledge requires safety: in nearby possible worlds where the agent forms the belief in the same way, the belief is still true. But this requires modal access to nearby possible worlds -- which is precisely the kind of access that finite agents do not have. An agent cannot inspect the space of nearby possible worlds to verify that her belief-forming method is safe. She can only form beliefs in the actual world and hope. Safety is a condition that can be evaluated only from outside the agent's epistemic situation, which means it is a truth condition in disguise.

**The Anti-Luck Condition (Pritchard, 2005).** Pritchard proposed that knowledge must not be true by luck. But "luck" is notoriously difficult to define with the precision required for an epistemic condition. Is it luck if my belief-forming process is generally reliable but happens to produce a true belief in this case for atypical reasons? Is it luck if I form a true belief by a method that usually works but could easily have failed? The concept of epistemic luck is parasitic on our pre-theoretical intuitions about knowledge, which means it cannot serve as an independent condition that explains those intuitions.

**The Common Failure.** All of these patches share the same structural problem: they attempt to bridge the gap between local justification and global truth from within a framework that treats knowledge as a snapshot -- a static state of affairs that either obtains or does not at a single moment in time. None of them can accommodate the temporal, dynamic, process-oriented nature of how knowledge actually works. Knowledge is not a crystal that you either possess or lack at an instant. It is a trajectory through epistemic space, a process that unfolds over time, a relationship between agent and world that is constantly being renegotiated.

The patches fail because the framework is wrong. No amount of additional conditions can fix a definition built on a category error.

## 4. The Oracle Problem

Here is the argument that, to my knowledge, no epistemologist has made -- and it is the most damaging critique of JTB that can be constructed.

### The Central Argument

JTB implicitly assumes that agents cannot have direct epistemic access to future truths. This assumption is so deeply embedded in the framework that it is invisible -- like water to a fish, or gravity to a human who has never been in space. But once you see it, you cannot unsee it, and the entire JTB edifice becomes visibly contingent on an unargued metaphysical commitment.

The assumption works like this:

1. JTB requires that knowledge be **justified** -- that the agent have adequate evidential support for the belief at the time of believing.

2. The standard conception of "evidence" is backward-looking: evidence consists of observations already made, data already collected, experiences already had. Evidence flows from past to present.

3. This conception of evidence **rules out by fiat** any form of direct future-knowing: precognition, premonition, prophetic insight, or any epistemic process that gives an agent access to truths about events that have not yet occurred.

4. But the assumption that such access is impossible is **itself a metaphysical claim** -- and a strong one. It asserts something about the structure of time, the nature of causation, and the limits of consciousness.

5. This claim is **not justified by evidence**. It functions as a methodological default in mainstream analytic epistemology -- a working assumption that has hardened into apparent certainty through repetition rather than argument. How would you empirically prove that precognition is impossible? You would need to survey every possible epistemic process, in every possible mind, across every possible moment, and demonstrate that none of them ever produces veridical information about future states of affairs. This is not merely difficult; it is impossible in principle. The impossibility of precognition cannot be established by the very evidential methods that JTB privileges.

6. The claim is **not self-evident**. Across human history and across cultures, a wide variety of traditions have accepted forms of foreknowledge -- from the Oracle at Delphi to prophetic traditions in Judaism, Christianity, and Islam, to indigenous knowledge systems, to modern parapsychological research (however controversial). Whatever one thinks of these traditions, they demonstrate that the impossibility of precognition is not a universal intuition.

7. The claim is **smuggled into epistemology as a background assumption**, never argued for, never defended, never even acknowledged. It operates as invisible infrastructure, shaping what counts as "justification" and therefore what counts as "knowledge."

This is the **Oracle Problem**: the epistemological framework that demands justification for every knowledge claim exempts its own most consequential metaphysical assumption from justification.

### The Hypocrisy

The Oracle Problem exposes a deep hypocrisy at the heart of analytic epistemology:

**Philosophers say:** "You need justification for your knowledge claims. Every belief that aspires to the status of knowledge must be supported by adequate evidence, arrived at through reliable processes, and resistant to reasonable doubt."

**Also philosophers:** "Obviously, direct knowledge of the future is impossible. No one can know what will happen before it happens. This is just how reality works."

No justification offered. No evidence cited. No argument given. The impossibility of precognition is treated as a background assumption so obvious that it need not be stated, let alone defended.

This is what I call **"metaphysical policing in a lab coat."** Analytic epistemologists present themselves as neutral referees of justification -- disinterested umpires calling balls and strikes on knowledge claims. But they are not neutral. They are enforcing a particular metaphysical worldview while pretending to be mere methodologists. The lab coat of "conceptual analysis" conceals a metaphysical uniform.

The anti-precognition assumption does not travel alone. It smuggles in an entire suite of metaphysical commitments:

- **Anti-determinism** (or at least a specific view of it): If the future is not yet determined, then of course no one can know it. But the question of whether the future is determined is one of the deepest open questions in physics and philosophy of physics. To build it into one's epistemology as a background assumption is to beg one of the biggest questions in metaphysics.

- **Anti-block-universe**: The block universe view (eternalism), defended by serious physicists and philosophers from Minkowski to Putnam to Price, holds that past, present, and future are equally real. If the future is as real as the past, then there is no principled reason why epistemic access should be temporally asymmetric. JTB assumes it is.

- **A specific view of causation**: JTB assumes that epistemic causation flows only from past to present -- that the causal processes underwriting knowledge run in one temporal direction. But retrocausation has been seriously discussed in quantum mechanics (the transactional interpretation, the two-state vector formalism), and backward-in-time influences are at least consistent with the fundamental equations of physics, which are time-symmetric.

- **A specific view about the limits of consciousness**: JTB assumes that consciousness can access only present and past states of affairs. But this is an empirical claim about consciousness, and empirical claims about consciousness are notoriously underdetermined by current evidence. We do not understand consciousness well enough to declare with confidence what it can and cannot access.

Each of these is a substantive, debatable metaphysical position. None of them is self-evident. All of them are silently built into JTB's foundation.

## 5. What Precognition Would Mean for Epistemology

Let me be precise about the logical structure of the argument. I am not arguing that precognition is real. I am arguing that epistemology should not build in its impossibility. These are very different claims.

**Conditional claim:** IF direct future-knowing is possible -- even rarely, even unreliably, even in ways we do not yet understand -- then JTB collapses.

Here is why. Consider an agent who forms a belief about a future event through some non-standard epistemic process -- call it a "premonition," a "gut feeling," an "intuition," or whatever term is least philosophically loaded. The agent believes, at time t1, that event E will occur at time t2 > t1. At t2, event E does in fact occur.

On JTB's analysis, this belief is:

- **True**: Yes. E occurred. The belief correctly represents reality.
- **Believed**: Yes. The agent genuinely held the belief.
- **Justified**: No -- not by standard evidential criteria. The agent had no past evidence, no reliable inference, no perceptual data supporting the prediction. The belief was formed through an epistemic process that JTB does not recognize as justificatory.

So JTB says: this is not knowledge. The agent had a true belief but lacked justification.

But consider: the belief was formed through a cognitive process that produced a veridical representation of reality. The agent got it right. Reality confirmed the belief. If this is not knowledge, what is it? And if we insist it is not knowledge, we must ask: are we making a principled epistemological judgment, or are we simply enforcing our prior metaphysical commitment that this kind of knowing is impossible?

The point is not that every premonition is knowledge. Obviously not -- most are false, vague, or unfalsifiable. The point is that **an epistemological framework should be neutral about the metaphysics of time.** It should not build in, as a structural assumption, a particular view about the direction of epistemic access.

JTB is not neutral. It presupposes that knowledge flows only from past to present -- that the evidential arrow points in one temporal direction. This presupposition is not a conclusion of epistemological inquiry; it is a premise that shapes the inquiry from the start. And it is a premise that has never been argued for.

An epistemology that is genuinely neutral about time would need to accommodate the possibility -- however remote, however empirically unsupported -- that some beliefs are formed through processes that access future states of affairs. It would evaluate such beliefs not by whether they conform to backward-looking evidential standards, but by whether they are formed through non-defective cognitive processes and whether reality vindicates them.

This is precisely what the temporal alternative offers.

## 6. The Temporal Alternative

I propose replacing JTB with a framework built on three conditions that do not presuppose a particular metaphysics of time:

### Epistemic Integrity (EI)

The belief was formed through a non-defective cognitive process. "Non-defective" means: the agent was not impaired (by intoxication, psychosis, coercion, or systematic deception), the cognitive process was functioning as designed (whether "design" is understood in evolutionary, developmental, or teleological terms), and the agent was responsive to available evidence (not ignoring relevant information or engaging in motivated reasoning).

Epistemic Integrity replaces "justification" by shifting the focus from the product (the evidential support for the belief) to the process (the cognitive mechanism that generated it). This shift is crucial because it allows the framework to accommodate epistemic processes that we do not yet fully understand. If an agent forms a belief through a process that is non-defective by the best available standards, the belief has epistemic integrity regardless of whether the process is perceptual, inferential, intuitive, or something else entirely.

### Temporal Vindication (TV)

Reality confirms the belief at some time t2 > t1, where t1 is the time of belief formation. "Confirms" means: the state of affairs represented by the belief obtains, and the obtaining is not a matter of the agent's having caused the state of affairs to obtain (no self-fulfilling prophecies, no manipulation).

Temporal Vindication replaces "truth" by distributing the truth condition across time rather than demanding it at a single moment. A belief does not need to be true at the moment of formation; it needs to be vindicated by subsequent events. This accommodates scientific knowledge naturally: a hypothesis formed at t1 on the basis of limited evidence is vindicated at t2 when further evidence confirms it. It also accommodates the possibility of future-directed knowledge: a belief about a future event formed at t1 is vindicated at t2 when the event occurs.

### No Active Defeaters (NAD)

There is no evidence that the agent is currently ignoring, suppressing, or failing to respond to that would undermine the belief. "Active" means: the defeating evidence is available to the agent and the agent is capable of processing it. This condition does not require the agent to have considered every possible defeater -- only that she not be actively ignoring relevant counter-evidence.

No Active Defeaters replaces the various fourth conditions proposed by post-Gettier epistemology (no false lemmas, undefeatedness, safety, etc.) with a simpler, more tractable condition. It asks not whether the belief could be defeated in some hypothetical scenario, but whether the agent is currently in an epistemically responsible relationship with the available evidence.

### How the Framework Works

**Standard empirical knowledge:** An agent observes that it is raining (non-defective perception = EI). She believes it is raining. Reality confirms: it is raining (TV). She is not ignoring any counter-evidence (NAD). Result: knowledge.

**Scientific knowledge:** A scientist hypothesizes that a new drug will reduce symptoms (non-defective inference from preliminary data = EI). Clinical trials confirm the hypothesis (TV at t2 > t1). The scientist has responded appropriately to all available evidence (NAD). Result: knowledge -- and the temporal structure of the framework naturally captures the provisional, confirmable nature of scientific knowledge.

**Alleged precognitive knowledge:** An agent forms a belief about a future event through an unusual epistemic process (EI is satisfied if the process is non-defective by available standards). The event occurs (TV). The agent was not ignoring counter-evidence (NAD). Result: the framework accommodates this as potential knowledge without either endorsing or denying the reality of precognition. It evaluates the belief on its merits -- process integrity, vindication, responsiveness to evidence -- rather than excluding it by metaphysical fiat.

**Gettier cases:** Consider the classic case: Smith believes Jones will get the job and Jones has ten coins in his pocket, so Smith infers "the person who will get the job has ten coins in his pocket." Smith himself gets the job, and he too has ten coins. On JTB, this is justified true belief but not knowledge. On the temporal framework: Smith's belief was formed through a defective inferential process (the inference went through a false intermediary about Jones getting the job), so EI is not satisfied. Result: not knowledge. The Gettier problem dissolves -- not by adding conditions, but by shifting the locus of evaluation from product to process.

This framework is developed in full formal detail in the companion paper, "Retroactive Knowledge Theory: A Temporal Epistemology for Finite Agents."

## 7. The Burden of Proof Reversal

The current epistemological default runs like this: anyone claiming knowledge must demonstrate that her belief meets the JTB conditions (or whatever patched version is in fashion). The burden of proof is on the claimant. A belief is epistemically "guilty until proven innocent" -- it does not count as knowledge until the agent can show that it is justified, true, and satisfies whatever fourth condition is currently popular.

I argue that this default should be reversed.

A belief that was formed with epistemic integrity and that has been temporally vindicated IS knowledge until someone shows a specific flaw. The burden should fall on the denier -- the person who claims that a vindicated, integrity-formed belief fails to constitute knowledge -- to identify what, specifically, is wrong with it.

This is not a radical proposal. It is how most domains of human inquiry already work:

**Courts of law:** The accused is presumed innocent until proven guilty. The burden of proof is on the prosecution. This is not because we assume everyone is innocent; it is because we recognize that the alternative -- presuming guilt until innocence is proven -- is both unjust and unworkable.

**Science:** Hypotheses are provisionally accepted until falsified (Popper, 1959). A well-supported scientific theory is treated as knowledge until someone produces a specific counter-example, a failed prediction, or a better alternative. The burden is on the challenger, not the proponent. Kuhn (1962) showed that this is how science actually works, even when philosophers of science wished it worked differently.

**Epistemology should follow the same principle.** Valid evidence counts until refuted. A belief formed with epistemic integrity that is temporally vindicated is knowledge until someone demonstrates a specific defect in the process, a specific piece of ignored counter-evidence, or a specific reason to doubt the vindication.

This reversal has a practical consequence: it opens epistemological space for non-standard knowledge claims. Instead of dismissing unusual epistemic processes (intuition, pattern recognition, even claimed precognitive experiences) by default because they fail to meet backward-looking evidential standards, we evaluate them on their track records. If a process consistently produces vindicated beliefs, it earns epistemic credibility -- not because we understand how it works, but because it works. Understanding can come later. Vindication comes first.

This is empirical epistemology. And it is more honest than the alternative.

## 8. Implications

The replacement of JTB with a temporal, process-oriented epistemology has consequences that extend well beyond the seminar room.

**Epistemology becomes empirical.** If knowledge is a matter of process integrity and temporal vindication, then we can study knowledge empirically. We can investigate which belief-forming processes lead to vindication and which do not. We can track the reliability of different cognitive mechanisms across different domains and different contexts. Epistemology ceases to be a purely a priori enterprise -- a philosopher in an armchair generating intuitions about hypothetical cases -- and becomes, at least in part, an empirical science of cognition. This is not eliminativism about epistemology; it is naturalism about epistemology, in the tradition of Quine but without Quine's behaviorism.

**The Gettier problem dissolves.** On the temporal framework, there is no Gettier problem because there is no requirement for simultaneous justification and truth. The conditions that generate Gettier cases -- justified but accidentally true beliefs -- cannot arise because the framework does not demand that justification and truth coincide at a single moment. It demands process integrity (which screens out defective inferences) and temporal vindication (which distributes the truth condition across time). The combination screens out Gettier cases without needing any additional condition.

**Scientific knowledge is naturally accommodated.** One of the persistent embarrassments of JTB is that it struggles to accommodate scientific knowledge. Scientific knowledge is provisional, revisable, and temporally extended. A scientific theory is accepted at t1 on the basis of evidence, revised at t2 in light of new evidence, and either vindicated or abandoned at t3. JTB, with its snapshot architecture, cannot capture this process. The temporal framework can: scientific knowledge is a paradigm case of belief formed with epistemic integrity and temporally vindicated by subsequent evidence.

**Non-standard epistemic practices can be evaluated fairly.** JTB excludes non-standard epistemic practices -- intuition, pattern recognition, gut feelings, claimed psychic experiences -- not because they have been shown to be unreliable, but because they do not conform to the framework's backward-looking conception of justification. The temporal framework does not exclude them by assumption. It evaluates them on their track records. If an intuitive process consistently produces vindicated beliefs, it earns epistemic credibility. If it does not, it does not. This is fairer, more empirical, and more honest than ruling out entire categories of epistemic practice by metaphysical decree.

**Philosophy becomes more honest about its own assumptions.** The Oracle Problem reveals that analytic epistemology has been conducting its business on the basis of unargued metaphysical commitments that it has never acknowledged, let alone defended. The temporal framework forces these commitments into the open. It asks: What are you assuming about the structure of time? About the direction of causation? About the limits of consciousness? If your epistemological framework presupposes answers to these questions, it is not a neutral analysis of knowledge -- it is a metaphysical theory wearing an epistemological costume. Taking off the costume is the first step toward genuine philosophical honesty.

---

## 9. Conclusion

JTB is a relic of a time when philosophers assumed -- without argument, without evidence, without even the awareness that they were assuming -- that the epistemic situation of finite agents could be evaluated against an omniscient standard of truth without contradiction. Plato proposed it in the *Theaetetus*. Gettier exposed its fault line in 1963. Sixty years of patches -- no-false-lemma conditions, defeasibility theories, causal theories, relevant alternatives, safety conditions, anti-luck conditions -- have failed to repair it. They have failed because they are attempting to fix the plumbing when the foundation is cracked.

The Oracle Problem reveals the crack. JTB is not metaphysically neutral. It presupposes that epistemic access is temporally asymmetric -- that knowledge flows only from past to present, that evidence is always backward-looking, that the future is epistemically dark. This presupposition is not self-evident, not empirically established, and not philosophically argued for. It is metaphysical policing in a lab coat.

A temporal, process-oriented epistemology -- built on Epistemic Integrity, Temporal Vindication, and No Active Defeaters -- is both more rigorous and more honest. It is more rigorous because it replaces the unstable conjunction of local justification and global truth with tractable, evaluable conditions. It is more honest because it does not smuggle metaphysical commitments into its foundations and then pretend they are not there.

Knowledge is not a crystal you possess. It is a trajectory you travel. And sometimes, the destination reaches back to meet you.
