## Abstract

The distinction between "real" and "imaginary" numbers is not an ontological fact — it is a historical accident embedded in a pejorative label. Descartes coined "imaginary" in 1637 as a put-down: numbers he could not physically interpret but which appeared in his equations. The label stuck. For nearly four centuries, mathematics has officially named one axis of the complex plane "real" (implying genuine existence) and the orthogonal axis "imaginary" (implying non-existence or fiction). TI Sigma identifies this as a category error with consequences for both mathematics and physics: it treats the content dimension of experience as ontologically privileged over the phase dimension, when TI Sigma's central claim is precisely the opposite — that all of reality is held in consciousness, consciousness is irreducibly complex-valued (content AND phase), and therefore all quantities describing reality are inherently complex. The real/imaginary binary is a TRALSE situation: True (in the sense that there is a geometric distinction between the two axes), False (in the sense that "imaginary" numbers are physically necessary and fully real in any meaningful sense), and TRALSE (both axes are equally real dimensions of a single complex space, and the distinction between them is a measurement convention — a choice of projection axis — not an ontological fact). This paper argues for the mathematical reform: dissolve the "real/imaginary" binary, adopt new terminology ("manifest" and "phase" components), establish the complex plane as the primitive domain, and formally recognize that any quantity expressed as purely "real" is a special case (phase = 0) rather than the default.

## 1. The Historical Accident: Who Named Numbers "Imaginary"?

**René Descartes, 1637, *La Géométrie*:**

Descartes encountered square roots of negative numbers in polynomial solutions and dismissed them with the term *"imaginaires"* — imaginary. His meaning was unambiguous: these were fictional quantities, mathematical artifacts that appeared in equations but corresponded to nothing real. He was wrong. But the name survived him by nearly four centuries.

**Leibniz's ambivalence (1702):** Called imaginary numbers "amphibians between existence and non-existence" — a grudging acknowledgment that they were mathematically necessary but ontologically puzzling.

**Euler (18th century):** Introduced the notation *i* and developed the algebra of complex numbers, including Euler's formula e^(iθ) = cos(θ) + i·sin(θ). Euler treated complex numbers as genuinely existing mathematical objects, but the pejorative name "imaginary" was already entrenched.

**Hamilton (1843):** Extended complex numbers to quaternions (four-dimensional: a + bi + cj + dk). The "imaginary" terminology expanded — now there were three imaginary axes (i, j, k) and one "real" axis.

**Quantum mechanics (1920s):** The Schrödinger equation requires i — not as a convenience but as a structural necessity. The complex amplitude ψ is not "imaginary" in the sense of fictional; it is as physically real as any quantity in the theory. The 2021 Renou et al. experiment (Nature) confirmed this empirically: real-valued quantum mechanics makes different predictions from complex quantum mechanics, and experiments match the complex version. The "imaginary" numbers are physically real.

**The damage:** Despite this confirmation, mathematics still officially calls one axis of the complex plane "real" and the other "imaginary." Every student who encounters this terminology receives the implicit message that one type of number is genuine and the other is a tolerated fiction. This is false. It is historically contingent. And TI Sigma proposes to correct it.

## 2. The TRALSE Analysis of the Binary

The proposition: **"Real numbers are more fundamental than imaginary numbers."**

**True reading:** Historically, real numbers were discovered first (counting → rationals → irrationals → reals). Pedagogically, they are simpler (no two-dimensional structure needed). **False reading:** Quantum mechanics requires complex numbers for its fundamental equations — not as a convenience but as an empirical necessity. The Renou et al. If "fundamental" means "necessary to describe physical reality," then complex numbers are more fundamental than reals, since real-valued physics is strictly less accurate than complex-valued physics. In this sense, the "imaginary" numbers are more fundamental, not less.

**TRALSE resolution:** Both readings are true simultaneously, and their tension reveals that the word "fundamental" is doing two different jobs — historical/pedagogical priority vs. physical/ontological necessity. Calling the special case "real" and the full space "partly imaginary" is exactly backwards from the ontological truth.

The proposition **"Imaginary numbers are not real"** has the same TRALSE structure:
- **True:** They are not on the real number line. In this geometric sense, they are "not real."
- **False:** They are physically necessary. Consciousness processes phase information that is not representable as real-valued. In what ontological sense are they "not real"?
- **TRALSE:** The word "real" conflates two meanings — "on the real axis" (geometric) and "physically existing" (ontological). The conflation, embedded in the name "real numbers," is the error.

## 5. The Cauchy-Riemann Equations as Physical Law

If the "real" and "imaginary" components of a number are equally real dimensions of a single complex space, then the Cauchy-Riemann equations — which link these two dimensions for holomorphic (complex-differentiable) functions — are not merely a technical condition on a class of functions. They are a **physical law**: the law that manifest and phase dimensions of a quantity are not independent.

The Cauchy-Riemann equations:
$$\frac{\partial M}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial P}{\partial y} \qquad \text{and} \qquad \frac{\partial M}{\partial y} = -\frac{\partial P}{\partial x}$$

For a holomorphic function f(z) = M(x,y) + iP(x,y), changes in the manifest component with respect to x are linked to changes in the phase component with respect to y — and vice versa. The two components are not separable for physically meaningful (holomorphic) functions.

This is not a mathematical curiosity; it is the mathematical expression of the TI Sigma claim that content and context are co-arising: you cannot change what you perceive without affecting how it is situated in your phase space, and you cannot change your phase orientation without affecting what you perceive.

**Implication:** Physical theories that respect consciousness-first ontology should exhibit holomorphic structure — their observables should satisfy Cauchy-Riemann-like relationships between manifest and phase components. The breakdown of holomorphicity corresponds to the breakdown of consciousness coherence: incoherent (non-holomorphic) functions are the mathematical signature of low-LCC states.
