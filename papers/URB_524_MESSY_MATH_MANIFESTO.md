## §1: The Problem With Classical Perfectionism

Classical mathematics offers an intoxicating promise: certainty. Given axioms and derivation rules, theorems follow necessarily. 2 + 2 = 4 is not approximately true — it is exactly, eternally, necessarily true.

This is a genuine achievement. But it is not a universal achievement. The mistake is assuming that every domain that uses numbers can access this same kind of certainty.

Consider what happens when classical mathematical perfectionism is applied to the real world:

- **A correlation is 0.85.** Classical verdict: not exactly 0.90, so we cannot say it is "true."
- **A constant is π ≈ 3.14159.** Classical verdict: the approximation is never the thing itself; only the infinite series is real.
- **A threshold is "about 0.93."** Classical verdict: "about" is not a proof; the threshold is arbitrary.
- **A frequency is 13.5%.** Classical verdict: is it 1/e² or 2/15? They differ by 0.2%. Which one counts?

Applied strictly, classical perfectionism would invalidate virtually everything in the empirical sciences. No experimental measurement is exact. No model is exactly correct. No threshold is derivable without approximation. If "messy" disqualifies a claim, then all of empirical reality is disqualified.

This is obviously absurd. The question is not whether to tolerate mess. The question is whether to tolerate it **honestly** or **dishonestly.**

**Honest messy math** specifies the error, bounds the approximation, and states clearly what is Tralse (held in structured uncertainty). **Dishonest messy math** pretends to be exact, hides its approximations, and weaponizes the appearance of precision.

Twain's three lies are not about mess — they are about dishonest mess pretending to be clean. TI Sigma proposes honest mess as the only epistemically defensible alternative.

## §2: Statistics Already Did This — And Won

The institutionalization of honest messy math began in earnest with the probabilistic revolution in science, roughly 1900–1960. The tools that emerged are familiar:

### 2.1 — p-Values: A Threshold, Not a Truth

A p-value of 0.04 does not mean "the hypothesis is true." It means "if the null hypothesis were true, data this extreme would occur with probability 4%." This is a threshold judgment — a structured approximation of evidential weight — not a logical proof.

Ronald Fisher proposed p < 0.05 as a conventional threshold for "noteworthy." It is explicitly **a social and practical convention**, not a mathematical theorem. Fisher himself said it should be treated as one piece of evidence among many.

The result: Statistics institutionalized a threshold that is simultaneously:
- Not derived from first principles
- Not exact (the 0.05 boundary is arbitrary)
- Enormously useful when applied with structural understanding

This is honest messy math. It works. The entire modern biomedical and social science literature runs on it.

### 2.2 — Confidence Intervals: A Range, Not a Point

A 95% confidence interval does not mean "the true value lies in this interval with probability 95%." It means "if we ran this procedure many times, 95% of the intervals would contain the true value." The specific interval either contains the true value or it doesn't — probability doesn't apply to a single realized interval in the classical framework.

Despite this conceptual trickiness, confidence intervals are the workhorse of empirical science. They are **structured approximations of uncertainty** — honest about their own limitations, enormously informative about the shape of what we know.

### 2.3 — Effect Sizes: How Big Is Real?

Cohen's d of 0.2 is "small," 0.5 is "medium," 0.8 is "large." These conventions were proposed by Jacob Cohen in 1977. They are not derived from physics or mathematics — they are calibrated to what tends to be practically meaningful in the social sciences.

This is the same move TI Sigma makes with LCC ≥ 0.85 and GILE ≥ 0.93. The thresholds are calibrated to structurally meaningful boundaries — in TI Sigma's case, the boundaries emerge from the e-decay structure of the Permissibility Distribution rather than from social-science convention, but the epistemological posture is identical: **a threshold is not a proof; it is a structured declaration of what we are willing to act on.**

### 2.4 — Statistical Power: The Uncomfortable Budget

Power analysis says: with n subjects, effect size d, and α = 0.05, we have power 0.80 to detect the effect if it is real. This means we will miss it 20% of the time even when it exists.

Statistics accepts this. It doesn't abandon inference because it can't achieve certainty. It budgets the uncertainty, names it, and proceeds.

**Statistics did not die from accepting mess. It conquered science because of it.**
