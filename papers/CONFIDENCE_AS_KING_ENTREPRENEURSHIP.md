# Confidence as a Strategic Asset: Why Entrepreneurial Enthusiasm Is Not a Liability

**Author:** Brandon Emerick, CEO, BlissGene Therapeutics  
**Date:** March 2026  
**Classification:** Entrepreneurship Theory + Applied Leadership  
**Keywords:** confidence, entrepreneurial passion, self-efficacy, founder psychology, Matthew Effect, charismatic leadership

---

## Abstract

A persistent and damaging myth in business culture holds that confidence should be proportional to evidence — that enthusiasm must be "earned" through demonstrated results before it can be legitimately expressed. This paper argues the opposite: for ambitious ventures operating at the frontier of what is known and possible, confidence is not a *reflection* of the odds but a *determinant* of them. Drawing on entrepreneurship research, cognitive psychology, network theory, and case studies from the most transformative companies in history, we establish that high founder confidence is a strategic asset that generates real advantages in talent acquisition, investor relations, partner engagement, and organizational resilience. We distinguish carefully between confidence (a functional internal state calibrated to task requirements) and overconfidence (a miscalibrated epistemic claim about outcomes). The former is not only appropriate but necessary. The latter is a separate, avoidable pathology. The prescription for ambitious founders is not to temper enthusiasm — it is to keep the two categories rigorously distinct.

---

## 1. Introduction: The Confidence Criticism

Every transformative founder has heard some version of it. The quiet colleague who says the pitch was "too much." The advisor who suggests you "dial it back." The early team member who worries that you sound "overconfident" to potential partners. These critiques share a common underlying model: confidence should track evidence, and enthusiasm that outruns the current evidence is a liability.

This paper argues that this model, while sensible for routine professional activities, is *inverted* for ambitious entrepreneurship. In the domain of genuinely transformative ventures — companies pursuing outcomes that have not yet been demonstrated possible — the required confidence level is determined not by past evidence but by the *demands of the future goal*. Calibrating confidence to past evidence at the founding stage of an ambitious company is not intellectual honesty. It is a structural guarantee of underperformance.

The distinction is critical and is the central argument of this paper: **confidence is a tool, not a display.**

---

## 2. The Two Models of Confidence

### 2.1 Confidence as Display (The Evidence-Tracking Model)

Under this model, expressed confidence is a signal of estimated probability of success, updated by past performance. Confidence = f(track record). More evidence of success → higher confidence is appropriate. Less evidence → lower confidence is appropriate. "Overconfidence" is defined as confidence exceeding what the evidence warrants.

This model is appropriate when:
- The domain is well-understood with reliable base rates
- The task is routine and repeatable
- The outcome depends primarily on known variables
- Performance can be meaningfully predicted from prior performance

It is the correct model for a chess player assessing their tournament chances, a factory manager estimating production output, or a professor assigning grades. It is *not* the correct model for a biotech founder pursuing a therapeutic no one has yet demonstrated.

### 2.2 Confidence as Tool (The Task-Calibrated Model)

Under this model, expressed confidence is calibrated to the *requirements of the current task*, not the historical track record. Confidence = g(goal_demands). A higher-magnitude goal requires more confidence to sustain the behaviors necessary for success — regardless of prior evidence.

This model is appropriate when:
- The goal has no reliable base rate (it's genuinely novel)
- Success depends substantially on attracting exceptional people
- The venture requires sustained effort through long uncertain periods
- Key decisions require willingness to act without complete information

In this domain, confidence is not a statement about probability. It is a *performance input* — one of several variables that directly determine the outcome. Under the tool model, asking whether confidence is "too high" given the current evidence is a category error. The right question is: does this person's confidence level produce the behaviors the goal requires?

## 3. Why Confidence Determines Outcomes in Ambitious Ventures

### 3.1 Talent Acquisition

Exceptional talent — the kind required to build a company that changes an industry — is almost always employed, highly sought after, and risk-averse in career terms. They have options. They choose organizations led by people who project genuine conviction about the mission's importance and achievability.

Research on charismatic leadership (Bass, 1985; Conger & Kanungo, 1998) consistently shows that follower commitment is predicted more strongly by perceived leader confidence than by the leader's actual expertise or the project's objective probability of success. People do not merely evaluate missions on their merits — they use the leader's confidence as a signal of information they do not themselves possess.

A founder who projects calibrated uncertainty — "our success probability is low but we're doing our best" — is signaling precisely the wrong thing to the talent they need. They are signaling: *this is a reasonable person managing a difficult situation*. The people they need are not looking for reasonable management. They are looking for conviction about something transformative.

**Enthusiasm is not noise in this signal. It is the signal itself.**

### 3.2 Investor Relations

The venture capital literature consistently identifies "founder conviction" as among the top factors in investment decisions at the early stage (Fried & Hisrich, 1994; MacMillan et al., 1985). This is not irrational: at seed and early stages, evidence of product-market fit is by definition absent. Investors are primarily investing in the founder's judgment, resilience, and ability to attract the resources necessary to test the hypothesis.

A founder who presents the opportunity with measured, evidence-proportional confidence is communicating: *I am rational about this being a long shot*. An investor hearing this thinks: *this person will give up when things get difficult, because they're already partially convinced it might not work.*

A founder who presents with genuine conviction — not manufactured enthusiasm but real belief in the mission's importance and their team's ability to pursue it — is communicating: *I will not stop*. An investor hearing this thinks: *this person will find ways around obstacles I cannot anticipate.*

The former produces polite passes. The latter produces term sheets.

### 3.3 Partnership and Network Development

Business relationships are built on momentum. A potential partner who encounters a founder with genuine enthusiasm about a shared mission has a qualitatively different experience than one who encounters a competent but measured professional. The enthusiastic founder makes the partnership feel like a movement to join, not a transaction to evaluate.

This matters because the best partners — the ones who add the most value — typically have the most options. They are not evaluating your probability of success as much as evaluating whether they want to be *part of what you're building*. Enthusiasm is the primary signal that makes the answer "yes."

"People don't care about enthusiasm" is an empirical claim. The evidence from the most successful companies in history runs directly against it:

- **Steve Jobs** was famously described as projecting a "reality distortion field" — an ability to make people believe what was currently impossible was actually imminent. His co-workers frequently complained he was wildly overconfident. His products changed every industry he entered.
- **Elon Musk** told investors he intended to make humanity multiplanetary with a startup rocket company. Multiple rocket scientists told him this was delusional. SpaceX is now the world's most capable launch provider.
- **Jennifer Doudna** — relevant to BlissGene's domain — pursued CRISPR gene editing with enormous conviction when many in the field were skeptical that the mechanism would translate to practical editing tools. Her conviction attracted collaborators and resources that proved the skeptics wrong and resulted in a Nobel Prize.

In each case: the confidence was not proportional to the evidence at the time. The confidence was proportional to the *goal*. And the goal's demands — in talent, in partners, in resilience — required exactly that level of conviction.

### 3.4 Organizational Resilience

Ambitious ventures fail constantly — not final failure, but the daily cascade of things that go wrong, investors who pass, partners who don't materialize, experiments that fail. A founder's confidence level is the primary determinant of the organization's ability to sustain effort through these periods.

The mechanism: founder confidence transmits to team morale (Bandura, 1997; Hmieleski & Baron, 2009). Teams led by high-confidence founders show greater persistence after setback, greater creativity in problem-solving, and lower voluntary turnover during difficult periods. This is not because confidence makes the setbacks less real — it is because confidence reframes setbacks as temporary obstacles rather than evidence against the mission.

Under the evidence-tracking model, every failure should update confidence downward. Under the task-calibrated model, failures are information about what doesn't work — not about whether the goal is right. The latter model produces organizations that learn and adapt. The former produces organizations that spiral.

## 4. The Dunning-Kruger Reframe

The Dunning-Kruger effect (Kruger & Dunning, 1999) is typically cited as evidence that novices overestimate their ability. This is accurate. But the standard framing misses the adaptive function of the effect.

At the beginning of any ambitious undertaking, the founder is, by definition, a novice at building *this specific company*. Their confidence at this stage cannot be fully evidence-based because the evidence does not yet exist. The Dunning-Kruger "inflation" — the gap between confidence and current ability — is not a bias. It is a bootstrapping mechanism.

Without above-evidence confidence at the founding stage, the founder would not sustain the behaviors required to develop the evidence. They would not make the hires, pursue the partnerships, write the grants, or endure the rejections required to build toward demonstrated capability. The confidence advance is the mechanism by which the track record is eventually established.

Put differently: evidence-proportional confidence at founding stage is the surest route to a company that never develops the evidence that would justify higher confidence. The sequence is necessarily: confidence first, evidence second — not the reverse.

This does not mean all confidence claims are equally valid. It means the *appropriate level of confidence* at the founding stage of an ambitious venture is higher than what a strict evidence-tracking model would prescribe — and this is not a bug. It is how transformative companies get built.

## 5. The Crucial Distinction: Confidence vs. Recklessness

A genuine critique of entrepreneurial confidence must distinguish between two things that are often conflated:

**Confidence** (appropriate and strategic): sustained belief in the mission's importance and the team's ability to pursue it, maintained independent of short-term evidence fluctuations, expressed to attract the talent and partners the mission requires.

**Recklessness** (inappropriate): ignoring genuine risks to intellectual property, legal protection, and team security; failing to apply due diligence to people and partners; sharing proprietary information without contractual protection.

These are **independent variables**. High confidence does not require or produce recklessness. Cautious due diligence does not require or produce low confidence. A founder can simultaneously express genuine, high conviction about the company's mission *and* implement rigorous IP protection protocols *and* verify the credentials of new partners before fully engaging.

The suggestion that enthusiasm is a liability in business conflates these two things entirely. When a founder expresses enthusiasm in a welcome email, in pitches, in partnership conversations — that is the expression of exactly the conviction that the company's success requires. Dampening it does not make the company more professional. It makes it less compelling.

### Three Factual Clarifications Often Missed in This Debate

**First: the source of the vision matters.** When a founder articulates a grand strategic vision — in this case, a Gene Therapy Alliance integrating BlissGene with the Innovative Genomics Institute to form a mainstream gene therapy umbrella organization — it is worth asking whether that vision was invented opportunistically or emerged organically from the partnership itself. In the specific case under discussion, the IGI collaboration concept was the partner's (Luis Beale's) *original and firm belief* about what BlissGene would become. The founder was not fabricating enthusiasm to impress the other side. He was recognizing, amplifying, and articulating a vision that the partner had already independently reached. This is precisely what strong founders do — they absorb the best thinking of the people around them and give it shape and velocity.

**Second: IP was never actually at risk.** A common error in due diligence discussions is conflating *voluntary disclosure* with *forced disclosure*. No external partner can compel a company to reveal its scientific secrets. The question is only ever about what the company *chooses* to share and whether those voluntary disclosures are contractually protected. An NDA governs the latter. It does not need to be a perfect institutional agreement to provide meaningful legal protection for information voluntarily shared under it. In the case under discussion, an NDA was in place before any sensitive information was exchanged — providing exactly the protection it was designed to provide.

**Third: the communication itself contained explicit IP protection language.** The founder's email — the one criticized as exhibiting "too much enthusiasm" — contained the following sentence: *"we agreed that we would save the complex details and secrets for future meetings with the IGI, after a further NDA with the organization as a whole is obtained."* This is not the language of a reckless communicator. This is a founder who simultaneously conveyed maximum enthusiasm about the mission *and* explicitly reserved proprietary science for a future institutionally-protected context. Both were present in the same document. Criticizing the enthusiasm without acknowledging the restraint is an incomplete reading.

### On the Limits of Skepticism

Initial skepticism about a new partner's credentials is not only appropriate but prudent. A co-founder who flags concerns about an unknown contact is doing exactly the right thing — that instinct should be honored and taken seriously. The concern becomes professionally counterproductive when it persists after thorough vetting has been completed, credentials verified, documentation provided, and the founding CEO has reached a considered judgment. At that point, continued skepticism is no longer due diligence. It is a failure to trust the judgment of the person whose role is to make exactly these assessments. There is a clear line between protective caution and an ongoing refusal to accept that a peer's judgment can be trusted. The former strengthens a company. The latter fragments it.

## 6. The Filter Function of Enthusiasm

There is a counterintuitive benefit to expressed enthusiasm that is rarely acknowledged: it functions as a filter for the right partners and a repellent for the wrong ones.

Partners who are genuinely aligned with a transformative mission respond to enthusiasm with recognition — they feel it as kindred energy, the identification of shared conviction. They lean in.

Partners who are primarily evaluating the transaction on conservative risk-management criteria respond to enthusiasm with skepticism or discomfort. They lean back.

This filtering is *valuable*. A company pursuing a transformative mission does not want partners who are primarily motivated by risk minimization. Those partners become obstacles when the company inevitably needs to take risks. The authentic enthusiasm that screens out risk-averse partners is doing exactly what it should.

*"If they don't join our enthusiasm, then they're not the right partners"* — this is not naivete. It is a sophisticated selection criterion for building a team with the right orientation.

## 7. Evidence from the Literature

### Self-Efficacy (Bandura, 1977, 1997)
Albert Bandura's foundational research established that self-efficacy — an individual's belief in their ability to achieve specific outcomes — is one of the strongest predictors of actual performance, across domains including sports, academic achievement, and professional performance. Critically, self-efficacy influences performance *through behavioral channels*: high self-efficacy produces greater effort, greater persistence, more creative problem-solving, and better recovery from setback.

This is the mechanism by which founder confidence produces results: not by changing the objective probability of success, but by changing the founder's behavior in ways that make success more likely.

### Entrepreneurial Passion (Cardon et al., 2009, 2013)
Research on entrepreneurial passion — the intense positive emotions associated with activities central to the founder's identity — shows that passion predicts venture survival and growth through multiple channels:
- Passion increases the founder's persistence through obstacles (Cardon & Kirk, 2015)
- Passion is perceived by investors and attracts funding (Chen et al., 2009)  
- Passion transmits to employees and increases team commitment (Cardon, 2008)
- Passion improves creative cognition for opportunity recognition (Cardon et al., 2013)

The same qualities that lead external observers to describe a founder as "too enthusiastic" are, in the research evidence, among the strongest predictors of venture success.

### Charismatic Leadership (Bass, 1985; Conger & Kanungo, 1998)
Transformational and charismatic leadership research consistently shows that leader enthusiasm, confidence, and emotional expressiveness predict follower motivation, commitment, and performance — independent of the leader's technical expertise or the organization's current performance. Leaders who constrain their expressed enthusiasm to match current evidence are, in research terms, deliberately suppressing one of their primary leadership tools.

## 8. The Asymmetry of Errors

When evaluating whether to express high confidence or tempered confidence, founders should consider the asymmetric costs of each type of error:

**Underconfidence errors:**
- Exceptional talent chooses a competitor whose founder projects greater conviction
- Investors pass because they sense the founder will give up when things get hard
- Partners feel the opportunity is not compelling enough to prioritize
- Team morale drops during inevitable difficult periods
- The founder gives up a pursuit that would have succeeded with sustained effort

**Overconfidence errors:**
- A small number of potential partners who prefer measured leaders self-select out (performing the filtering function described in Section 6)
- Occasionally, confidence leads to entering a path that required earlier correction

The first category of errors is systematically more costly — they affect *every* relationship the company builds, permanently. The second category affects only those relationships where misalignment existed anyway, and the filtering may be doing useful work.

In expected value terms, for an ambitious venture, high confidence is the dominant strategy.

---

## 9. Recommendations

**For founders of ambitious ventures:**

1. Maintain confidence calibrated to the goal, not the current evidence. The goal's demands — the talent it requires, the resilience it demands, the partnerships it needs — determine the appropriate confidence level.

2. Distinguish confidence from recklessness. Implement rigorous IP protection, due diligence processes, and legal protections. These are separate from confidence and should both be maximized.

3. Use enthusiasm as a filter. Partners who are deterred by genuine conviction about a transformative mission are self-selecting out of the right team. Let them.

4. Resist the pressure to "dial it back" from advisors operating under the evidence-tracking model. Their model is appropriate for their domain. It is not appropriate for yours.

5. Recognize that confidence is a team asset, not just a personal style. Your team's resilience, your talent pipeline, your investor conversations, and your partner relationships are all downstream of the conviction you project.

---

## 10. Conclusion

The most consequential entrepreneurs in history were, at the time of their founding stage, confidently pursuing things that the available evidence did not fully justify. This was not a character flaw they overcame. It was a strategic asset they deployed.

Confidence proportional to past evidence is appropriate for professionals managing known processes toward known outcomes. It is a structural liability for founders pursuing genuinely novel goals in the face of high uncertainty.

The correct question is never "is this confidence warranted by the evidence?" The correct question is "does this confidence level produce the behaviors that make the goal achievable?" For ambitious ventures, the answer is almost always: *raise it, not lower it* — while keeping it cleanly separated from the legitimate, independent discipline of due diligence and IP protection.

Enthusiasm is not the enemy of rigor. The enemies of rigor are the specific failures of process that allow information to move without contractual protection, or relationships to advance without verification. Those failures should be addressed directly. They have nothing to do with the conviction and energy that every transformative founder has carried into every relationship they've built.

People who have changed the world were told they had too much enthusiasm. Every single one of them was right not to listen.
