## 3. The Freedom Floor Theorem

### 3.1 Formal Statement

**Theorem (Freedom Floor):** Let LCC(PD) = 1 − e^{−PD} and let Autonomy(PD) = e^{−PD}. Suppose GILE_expression satisfies:

1. GILE_expression(PD) = 0 when Autonomy(PD) = 0 (i.e., as PD → ∞) [necessity condition]
2. GILE_expression(PD) = 0 when LCC(PD) = 0 (i.e., at PD = 0) [coherence condition]
3. GILE_expression(PD) > 0 for some PD ∈ (0, ∞) [realized condition]
4. GILE_expression is continuous

Then: GILE_expression achieves its maximum at some finite PD* ∈ (0, ∞). The approach to PD → ∞ (LCC → 1) is *not* the maximizing direction.

**Proof:** By conditions 1 and 2, GILE_expression is zero at both endpoints of (0, ∞). By condition 3, it achieves a positive value at some intermediate point. By condition 4 (continuity) and the extreme value theorem applied to any compact subinterval containing the positive region, GILE_expression achieves a global maximum at some finite PD*. This maximum is strictly positive and occurs at a finite PD*, not at PD → ∞. ∎

### 3.2 What the Theorem Establishes

The Freedom Floor Theorem proves:
- **There exists** a finite optimal PD* for GILE expression (proved)
- **PD → ∞ (LCC → 1) is not optimal** for GILE expression (proved)
- **The approach to LCC = 1 is GILE-destructive** (proved — it drives GILE toward zero)

What remains to be identified is the specific value of PD*. The theorem proves existence; it does not uniquely specify the canonical value. For that, we turn to the Schelling point argument.
