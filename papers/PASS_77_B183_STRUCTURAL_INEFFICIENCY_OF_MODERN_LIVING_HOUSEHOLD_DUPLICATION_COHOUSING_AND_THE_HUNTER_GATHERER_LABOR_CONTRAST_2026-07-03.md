## 0. The prompt (user request)

> In the Real Work chapter, add a section on the total inefficiency of modern living — mowing lawns, maintaining large houses, hours of chores daily because there are only 2–3 people per household. Contrast permaculture approaches like ecovillages, where people live efficiently and split costs/labor via cohousing. Also the time-tested hunter-gatherer contrast: ~15 hours/week of work, with only ~40% of the population consistently working. Modern life is NOT lazy as people falsely claim — sustaining oneself financially and occupationally is FAR harder than the hunter-gatherer lifestyle, especially for those who isolate themselves and manage a ton of material possessions.

## 2. Claims made, and their honest status

**Defensible / well-supported:**
- Average U.S. household size fell ~3.4 (1950) → ~2.5 (today), single-person homes rising (U.S. Census Bureau).
- Small households are a **diseconomy of scale**: shrinking household size raises per-capita resource use even at fixed population (**Liu, Daily, Ehrlich & Luck, *Nature* 2003**, "Effects of household dynamics on resource consumption and biodiversity").
- Labor-saving appliances did **not** cut domestic labor, because standards rose and the work was **privatized** into individual homes (**Ruth Schwartz Cowan, *More Work for Mother*, 1983**) — the load-bearing citation for "isolation manufactures work."
- Turfgrass ≈ largest irrigated "crop" in the U.S., ~40 million acres (**Milesi et al., 2005**, remote-sensing estimate).
- **Cohousing** (private homes + shared common facilities) originated in Denmark in the 1970s, introduced to English readers by **McCamant & Durrett, *Cohousing*, 1988**. Not communal ownership — shared *labor + infrastructure*.
- **Permaculture** term coined by **Mollison & Holmgren, *Permaculture One*, 1978**. **Findhorn** ecovillage measured at ~half the UK average ecological footprint / among the lowest in the industrialized world (**Tinsley & George, 2006**).
- **Keynes, "Economic Possibilities for Our Grandchildren" (1930)** predicted a 15-hour work week; productivity arrived, the leisure did not (consumption standards + duplication absorbed it).

**CONTESTED — handled with an explicit #69 flag in the text (see §3):**
- The hunter-gatherer "~15 hours/week" figure (Richard B. Lee's Ju/'hoansi data; Marshall Sahlins's "**original affluent society**," *Stone Age Economics*, 1972).

## 3. The #69 honesty rail on the hunter-gatherer figure (critical — do not drop)

The user's "~15 hours/week, ~40% working" is the popular reading of Lee/Sahlins, and it is **genuinely contested**. The section states this in the open rather than repeating the headline:
- Lee's ~15 hours counted **foraging only** — the trip out and food brought back. It **excluded** food processing, tool manufacture/repair, firewood/water hauling, childcare, and camp maintenance.
- Adding those in raises total working time **substantially** — by some estimates into the 40+ hour range, i.e., not obviously below a modern week (**David Kaplan, "The Darker Side of the Original Affluent Society," *J. Anthropological Research*, 2000**).
- On "40% working": Lee observed the **young and old were largely exempt**, a substantial fraction of the band supported by the rest. The section keeps the *direction* of this finding (many were supported by others' labor) but **does not assert a precise percentage** it cannot source cleanly — the exact figure varies by study and definition.

**Therefore the claim the section actually makes is the narrower, defensible one:** foragers spent **little time specifically securing food/shelter** and carried a **near-zero maintenance burden** on few possessions — precisely the redundancy-free condition cohousing/ecovillages try to recover. NOT "foragers barely worked and moderns are dupes."

Two further guardrails in the text:
- **No romanticizing.** The comparison is about *hours of labor and maintenance burden*, explicitly **not** about health, safety, violence, or overall welfare (those carried real hardship the comparison does not touch).
- **Ecovillages are self-selected**; their measured efficiencies may not transfer wholesale to a whole society.

## 4. The thesis the section defends (the "not lazy" point)

Framed as a **reasoned convergence**, not a measured law (matching the chapter's stated "framework-internal proposal" register): sustaining oneself in a market economy means first earning money for housing/transport/insurance/utilities (labor with **no forager equivalent**) *and then* privately performing the cooking/cleaning/maintenance/yard work a shared arrangement would pool. Merely **staying afloat** is plausibly a **heavier total load** than the forager's subsistence week — the opposite of laziness: large, unrewarded effort poured into infrastructure that was **inefficient by design**. This is the macro-scale statement of the chapter's own SIS-1 point.

### One-line summary

Scaled the chapter's busywork thesis up to society: the isolated 2–3-person household duplicates a full private infrastructure (lawns, appliances, chores) that cohousing/ecovillages dissolve by pooling labor (Cowan 1983; Liu et al. 2003; McCamant & Durrett 1988; Findhorn/Tinsley & George 2006); the hunter-gatherer contrast is used **honestly** — low *food-getting* time and near-zero maintenance burden, with the Sahlins/Lee "15 hours" flagged as contested (Kaplan 2000) — to show the modern load is heavy **by design, not lazy** (Keynes 1930). No new principle; count **80**.
