## Abstract

The standard clinical picture of autism spectrum condition (ASC) emphasizes deficits in social cognition, reduced empathy, and difficulty reading emotional cues. This picture describes a real subset of the autistic population. However, a substantial and undercharacterized subset presents with the **inverse profile**: high emotional sensitivity (affective empathy), deep awareness of social dynamics, and acute capacity for emotional reading — combined with difficulty navigating those dynamics in real time, chronic social exhaustion, and outcomes (social isolation, relationship difficulty, vocational struggle) that look clinically identical to low-empathy autism from the outside. We term this profile **High-EQ Autism**. We review the empirical literature on: camouflaging/masking, empathy fragmentation in autism, 2e (twice-exceptional) profiles, and autistic burnout. We estimate High-EQ Autism may constitute 25-40% of the autistic population, with significant diagnostic underidentification — particularly in women, late-diagnosed adults, and individuals in intellectually demanding fields. The TI Sigma analysis: High-EQ Autism is TRALSE with respect to the low-EQ/high-EQ axis — it possesses both poles simultaneously, and the simultaneous possession is the source of its distinctive suffering.

## 1. The Empathy Fragmentation Hypothesis

The central empirical finding that structures this paper: **empathy is not a unitary trait**.

Empathy research distinguishes at minimum two components (Davis, 1983; Blair, 2005; Shamay-Tsoory, 2011):

- **Cognitive empathy (CE)**: The ability to accurately infer another person's mental state — what they are thinking, what they know, what they believe. Sometimes called "Theory of Mind" (ToM) or "mentalizing."

- **Affective empathy (AE)**: The capacity to feel what another person feels — emotional contagion, sympathetic resonance, distress at others' distress.

The standard autism picture assumes both CE and AE are reduced. Multiple lines of evidence refute this as a universal description:

### 1.1 The Empathy Imbalance Finding

Shah et al. (2019) and Schwenck et al. (2012) document a consistent pattern in autistic samples: **reduced CE, preserved or elevated AE**. The autistic individual has difficulty inferring what others are thinking (ToM deficit) but experiences strong emotional resonance with others' feelings (AE intact or enhanced).

Markram & Markram (2010) — the **Intense World Theory** — proposes the opposite of reduced empathy: autistic neurology produces hyperreactivity, including hyperreactivity to emotional stimuli. The social withdrawal associated with autism in this model is not due to lack of care or interest, but to being overwhelmed by the intensity of social-emotional input.

### 1.2 Alexithymia as Confound

A critical confound in autism empathy research: **alexithymia** — difficulty identifying and describing one's own emotional states. Alexithymia co-occurs with autism at rates of approximately 50% (Kinnaird et al., 2019), compared to ~10% in the general population.

Studies that measure empathy using self-report (e.g., "How often do you feel what others feel?") will underestimate AE in autistic individuals with alexithymia — not because they do not feel it, but because they have difficulty recognizing and naming the feeling they are having. When physiological measures replace self-report, the empathy deficit often disappears or reverses (Bird et al., 2010).

This means: a large fraction of "low empathy" autism findings in the literature are artifacts of the alexithymia confound. The person feels deeply; they cannot name what they are feeling; the questionnaire records "low empathy."

## 2. The High-EQ Autism Profile: Clinical Description

High-EQ Autism presents with the following constellation:

**Social-emotional capacities (intact or elevated)**:
- High affective empathy — feels others' emotions strongly, sometimes overwhelmingly
- Acute observation of social dynamics — notices micro-expressions, tone shifts, relationship tensions that others miss
- Deep care about social relationships — places high value on connection
- Strong moral sensitivity — distress at injustice, acute fairness concerns
- Capacity for emotional attunement in one-on-one close relationships

**Social-executive difficulties (impaired)**:
- Difficulty regulating emotional responses in real-time social situations — the input is processed correctly but the output management fails
- Social exhaustion from sustained interaction — the processing cost is high even when (especially when) the engagement is meaningful
- Difficulty with rapid social inference in group contexts — one-on-one comprehension does not generalize to multi-person dynamics
- Camouflaging/masking — consciously performing social norms rather than executing them automatically, requiring constant cognitive load
- Mismatch between felt understanding and expressed understanding — knows what the person feels but cannot find the socially expected response

**Why this mimics low-EQ autism**:

| Behavior | Low-EQ autism cause | High-EQ autism cause |
|---|---|---|
| Social withdrawal | Lack of social interest or motivation | Social exhaustion from high-intensity processing; overload protection |
| Few close relationships | Difficulty forming emotional bonds | Difficulty sustaining the performance cost of relationships simultaneously |
| Appearing "cold" in group settings | Reduced affective response | Overwhelmed; shutdown; masking breakdown |
| Seeming not to notice social cues | Genuine ToM deficit | Noticing everything but unable to respond appropriately in real-time |
| Social skill deficits | Never acquired the underlying capacity | Acquired the capacity but cannot execute it under resource constraints |

From the outside, both profiles produce similar observable behaviors. They have opposite internal experiences and require opposite therapeutic approaches.

## 3. Prevalence Estimation

Precise prevalence of High-EQ Autism is difficult to establish because:
- Most autism research uses instruments that do not disaggregate cognitive and affective empathy
- Camouflaging makes many High-EQ autistic individuals undiagnosed or misdiagnosed
- The diagnostic criteria (DSM-5, ICD-11) do not distinguish subtypes on the empathy dimension

Estimates from available evidence:

**Base rate of autism**: ~2-3% of population (CDC 2023)

**Fraction with preserved/elevated AE**: Studies using AE-specific measures suggest 30-50% of autistic individuals have AE within or above the neurotypical range (Bird et al., 2010; Moriguchi et al., 2006; Dziobek et al., 2008).

**Broad Autism Phenotype with high emotional sensitivity**: The BAP (affecting ~20% of the general population with subclinical autistic traits) includes a substantial fraction of high-systemizing, high-sensitivity individuals who would score as High-EQ Autism if formally assessed.

**Conservative estimate**: 0.5-1.5% of the general population qualifies as High-EQ Autism (diagnosed or undiagnosed). Potentially 25-40% of formally diagnosed autistic individuals.

**Gender distribution**: Evidence strongly suggests High-EQ Autism is more prevalent in women and non-binary individuals, contributing to the historical underdiagnosis of autism in these groups (Lai et al., 2015; Bargiela et al., 2016). The "female autism phenotype" closely matches the High-EQ Autism profile.

## 4. Autistic Burnout as High-EQ Phenomenon

**Autistic burnout** — a state of chronic exhaustion, reduced function, and loss of previously managed skills following sustained demands — is particularly common in High-EQ Autism and provides strong convergent evidence for the profile's existence.

The burnout mechanism (Raymaker et al., 2020):
1. High-EQ autistic individual has genuine social-emotional capacities and genuine social motivation
2. Sustains social performance through conscious camouflaging (masking) over months or years
3. Masking is cognitively expensive even when underlying social understanding is intact
4. Cumulative cost exceeds resource capacity → burnout
5. In burnout: skills that appeared in high-function periods disappear; social withdrawal maximizes; looks like severe autism

The high-EQ individual is MORE vulnerable to burnout than the low-EQ individual precisely because their social motivation drives them to attempt more social performance at a higher cost. Low-EQ autism may produce consistent reduced social engagement; High-EQ Autism produces cycles of high engagement / burnout / withdrawal.

## 5. The TI Sigma Analysis

High-EQ Autism is a **Major Tralsity** (following URB #513) in the empathy domain:

The binary (high empathy / low empathy) fails. High-EQ Autism is TRALSE on the empathy axis:
- AE component: **TRUE** (high)
- CE real-time execution: **FALSE** (impaired under load)
- Self-attribution: **MR_PEND** (alexithymia blocks accurate self-report)
- Social outcomes: **TRALSE** (appear low-empathy externally while being high-empathy internally)

This TRALSE structure is the source of the distinctive suffering. The High-EQ autistic person knows others are suffering (AE intact) and cannot respond in the way that would help in the way they want to (CE execution impaired). This is not the suffering of indifference. It is the suffering of blocked caring.

The therapeutic implication: approaches designed for low-EQ autism (social skills training, ToM coaching) address the wrong target for High-EQ Autism. The target should be: **resource management** (reducing masking cost), **alexithymia treatment** (improving emotional self-recognition), and **burnout prevention** (sustainable social engagement scheduling) — not empathy development, which is already present.

## 6. Diagnostic and Clinical Implications

### 6.1 Misdiagnosis Risk

High-EQ Autism is frequently misdiagnosed as:
- Borderline Personality Disorder (high emotional sensitivity, relationship difficulty, identity confusion from masking)
- Social Anxiety Disorder (social avoidance, performance anxiety, exhaustion after social events)
- Depression (withdrawal, anhedonia — which in High-EQ autism is burnout, not primary depression)
- ADHD (executive function difficulties, emotional dysregulation)

These diagnoses address symptoms rather than causes. The treatment implications differ substantially.

### 6.2 Screening

A two-stage screen for High-EQ Autism:
1. Standard AQ screen (Baron-Cohen, 2001) — identifies autistic trait concentration
2. Empathy fragmentation assessment: separate AE and CE measures (e.g., Empathy Quotient with subscale analysis, Toronto Empathy Questionnaire alongside Baron-Cohen's Eyes Test)

Individuals scoring high on AQ AND high on AE measures are the High-EQ Autism target population for further assessment.

---

## 7. Summary

High-EQ Autism — elevated affective empathy combined with impaired real-time cognitive empathy execution — is a real, prevalent, and systematically underidentified profile within the autistic population. Conservative estimates suggest it constitutes 25-40% of diagnosed autism and 0.5-1.5% of the general population. It mimics low-EQ autism in observable outputs (social withdrawal, relationship difficulty, apparent emotional blunting) while having an opposite internal profile (high sensitivity, strong social motivation, intense emotional experience). The TI Sigma analysis: it is TRALSE on the empathy axis simultaneously. Its distinctive suffering is not indifference — it is blocked caring. Clinical, therapeutic, and social responses to autism that do not distinguish this profile will systematically misidentify and mistreat a large fraction of autistic individuals.
