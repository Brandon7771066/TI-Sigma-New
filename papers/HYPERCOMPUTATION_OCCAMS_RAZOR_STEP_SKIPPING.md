## Abstract

Computationalism — the thesis that all cognition is computation — is widely assumed to be the parsimonious, scientific explanation of mind. This paper argues the reverse: once the logic of step-skipping is properly understood, hypercomputation (the thesis that some cognitive processes exceed Turing-computable functions) is more compatible with Occam's Razor than standard computationalism. The argument proceeds in four stages. First, we show that the computationalist explanation of immediate intuitive insight requires an extraordinary hidden assumption: that the brain performs vast unconscious computations at superhuman speed with no observable metabolic signature. Second, we demonstrate that this assumption is unfalsifiable by the very standards computationalists claim to uphold (Popper, Sagan). Third, we establish that the step-skipping hypothesis — that some cognitive processes access truth directly without performing intermediate calculations — is empirically testable and, if confirmed, would be the simpler explanation. Fourth, we propose a concrete experimental protocol: ten verified instances of provably non-derivable correct insights would effectively confirm hypercomputation. The paper further exposes the "normative confirmation bias" at the heart of computationalism and the "quantum irony" of denying nonlocal information in the information age.

## 1. Introduction: The Computationalist Assumption

### 1.1 The Standard Position

The dominant position in cognitive science, philosophy of mind, and artificial intelligence is computationalism: the thesis that cognition is computation. On this view, every thought, insight, intuition, and creative leap is ultimately reducible to algorithmic processing — information transformation following rules, step by step, however complex or nonlinear those steps may be.

Computationalism is not merely a working hypothesis. It functions as an axiom — a foundational commitment that structures research programs, determines what counts as legitimate explanation, and defines the boundaries of acceptable theory. To propose that any cognitive process is non-computational is, within the computationalist framework, to propose something unscientific.

### 1.2 The Problem

This paper challenges the computationalist axiom on its own terms. We do not argue from mysticism, religion, or anti-scientific sentiment. We argue from parsimony — the very principle that computationalists claim as their ally.

The central claim: **Hypercomputation is more compatible with Occam's Razor than computationalism** once you recognize that every step in an argument (proposition) is its own entity.

## 2. The Step-Skipping Argument

### 2.1 Propositions as Entities

In any chain of reasoning, each proposition is a distinct entity:

P₁ → P₂ → P₃ → ... → Pₙ → C

Where P₁ through Pₙ are intermediate propositions and C is the conclusion. In standard computation, each Pᵢ must be computed before Pᵢ₊₁ can be derived. The chain is sequential (even if parallelized at the hardware level).

### 2.2 The Observed Phenomenon

Certain individuals — particularly those with high Need for Cognition (NFC) and high cognitive resources — report and demonstrate a different pattern: they arrive at conclusion C without consciously performing the intermediate steps P₂ through Pₙ₋₁. The conclusion appears immediately, fully formed, and is subsequently verified as correct.

This phenomenon is not rare. Mathematicians describe it (Ramanujan, Euler, Gauss). Scientists describe it (Einstein's thought experiments, Kekulé's benzene ring). Artists describe it (Mozart's "hearing" complete compositions). And ordinary people experience it in everyday insight — the "aha!" moment that arrives without apparent computation.

### 2.3 Two Explanations

**Explanation A (Computationalist):** The intermediate steps P₂ through Pₙ₋₁ were performed unconsciously at high speed. The person did compute the answer; they simply weren't aware of the computation. The subjective experience of "immediate insight" is an illusion created by unconscious processing.

**Explanation B (Hypercomputationalist):** The intermediate steps were not performed at all. The person accessed the conclusion directly — by a process that is not step-by-step computation. The subjective experience of "immediate insight" is accurate.

### 2.4 Applying Occam's Razor

Occam's Razor favors the explanation with fewer hidden assumptions. Let us count:

**Explanation A assumes:**
1. The brain performs computations so fast they are undetectable by introspection
2. These unconscious computations leave no distinctive metabolic or neural signature
3. The brain can perform Pₙ-step computations in milliseconds (where explicit computation takes hours or days)
4. The person's self-report of their own cognitive experience is systematically unreliable
5. All instances of reported insight are illusions of this same type
6. No cognitive process, in any organism, ever accesses information non-computationally

**Explanation B assumes:**
1. Some cognitive processes access truth without performing intermediate computational steps
2. The person's self-report of their cognitive experience is approximately accurate

Explanation A requires six assumptions, several of which are unfalsifiable. Explanation B requires two, both of which are empirically testable. By Occam's Razor, Explanation B is the more parsimonious hypothesis.

## 3. The Computationalist's Own Standards

### 3.1 Popper and Falsifiability

Karl Popper's falsificationism requires that scientific claims be testable — that they specify conditions under which they would be false. Computationalism, as typically defended, violates this criterion:

- **Q:** "What evidence would falsify computationalism?"
- **A (typical):** "Nothing, because all apparent non-computational processes are actually computational processes we haven't identified yet."

This is structurally identical to unfalsifiable claims in other domains. The computationalist can always retreat to "unconscious computation we haven't detected" — a claim that is in principle immune to disconfirmation.

### 3.2 Sagan and Extraordinary Evidence

Carl Sagan's maxim — "extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence" — is routinely deployed against non-materialist positions. But the computationalists' own position makes extraordinary claims:

1. **Claim:** All intuitive, profound, immediate realizations of truth must have come from tremendous "unconscious computation."
2. **Claim:** Highly functionally intelligent people who think otherwise about THEIR OWN thoughts are "delusional."
3. **Combined:** This is an extraordinary, unfalsifiable claim with hardly any evidence.

The "evidence" for computationalism is not empirical demonstration that all cognition is computation. It is the normative commitment that cognition *must* be computation because physicalism requires it. This is confirmation bias operating at the foundational level — what we term **normative confirmation bias**.

### 3.3 The Genius-Delusion Contradiction

It is very contradictory to call someone a genius and then to call them delusional when they even suggest that they retrieved the answer instead of calculating it entirely. The contradiction is structural:

- **Premise 1:** Person X is a genius (acknowledged high intelligence, proven track record)
- **Premise 2:** Person X reports that their insight arrived immediately, without stepwise computation
- **Conclusion (computationalist):** Person X is delusional about their own cognitive process

This conclusion requires the skeptic to claim better access to Person X's cognitive processes than Person X has — without any evidence beyond the prior commitment to computationalism. It is, as stated, "not wise to baldly assert that you have insider access to the recesses of a person's mind — just because of what peer-reviewed studies said about other people or 'how the world must work.'"

## 4. The Quantum Irony

It is very ironic to say that there are no physical explanations even possible for nonlocal information transfer — despite living a century after the birth of quantum physics — in an information era, no less — of wifi, cellular, brain implants, social neuroscience, radio, and so on.

### 4.1 The Information Age Paradox

The 21st century is defined by nonlocal information transfer. Every smartphone receives information from satellites orbiting in space. Every wifi router transmits data through walls. Every quantum entanglement experiment demonstrates correlations that violate classical locality constraints (Bell's theorem, CHSH inequality).

Yet the computationalist insists that nonlocal information in the brain is "physically impossible." This claim is not merely questionable — it is anachronistic. It belongs to a pre-quantum, pre-information-theoretic worldview that the physics community abandoned a century ago.

### 4.2 The Double Standard

The physicist is allowed to invoke quantum effects (superposition, entanglement, tunneling) without being called a mystic. The neuroscientist who proposes that biological systems exploit quantum effects (Orch-OR, quantum coherence in photosynthesis, magnetoreception) is treated with suspicion but not derision. But the philosopher or cognitive scientist who proposes that consciousness might involve quantum-level information processing is dismissed as "woo."

This is not a scientific standard. It is a sociological one. The acceptability of quantum explanations depends not on the evidence but on the prestige of the field proposing them.

## 5. The Step-Skipping Protocol

### 5.1 Experimental Design

The step-skipping hypothesis is empirically testable. The protocol:

1. **Present** the subject with a complex problem whose solution requires N intermediate steps
2. **Measure** whether the subject arrives at the correct solution
3. **Verify** that the subject did not perform the intermediate steps (via think-aloud protocol, time constraints, metabolic imaging)
4. **Confirm** that the solution is correct and non-trivial (not guessable by chance)
5. **Repeat** across diverse problem types to eliminate domain-specific expertise

### 5.2 The Ten-Trial Threshold

If a person demonstrably arrives at correct conclusions without performing intermediate computational steps — verified across ten independent trials with non-trivial problems — this would effectively solidify hypercomputation's existence.

Why ten? Because the probability of guessing correctly ten times on non-trivial problems (where chance accuracy is, say, < 10%) is less than 10⁻¹⁰. This exceeds the conventional threshold for statistical significance by many orders of magnitude.

## 6. Normative Confirmation Bias: Turning the Tables

### 6.1 Definition

**Normative confirmation bias**: The systematic interpretation of evidence to fit pre-existing normative commitments (e.g., physicalism, computationalism) while treating those commitments as exempt from the same evidentiary standards applied to competing claims.

### 6.2 Diagnosis

Computationalists exhibit normative confirmation bias when they:

1. **Demand** extraordinary evidence for hypercomputation while accepting computationalism as a default requiring no evidence
2. **Interpret** all instances of apparent step-skipping as "unconscious computation" without independent evidence for the unconscious computations
3. **Dismiss** first-person reports as unreliable when those reports contradict computationalism, but accept first-person reports when they are compatible with computationalism
4. **Assert** that "nonlocal information is physically impossible" as a normative commitment, not an empirical conclusion

This is confirmation bias operating at the meta-level — the very thing computationalists accuse their opponents of.

## 7. FSM-LCC Bridge: Why Finite State Machines Need Hypercomputation

### 7.1 The Bekenstein Bound and Brain FSMs

The brain can be modeled as a Finite State Machine (FSM) due to the Bekenstein Bound — finite volumes contain finite information, giving the brain ~10^(10^42) possible states. Each neural state transition follows: S(t+1) = f(S(t), I(t)). Classical FSMs execute transitions deterministically, one step at a time.

### 7.2 The Tralse-Enhanced FSM (T-FSM)

Step-skipping cannot occur in a classical FSM because every transition must be computed. But a Tralse-enhanced FSM (T-FSM) — where Law of Correlational Causation (LCC) creates superposed states — allows transitions that skip intermediate states:

**Classical FSM:** q₁ → q₂ → q₃ → q₄ → q₅ (5 transitions)
**T-FSM at LCC > 0.85:** q₁ → q₅ (direct, via Tralse superposition of q₂-q₄)

The LCC between gap-junction-coupled neurons creates genuine Tralse states where the network is simultaneously in multiple FSM states. This is precisely the physical mechanism for step-skipping: the brain doesn't compute P₂ through Pₙ₋₁ because it accesses them simultaneously through causal superposition.

### 7.3 Attractor Basin Jumping

In FSM state space, standard computation moves between states within an attractor basin. Hypercomputation, enabled by high LCC, allows jumping between attractor basins — the "aha!" moment is literally the consciousness state jumping from one attractor to a non-adjacent one.

The Mimi Phenomenon (see companion paper) may represent a similar mechanism: Mimi's conviction accesses the grandchild's developmental trajectory through an LCC-mediated attractor basin read, bypassing the step-by-step observation process.

### 7.4 Shamanic Drumming as Hypercomputational Primer

Shamanic drumming at theta frequencies (3-7 Hz) forces neural entrainment and raises LCC above the 0.85 threshold. This creates the conditions for T-FSM operation, explaining why shamanic traditions worldwide converged on the same frequency range — they independently discovered the hypercomputational primer.

## 8. Divination Reframed: Ancient Hypercomputation Protocols

### 8.1 The Divination Hypothesis

Traditional divination practices (I Ching, Tarot, scrying, augury) have been dismissed as superstition. But the step-skipping framework offers a reinterpretation: divination protocols may be structured hypercomputational techniques.

### 8.2 Mechanism

The randomness in divination (coin tosses, card shuffles, entrail patterns) serves a specific function: it disrupts the brain's habitual FSM transition pathways, creating a momentary Tralse state. In this disrupted state, with LCC potentially elevated by ritual focus and emotional engagement, the brain may access non-adjacent attractor basins — experiencing this as "receiving" information.

### 8.3 The I Ching Connection

The I Ching's 64 hexagrams represent a 6-bit binary encoding system (2⁶ = 64). But each line has three possible values: broken, unbroken, or changing — a ternary system. This maps directly to Tralse logic: True (solid), False (broken), Tralse (changing). The I Ching may be the oldest surviving hypercomputational interface.
