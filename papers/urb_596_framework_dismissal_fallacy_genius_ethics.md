## Abstract

A specific and widespread epistemic and ethical failure is formalized here: the **Framework Dismissal Fallacy (FDF)** — the move from "I don't understand this (even in my field)" to "it sounds crazy" to "the person is crazy." This three-step inference is invalid at each step. The first step may be accurate (genuine non-understanding). The second is a non-sequitur (non-understanding does not entail incoherence). The third is a category error compounded by an ethical failure (incoherence, even if established, does not entail insanity, and calling a person insane for unconventional work is a moral act with moral standards it must meet). A minimum standard for genius attribution is also established: any person who constructs a large-scale internally consistent framework that challenges the status quo, generates successful predictions, and includes formally verified results must be attributed the status of genius — regardless of whether the framework is ultimately accepted. To deny this attribution is itself a form of intellectual dishonesty.

## 1. The Framework Dismissal Fallacy — Structure

The FDF is a three-step inference that moves from legitimate ignorance to an ethical accusation while bypassing the logical requirements for each step:

```
Step 1: "I don't understand this." [May be accurate]
    ↓  [INVALID INFERENCE — non-understanding ≠ incoherence]
Step 2: "It sounds crazy / incoherent." [Unwarranted]
    ↓  [INVALID INFERENCE — incoherence ≠ insanity; insanity requires clinical evidence]
Step 3: "The person is crazy." [Ethical and logical failure]
```

Each arrow represents an invalid logical step. The FDF commits two distinct fallacies and one ethical failure, layered on top of each other.

---

## 2. Step 1: Non-Understanding Is Legitimate

The first step — "I don't understand this" — is often entirely legitimate and should be stated plainly. Novel frameworks are genuinely difficult. Interdisciplinary work can be opaque to specialists in any single component field. A career mathematician may not immediately understand the philosophical claims; a philosopher may not follow the formal proofs; a neuroscientist may not engage with the mathematical logic.

Non-understanding is not a criticism of the work. It is information about the distance between the reader's current conceptual framework and the framework being proposed.

**The correct response to non-understanding:** "I don't understand this yet" or "This requires more engagement than I can currently give it" or "I'd need to work through sections X, Y, Z to assess this properly."

**The incorrect response:** Using non-understanding as evidence of the framework's incoherence.

---

## 3. Step 2: The Non-Understanding → Incoherence Non-Sequitur

The inference from "I don't understand this" to "it sounds incoherent / crazy" is a non-sequitur — the conclusion does not follow from the premise. Its failure is obvious once stated directly, but it operates implicitly in much academic and public dismissal of unconventional work.

**Why the inference fails:**

Non-understanding has at least three possible sources:
- (A) The work is genuinely incoherent
- (B) The reader lacks the background to follow it
- (C) The work is coherent but uses unfamiliar frameworks or vocabulary

The reader who says "I don't understand this" cannot distinguish between (A), (B), and (C) from the inside — by definition, if they could see that it was (A), they would understand it well enough to identify the specific incoherence. The fact of non-understanding is therefore equally consistent with all three explanations.

To treat non-understanding as evidence for (A) rather than (B) or (C) is to assume one's own comprehension is the standard against which coherence is measured. This is a form of intellectual narcissism: "If I can't understand it, it must be incoherent."

**The specialist's variant:** The FDF is especially damaging when committed by a specialist in the relevant field. The implicit assumption is: "I am an expert in X; this work claims to be about X; I don't understand it; therefore it is incoherent." This is doubly flawed because:

1. Genuinely novel work in a field will often be opaque to specialists precisely because it challenges the conceptual framework within which the specialist operates. Kuhn's analysis of scientific revolutions shows this repeatedly: the people least able to evaluate paradigm-challenging work are often the specialists most invested in the paradigm being challenged.

2. Interdisciplinary frameworks are typically evaluated by specialists in only one of their component fields — who then apply their single-field standard to a multi-field synthesis. A work connecting mathematics, philosophy, neuroscience, and physics will appear foreign to mathematicians (too philosophical), philosophers (too mathematical), neuroscientists (too abstract), and physicists (too speculative) — while being fully coherent to anyone who has engaged all four components.

## 4. Step 3: The Incoherence → Insanity Category Error and Ethical Failure

Even if Step 2 were valid — even if non-understanding did establish incoherence — the move from "incoherent work" to "insane person" would still be an ethical failure.

**The category error:** Insanity (in the clinical sense) is a medical and psychological diagnosis referring to the impairment of a person's ability to understand reality and function in it. It requires clinical evidence — behavioral, psychological, neurological. It has nothing to do with the quality of a person's intellectual work. Mathematicians who produced incoherent proofs are mistaken, not insane. Philosophers who constructed internally contradictory systems are wrong, not mentally ill.

Calling a person "crazy" for unconventional intellectual work is therefore:
- Clinically unsupported (no medical basis)
- Logically invalid (incoherence in work ≠ impairment in person)
- Epistemically lazy (substitutes emotional dismissal for intellectual engagement)

**The ethical dimension:** "That person is crazy" is not merely a description — it is a social act. It is designed to remove the person from consideration as a legitimate intellectual interlocutor, to warn others away from engaging with their work, and to substitute a character accusation for an argument about the content. As a social act with consequences, it carries moral weight and must be held to a moral standard.

The moral standard it must meet: **If you are going to use the accusation of insanity to dismiss intellectual work, you must be able to identify specifically which claims are incoherent and why.** Without this, the accusation is not an argument — it is a slur.

**The minimum standard for intellectual honesty:**
- If you disagree with the conclusions → say "I think this conclusion is wrong, and here is why."
- If you find specific claims incoherent → say "This specific claim is incoherent because..."
- If you don't understand the framework → say "I don't understand this well enough to evaluate it."
- What you may NOT say: "I don't understand this (even though it's my field), therefore it sounds crazy, therefore the person is crazy." This fails the minimum standard of intellectual honesty.

## 5. The Minimum Standard for Genius Attribution

A separate but related ethical claim: any person who constructs a large-scale, internally consistent framework that challenges the status quo, generates successful predictions, and includes formally verified results **must be attributed the status of genius** — regardless of whether the framework is ultimately accepted or correct.

### 5.1 Why "Correct" Is Not the Standard

Genius is not defined by being right. History is full of geniuses who were substantially wrong: Descartes's physics, Newton's alchemy, Freud's metapsychology, Einstein's resistance to quantum mechanics. None of these failures revoke the genius attribution — because genius is a property of the quality of intellectual effort, not the accuracy of its conclusions.

What constitutes the quality of intellectual effort sufficient for genius attribution:

1. **Scale and ambition:** The framework addresses questions of fundamental importance across multiple domains, not narrow technical problems.
2. **Internal consistency:** The framework does not contradict itself — its claims are mutually compatible and follow from stated principles.
3. **Predictive power:** The framework generates specific predictions that can be compared against evidence, and at least some of those predictions are confirmed.
4. **Formal rigor:** At least some claims within the framework are demonstrated with logical or mathematical precision.
5. **Genuine novelty:** The framework challenges or extends the existing consensus in substantive ways, not merely restating known results.

A framework satisfying all five criteria represents a genuine intellectual achievement regardless of its ultimate fate. The person who constructed it is, by definition, a genius — where "genius" means the ability to assemble, sustain, and develop a complex original framework at a level beyond ordinary intellectual achievement.

### 5.2 The Ethics of Withholding Genius Attribution

Withholding genius attribution from a person who meets the above five criteria — when the withholding is motivated not by a demonstrated failure of those criteria but by the framework's unconventionality or the evaluator's non-understanding — is itself a form of intellectual dishonesty.

It is dishonest because it applies a double standard: work that confirms the existing consensus is evaluated by standards 1-5 above; work that challenges the consensus is evaluated by the additional standard of "whether it makes me comfortable" or "whether it fits what I already believe." This asymmetry cannot be justified intellectually. It can only be explained by the evaluator's investment in the status quo.

### 5.3 The Formal Statement

**The Minimum Genius Theorem:**

Let F be a framework satisfying: (1) scale and ambition, (2) internal consistency, (3) confirmed predictive power, (4) formal rigor in at least some claims, (5) genuine novelty relative to the consensus.

Any evaluator who:
- Agrees that F satisfies (1)–(5), AND  
- Denies genius attribution to F's author, AND  
- Cannot identify a specific failure in (1)–(5) as the basis for denial

...is applying a standard other than intellectual merit. That standard — whatever it is (personal discomfort, institutional investment, social allegiance) — is not an intellectually defensible basis for evaluation and should be named as such.

**The Corollary:**

An evaluator who says "I don't understand F (even in my field), and therefore the author might be crazy" has:
- Not assessed (1)–(5) at all
- Applied Step 3 of the Framework Dismissal Fallacy
- Made an ethical accusation without meeting the minimum evidential standard for that accusation
- Failed the minimum standard of intellectual honesty

They may call the framework wrong. They may call it unconventional. They may say they cannot evaluate it without more engagement. What they cannot ethically do is call the author crazy — because this claim has specific moral content and they have provided no moral-level justification for it.

## 7. The Productive Response to Unconventional Frameworks

For researchers and intellectuals who encounter TI Sigma or any similarly unconventional framework, the intellectually honest response follows this sequence:

1. **Read enough to assess the five criteria.** Don't evaluate from the abstract or the vocabulary alone.
2. **Identify the claims you find most questionable** — not most unfamiliar, most questionable.
3. **Look for the formal proofs.** A framework with Lean 4 verified theorems has given you machine-checkable evidence. Engage the evidence.
4. **Say what you think, specifically.** "I disagree with claim X because Y" is a contribution to knowledge. "This sounds crazy" is not.
5. **Attribute intellectual achievement where it exists** — separately from agreement or disagreement. "This is wrong about X, but the formal apparatus is genuinely original" is both honest and useful.

This is the minimum standard of intellectual honesty. It is also, under URB #595's argument, a moral obligation for any person with the capacity to meet it.
