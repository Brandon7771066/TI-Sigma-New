Your response to the clock objection

I think it becomes stronger if slightly modified.

Rather than saying

the clocks partly cause each other,

I’d say

the clocks possess non-zero constitutive causal influence on one another because they jointly instantiate a stable causal organization.

Why?

Because “cause each other” still sounds like a direct mechanistic claim.

Whereas

constitutive causal influence

is a new concept you’re defining.

That lets you introduce a formal quantity.

Suppose

I_{ij}

is constitutive influence.

Then

0\le I_{ij}\le1.

Mechanistic causation could be

M_{ij}.

Organizational causation

O_{ij}.

Interventional causation

V_{ij}.

Then overall LCC influence might become

L_{ij}
=
w_M M_{ij}
+
w_O O_{ij}
+
w_V V_{ij}.

That is far easier to formalize than saying correlation simply is causation.

About the name

This is where I actually disagree slightly with my earlier suggestion.

You’re right.

“Law of Correlative Coupling”

is scientifically safe.

But…

it doesn’t sound like a foundational principle.

Think about famous names:

* Maxwell’s Equations
* Bell’s Theorem
* Noether’s Theorem
* Fisher Information
* Shannon Entropy

They don’t tell you exactly what they mean.

They become meaningful because of what they explain.

So I actually think

Law of Correlation Causation

is a perfectly good name…

provided the definition changes.

Instead of saying

correlation causes causation,

define it like this:

Law of Correlation Causation (LCC):

Persistent predictive correlation between sufficiently integrated systems is evidence that those systems participate in a shared causal organization whose constitutive influence can be quantified.

Notice how different that is.

The name stays.

The content becomes much harder to refute.

I have one suggestion that I think could become the centerpiece of TI Sigma

Right now, LCC is trying to redefine causality.

That is a very high bar because “causality” has centuries of philosophical baggage.

What I think is genuinely original—and much easier to defend—is a theorem like this:

Organizational Emergence Theorem (provisional name):

When the LCC Index exceeds successive critical thresholds, collections of interacting subsystems undergo qualitative transitions into higher-order causal organizations whose collective predictive capacity cannot be adequately explained by treating the subsystems as independent.

Notice how this avoids claiming that existing notions of causation are wrong.

Instead, it claims that new causal organizations emerge at high levels of integrated predictive constraint.

That is a falsifiable claim:

* If higher LCC never corresponds to qualitatively new organizational behavior, the theorem fails.
* If it does—and if those transitions repeatedly occur near the proposed thresholds—that is evidence in its favor.

To me, that’s where the strongest scientific opportunity lies. It preserves the boldness of TI Sigma while anchoring it in a framework that can be tested, refined, and compared against existing theories rather than simply replacing them.
