You’re absolutely right: “insult” carries moral baggage that prejudges the act as vicious.
“Rebuke” or “censure” immediately reframes it as moral speech aimed at correction.

That semantic shift alone clarifies your project.

You’re not defending cruelty.
You’re defending moral enforcement through speech.

That’s a very different thing.

⸻

Step 1: Empirical Taxonomy of What We Call “Insults”

If we want red lines, we first need categories.

Most things labeled “insults” fall into a few empirically distinguishable types:

Category A: Identity Attacks
	•	“You’re worthless.”
	•	Slurs.
	•	Attacks on race, gender, disability, etc.
	•	Global condemnation of the person.

Function: Domination, exclusion, dehumanization.
Effect: High trauma risk, low corrective value.

→ These almost certainly fail your “nontraumatic restoration” criterion.

⸻

Category B: Character Condemnation
	•	“You’re dishonest.”
	•	“You’re lazy.”
	•	“You’re manipulative.”

Function: Moral labeling.
Effect: Can be corrective if tied to behavior, corrosive if global.

These become legitimate rebuke only when:
	•	Specific.
	•	Evidence-linked.
	•	Behavior-targeted.
	•	Revocable.

⸻

Category C: Behavioral Rebuke
	•	“That was cruel.”
	•	“You lied.”
	•	“You’re dodging responsibility.”

Function: Boundary enforcement.
Effect: Often productive if proportionate.

This is closest to your ideal JI.

⸻

Category D: Status Undermining / Mockery
	•	Sarcasm.
	•	Ridicule.
	•	Public shaming.
	•	Humor at someone’s expense.

Function: Ego destabilization.
Effect: Mixed — powerful leveling tool, but volatile.

In small groups, ridicule deters dominance.
In mass systems, it can spiral.

⸻

Category E: Gossip-Based Moral Signaling
	•	“He cheats people.”
	•	“She lies.”
	•	Reputation warnings.

Function: Information sharing + norm enforcement.
Effect: Highly efficient in tight communities, dangerous in viral ones.

⸻

Step 2: What to Keep vs. Reject

If we filter by your criteria (restoration, nontrauma, proportionality, IC-aligned intent):

Reject Completely:
	•	Identity annihilation.
	•	Slurs.
	•	Irreversible reputational branding.
	•	Exaggeration beyond evidence.
	•	Dogpiling.

Conditional (Use with High Caution):
	•	Public ridicule.
	•	Reputation sharing beyond local scope.
	•	Psychological destabilization tactics.

Keep / Legitimize:
	•	Direct behavioral rebuke.
	•	Character criticism tied to pattern and evidence.
	•	Proportionate, specific censure.
	•	Private correction escalating to public only if harm persists.

That already sketches red lines.

⸻

Step 3: Rough Estimate — How Often Should Rebuke Occur?

Now we get empirical and pragmatic.

In a functioning, healthy social system:
	•	Minor norm violations happen daily.
	•	Serious moral violations are rarer.
	•	Chronic ego rigidity is rarer still.

If rebuke becomes constant, it ceases to signal severity.

Anthropological studies of small-scale societies suggest:
	•	Gossip is frequent.
	•	Direct confrontation is less frequent.
	•	Severe public shaming is rare and escalatory.

A rough heuristic:
	•	Light corrective feedback: common (daily-scale).
	•	Sharp rebuke: occasional (when harm persists).
	•	Public censure: rare and reserved for repeated or serious violations.

If “sharp rebuke” happens more than, say, 10–20% of significant disagreements, you likely have overuse.

If it happens near 0%, norms decay.

You’re aiming for a middle zone.

⸻

Step 4: Proposed Hard Red Lines for Your JI Constitution

Here’s a draft:
	1.	No identity-based condemnation.
	2.	No irreversible reputational branding.
	3.	No mob amplification.
	4.	Must target behavior, not existence.
	5.	Must allow reintegration.
	6.	Must stop once order is restored.
	7.	Must remain open to evidence reversal.
	8.	Must be proportionate to harm.

These don’t require Kantian universalization.
They’re pragmatic safeguards.

⸻

Step 5: Reframing the Whole Project

If you rename your framework:
	•	From: Justified Insult (JI)
	•	To: Ethical Rebuke (ER) or Restorative Censure (RC)

The philosophy becomes much more defensible.

Because what you’re really arguing is:

A society that forbids sharp moral speech loses a key informal enforcement mechanism and drifts toward norm decay.

That’s arguable.
