1) IC-as-a-state is plausible — but you need a state-diagnostic that’s not just confidence

You’re right: requiring “default IC” would make JI practically impossible for almost everyone. And you explicitly argue open-mindedness is a trait architecture rather than a momentary posture.  ￼

So the key is: how do we recognize a temporary IC episode without turning it into “confidence with vibes”?

A non-rigid, pragmatic diagnostic (minutes, not years) could be:

IC-state markers (fast, behavioral)

A “JI-ready” state looks like:
	•	Precision: You can state the violation/harm clearly (not morally foggy).
	•	Update-readiness: You can name what evidence would change your view right now.
	•	Ego-lightness: If you’re shown wrong, your felt response is closer to relief than humiliation/rage. (Your paper contrasts this strongly with the dark imitation.  ￼)
	•	Stop-ability: You can stop after the boundary is enforced (no “victory laps”).
	•	Integration instinct: You spontaneously include an off-ramp (“Drop the act and we’re fine.” “Own it and we move forward.”)

Those don’t require five years of data, and they match your insistence that metacognition quality shows up in the action.

2) “Proof is in the pudding” works — but only if the pudding is visible and local

You’re making an important epistemic claim:

We don’t need to know the mechanism (PSI vs expertise). We just need to see consistent results.

That’s fair. It’s also how humans actually allocate trust.

But it only works if the “results” are:
	•	visible enough to be checked
	•	domain-bounded
	•	not manufactured by PR, status, or echo chambers

This matters because in online life, “performance” can look like accuracy.

So the needed refinement is:

Domain-bounded authority

Someone can be “JI-licensed” only inside the domain their pudding demonstrates.

Judge Judy can read human conflict patterns.
Holzhauer can do game-theoretic betting.
That doesn’t automatically give them moral authority over someone’s marriage or metaphysics.

Your paper itself notes domain specificity.  ￼

That single line prevents a lot of tyranny.

3) The central economic question you posed is the right one

You’re basically doing policy analysis:

Is tolerating virtuous insults + informal punishment better than the current baseline?

That’s exactly how to frame it.

You’re also right that “some abuse will happen” is not a knockdown objection; it’s true of any regime.

So the comparison set is:

Regime A (current):
	•	weak informal enforcement
	•	strong formal enforcement (but slow, expensive, blunt)
	•	reputational catastrophes via viral shame without cultural buffering
	•	low catharsis + high simmering resentment

Regime B (yours):
	•	stronger informal enforcement
	•	cultural buffering (people expect criticism/insults)
	•	cheaper deterrence
	•	higher catharsis + faster boundary enforcement

The question is: does B reduce net harm, or just redistribute it?

That’s testable in principle, at least at community scale.

4) Where your “snowflake buffer” idea is strongest

You’re saying: if insults were normalized, they would be less traumatic.

That’s psychologically plausible in some range.

In a culture where:
	•	criticism is common
	•	humor is normal
	•	reputational hits are reversible
	•	reintegration is expected

people become more resilient.

This is basically “vaccination logic” for ego: controlled exposure builds tolerance.

But it only works if there are strict taboos against:
	•	identity annihilation (“you should die,” “you’re nothing,” etc.)
	•	dogpiling
	•	permanent branding
	•	mass amplification

Otherwise resilience training becomes “learn to endure cruelty,” which is a different thing.

So the buffer idea needs one explicit clause:

Normalize bounded insult, not unlimited humiliation.
