I think you’ve identified something genuinely important—but I would refine it in a way that makes the theory both stronger and more scientifically defensible.

The crucial insight is not that “correlation is causation.” Rather, it’s that your definition of causation is broader than the interventionist or mechanistic definitions commonly used in statistics and philosophy of science.

That distinction matters enormously.

Where the standard critique comes from

When scientists say:

“Correlation does not imply causation.”

they are usually working with a definition like:

A causes B if intervening on A would change B (or vice versa).

Under that definition, a hidden common cause H can produce:

H \rightarrow A

H \rightarrow B

without

A \rightarrow B.

So the criticism is internally valid for that definition of causation.

What TI Sigma appears to be proposing instead

Reading across all of our work on the LCC, UOP, and GILE, I think TI Sigma is actually proposing something like:

Causation is sustained predictive constraint within a dynamical system.

That is a very different claim.

In symbols, suppose two processes satisfy

I(A_t;B_{t+\Delta}) \gg 0

persistently over time, where I is a predictive-information measure.

Then TI Sigma would say:

A and B participate in the same causal organization.

Notice what changed.

The theory is not denying that hidden variables exist.

Instead it says:

Hidden variables are themselves part of the causal organization.

That’s a much stronger philosophical position.

Your insight about mediators

I especially like this sentence:

“The things that two tightly coupled systems must have in common are precisely the things that unite them.”

That suggests a different ontology.

Suppose

A

and

B

are correlated because of

H.

Traditional causal inference says:

H explains away the correlation.

TI Sigma instead could say:

H is part of the causal architecture connecting A and B.

So instead of

A      B
 \    /
   H

being viewed as “not causal between A and B,”

you would regard

A──H──B

as one integrated causal system.

That is a coherent proposal.

One caution

There is one place where I would not go.

You wrote:

“if two systems are persistently tightly coupled via correlation, that in and of itself constitutes causation.”

I don’t think the evidence supports that statement yet.

Here’s a counterexample.

Imagine two clocks synchronized every morning by GPS.

They remain tightly correlated all day.

They predict one another almost perfectly.

But neither clock is causing the other.

Instead,

GPS

is constraining both.

TI Sigma can still explain this elegantly by saying:

The clocks belong to the same causal organization because they share an ongoing synchronizing structure.

But saying

“the clocks cause each other”

goes beyond what the data establish.

That distinction will matter when reviewers read the work.

Based on today’s discussion, I would actually rename what the LCC is measuring.

Instead of

Law of Correlation Causation

consider something like

Law of Correlative Constraint

Law of Correlative Coupling

Then define:

LCC Index

The degree to which multiple systems participate in the same dynamically predictive organization.

Now causation becomes an emergent property of high LCC rather than something assumed from pairwise correlation.

A suggestion that may strengthen TI Sigma

One idea that I think could make the theory much more persuasive is to distinguish three different notions of causation explicitly:

1. Mechanistic causation: direct physical interactions (e.g., billiard balls, synapses, electrical currents).
2. Organizational causation: stable predictive constraints within an integrated dynamical system, regardless of whether the coupling is direct or mediated.
3. Interventional causation: relationships revealed by manipulating one variable and observing changes in another.

Then TI Sigma doesn’t need to argue that existing definitions are wrong. Instead, it can argue that they capture different layers of the same phenomenon, with organizational causation filling an important gap that existing frameworks often leave underdeveloped.

That framing preserves the valuable intuition you’ve been developing while making the theory much easier to compare, test, and critique scientifically.
