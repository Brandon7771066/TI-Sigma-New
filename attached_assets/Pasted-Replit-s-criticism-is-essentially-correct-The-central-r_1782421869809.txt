Replit’s criticism is essentially correct.

The central result in FCF-1 is mathematically valid, but it is a wrapper theorem, not a theorem that discovers mathematical truth. As your own document states:

* G is defined from the proof checker’s verdict.
* Since J is monotonic in G, maximizing J simply returns the action with the largest checker-derived G.
* Therefore the UOP is just re-expressing the checker’s answer.  

That is why it is called “tautological” (or more precisely, definitionally true): once you define G using the checker, the theorem follows almost immediately.

I actually think your document identifies the important point itself. It says the real open problem is:

Find a non-trivial fidelity map that does not derive G from a proof checker or equivalent oracle.  

I agree completely.

Where I think the research becomes interesting

Rather than asking

“Can UOP prove RH?”

ask

“Can UOP predict or constrain mathematical truth before a proof exists?”

Those are very different.

Instead of

G = \text{checker verdict},

you need something like

G = F(\text{intrinsic mathematical structure}),

where F depends only on features of the problem itself.

Examples might include:

* proof complexity
* symmetry
* invariance
* compression/minimum description length
* algebraic consistency
* analytic continuation properties
* structural stability
* logical coherence measures

Notice none of those assumes you already know the answer.

Then the theorem would become something like

\arg\max J(F(P))

rather than

\arg\max J(\text{checker}(P)).

That would no longer be tautological.

I think there are three major milestones

1. Structural Fidelity Theorem

Show that G can be computed solely from intrinsic mathematical structure—not from a proof or oracle.

This is, in my view, the biggest missing theorem.

2. Soundness Theorem

Prove that whenever the UOP chooses “True,” the statement is in fact true.

This is much harder.

3. Completeness Theorem

Prove that every true statement eventually receives the highest score.

This is even stronger.

If you could establish all three for a meaningful class of mathematical statements, that would be a significant theoretical result.

One caution

You should be careful not to claim “all problems in a class” too early. Results like the Halting Problem show that there are unavoidable limits on what any algorithm can decide over sufficiently broad classes of problems. So you’ll likely need to define a restricted class (for example, a family of optimization problems or a family of formal conjectures with particular structural properties) where your assumptions can actually be proved.

My recommendation

I would set RH aside for now.

Instead, I would spend the effort on proving a theorem like:

Structural Fidelity Conjecture (SFC-1): There exists a computable mapping F from problems in class \mathcal C to UOP truth-support values such that F depends only on the intrinsic structure of the problem, not on any proof oracle or proof-checker.

That is a much more foundational objective. If such an F could be rigorously defined and shown to have useful predictive or optimality properties, it would eliminate the tautology objection and give you a genuinely new mathematical object to study.
