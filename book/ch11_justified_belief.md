## Chapter 11: Dropping the T — Justified Belief as Knowledge (JBK-1)

### The oldest definition, and its cracked condition

Coherentism (Chapter 10) forces a rethink of the oldest definition in the theory of knowledge. For two millennia the going analysis was **justified true belief (JTB)**: you *know* that p when you believe p, you are justified in believing it, and p is true. (Plato's *Theaetetus* floats it; the twentieth century made it the textbook account.) In 1963 Edmund Gettier famously showed all three conditions can be satisfied while we would still deny it is knowledge — a justified belief that happens to be true by luck. Sixty years of increasingly elaborate patches followed. The framework offers a more radical diagnosis: the culprit is the **T**.

Start by noticing that "truth" in JTB quietly names two very different things:

- **Justified truth** — a claim well-supported by the web of reasons and evidence available to us. This we can inspect, argue over, and actually possess.
- **Metaphysical truth** — correspondence to reality-in-itself, whether or not anyone could ever tell. This, by its own definition, we can never directly access; we never step outside all our beliefs to hold one up against bare reality.

### Two moves that finish the argument

**First, the T is unreachable**, so requiring it adds a condition we can never verify we have met. Everything we actually *do* to certify knowledge — check the reasons, weigh the evidence, test the coherence — certifies *justification*. Metaphysical truth is not a further box we ever get to tick. **Second, where metaphysical truth does hold, it holds regardless of justification** — a true belief is true whether or not you had good reasons, and an unjustified lucky guess that happens to correspond is still, metaphysically, true. So T floats entirely free of J.

Put those together and JTB welds two conditions that never meet on the same plane: one we can access but that does not guarantee correspondence (J), and one that guarantees correspondence but that we can never access (T). The definition **muddles two theories of truth** — a pragmatic/epistemic one and a metaphysical/correspondence one — and demands their conjunction as though it were a single property. That confusion, the framework argues, is the real engine of the Gettier mess.

Hence the replacement: **JBK-1 — Justified-Belief-as-Knowledge** **(framework-internal, candidate)**. Knowledge, as anything we can *aim for or claim to possess*, simply is **justified belief**. Drop the T — not because truth does not matter, but because the accessible half of it already lives inside J, and the inaccessible half was never ours to add in the first place.

### Tralse knowledge, and the factivity objection

The framework embraces a striking consequence rather than dodging it. A belief that was properly justified but turns out metaphysically false was still **knowledge** in the only sense we ever had — **tralse knowledge**: real, responsibly-held knowing that is not metaphysically true. Newtonian mechanics was knowledge — extraordinary, world-building knowledge — and it is, strictly, false. Declaring it "never really knowledge" because a truer theory arrived is the JTB confusion in miniature. (This is Chapter 2's *tralse-real* move applied to knowing: real ∧ not-classically-true.)

Now the strongest objection, stated before the defense. The reigning intuition is **factivity**: "she knows that p" seems to *entail* that p is true — and we do retract the word when a claim collapses ("she didn't really know, she just thought she did"). Most epistemologists treat factivity as non-negotiable. Notably, Timothy Williamson's influential **knowledge-first** program (2000) *also* rejects the JTB analysis — but from the opposite side: it keeps truth central and denies that knowledge is built up from belief plus justification. So the framework is in respectable company in abandoning JTB; it simply parts from Williamson over *which* term to drop.

The honest reply: when we retract "knew," we are almost always discovering the belief was **not, in fact, well-justified**, or we are *upgrading* to a stricter standard — both moves *inside* justification, not evidence that we ever independently checked correspondence. In the rare case where a belief was maximally justified and still failed, the framework bites the bullet and calls it tralse knowledge rather than pretend we once touched a truth-condition we never could. The pragmatists (Peirce, James, Dewey) already located truth in the ideal limit of inquiry, and anti-realists (Dummett; Putnam's internal realism) pressed the same "no God's-eye view" point; JBK-1 is the sharp, corpus-native form of that lineage.

JBK-1 also slots cleanly onto the Authority Axis (Chapter 9). An authority grant just *is* a justified commitment held with a live reservation — and JBK-1 says that reservation is permanent *by construction*, because the metaphysical truth we are reserving about is, in principle, out of reach. That is precisely the calibrated belief-and-doubt posture the Authority Axis already prescribes, now given its deepest reason.

> **Key insight:** We can never step outside our beliefs to check one against bare reality; every certification we actually run is a check on *justification*. So justified belief is not a lesser stand-in for knowledge — it is the whole of what knowledge could ever have been. The honest falsifier: exhibit a genuine case of knowing whose warrant *essentially* required inaccessible correspondence — a certification that is not, on inspection, a justification-check in disguise. The framework wagers there is none.

### In one paragraph

For two thousand years knowledge was **justified true belief** — until Gettier (1963) showed all three boxes can be ticked and the result still misses. The framework's diagnosis is that the culprit is the **T**: "truth" in JTB secretly welds two different things — *justified truth*, which we can inspect and argue over, and *metaphysical truth*, correspondence to reality-in-itself, which by its own definition we can never directly access. The metaphysical half is both unreachable (every certification we actually run checks *justification*) and redundant (where it holds, it holds regardless of justification), so it floats free of the rest. Drop it: knowledge, as anything we can aim for or claim to possess, simply is **justified belief** (JBK-1, framework-internal candidate). A belief that was well-justified but turns out metaphysically false was still knowledge in the only sense we ever had — **tralse knowledge** (Chapter 2's *real ∧ not-true* applied to knowing), the way Newtonian mechanics was world-building knowledge and is, strictly, false. The strongest objection is factivity — "if she really knew, it must be true" — and the reply is that our retractions of "knew" are almost always justification-downgrades in disguise, not independent checks on correspondence; Timothy Williamson's knowledge-first program (2000) also abandons JTB, from the opposite side, so the company is respectable. Read this way, justified belief is not a lesser stand-in for knowledge — it is the whole of what knowledge could ever have been, and the permanent reservation it carries is exactly the calibrated belief-and-doubt the Authority Axis (Chapter 9) already prescribed.
