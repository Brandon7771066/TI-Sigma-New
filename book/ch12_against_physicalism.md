## Chapter 12: The Case Against Physicalism, Skepticism, and Bayesianism

Three positions dominate the modern educated mind so thoroughly that most people mistake them for "just thinking clearly" rather than for the contestable philosophical commitments they actually are. The first is **physicalism** — the view that matter (and physical process) is all that fundamentally exists. The second is **doctrinaire skepticism** — the stance that withholding belief is always the safe, rigorous default. The third is **naive Bayesianism** — the claim that rational belief just *is* assigning numerical probabilities and updating them by Bayes' theorem.

This chapter argues that none of the three is the neutral, assumption-free starting point it pretends to be. But the framework's brutal-honesty discipline (#69) cuts both ways, so the rule here is strict: **state each opponent's strongest case first, in its own best words, before replying.** A critique that only attacks a weak version of its target has proven nothing.

> **Key insight:** Physicalism, skepticism, and Bayesianism are not the absence of a worldview. They are worldviews — strong ones — that have learned to present themselves as the mere lack of one.

One discipline runs through every reply that follows, because it is the move each position quietly relies on. Each defends its strong, distinctive thesis by pointing at something **trivial and universally accepted** — that the physical world is real, that one should update on evidence, that questioning beats blind confirmation — and treating *that* near-universal agreement as if it vindicated the contested claim. It does not. A framework earns its keep only by what its **central tenet** predicts and explains on its *own* merit, judged against rivals who actually deny it — not by re-winning ground nobody was contesting. So for each of the three we will name the trivial concession plainly, set it aside, and hold the real thesis to account: physicalism's *"matter and energy are all there is,"* Bayesianism's *"Bayes' theorem is the objective, complete default for all of truth,"* and skepticism's *"doubt and inaction are the rational default."*

### The case for physicalism (steel-manned)

Begin by granting physicalism everything it has earned, because it has earned a great deal.

- **It works.** Physical methods predict eclipses to the second, design the transistor, sequence the genome, and put a rover on Mars. No competing framework comes close to this track record of confirmed prediction. **(verified)**
- **It is parsimonious.** One kind of stuff, governed by uniform laws, is simpler than two kinds of stuff plus an account of how they interact. Occam's razor favors it.
- **Mind tracks brain.** Damage a brain region and a specific mental capacity degrades. Anesthesia abolishes consciousness; caffeine sharpens it. The tight, lawful correlation between neural states and mental states is among the best-replicated findings in science. **(verified)**

This is a formidable case — but it is worth being precise about *what* it establishes. Every one of those triumphs is a triumph of **physical method**, and of the modest claim that **the physical world is real** — and almost nobody disputes either. Even the idealist and the mystic *live as though* the physical world exists and the present life matters; their feet find the stairs in the dark like everyone else's. So the eclipses and the transistors are won on ground no opponent is contesting. To earn its **distinctive** thesis, physicalism would have to succeed at something its rivals actually deny — and that thesis is not "the physical world is real" but the far stronger **"matter and energy are *all* there is."** Grant physicalism every confirmed prediction and you have still granted it nothing about *that* claim, because the predictions read identically whether or not it is true. Notice, too, the quiet boundary in "overwhelmingly successful": physicalism is overwhelmingly successful *in the domains it accepts as valid*. Where it meets phenomena it cannot handle — first-person experience chief among them — its standard move is to **dismiss** them as illusory or not-yet-real, which is not the same as **explaining** them. A track record inside a chosen arena is not a licence over everything outside it.

### A definition we will need: evidence

Before replying, it helps to fix a word that does a great deal of quiet work in these debates — **evidence** — because the framework defines it precisely, and the definition is a genuine contribution.

> **Evidence** is *any proposition used in support of a proof*, and it counts as evidence **whether or not it is accepted, contested, or dismissed**. And, decisively: **evidence is not proof.**

Three consequences follow, and they cut in every direction (#69):

- **Being evidence is a role, not a grade.** A proposition is evidence by virtue of the *job it does* — standing in support of some claim — not by virtue of being convincing. So *dismissing* a body of data does not strip it of the status of evidence; it only contests its **weight**. When someone says "there is no evidence for X," they almost always mean "the evidence for X does not persuade me" — a weaker and very different statement. (Parapsychology research, for instance, *is* evidence in exactly this sense — propositions marshalled toward a conclusion — even though the mainstream dismisses it. Calling it "evidence" commits you to *nothing* about whether it is *good* evidence or whether the conclusion is true; this book takes no such stand.)
- **Weight is a separate, graded axis.** Because anything offered in support counts as evidence, the real work of judgment moves to *how much* a given piece supports its claim — which may be large, negligible, or even negative. Keeping the binary question ("is it evidence?") apart from the graded one ("how much does it weigh?") is precisely what stops the word from being used to win arguments by definition.
- **Evidence is not proof.** Proof is far stronger — a chain tight enough to compel assent. Almost every real dispute is fought with evidence of varying weight, not proof; pretending otherwise, in either direction, is a category mistake.
- **What counts as evidence cannot depend on who is right** — an uncomfortable but unavoidable consequence, and it follows from the **Authority Axis**. If a claim earned the *name* "evidence" only once an authority blessed its conclusion, the definition would run backwards — *conclusion-first, bidirectional* — and so become **arbitrary**, meaning whatever the powerful say it means. To stay principled it must cut the other way: the observations a flat-Earther marshals (the horizon *looks* level; still water *seems* to lie flat) are, for pragmatic purposes, **evidence** — propositions offered in support of a proof. Granting the label costs nothing, because (per the bullets above) it fixes their *weight* at essentially nil and says nothing about the conclusion. The price of a non-arbitrary definition is that even bad cases get *heard*; that price is worth paying.

This single distinction defuses a recurring move in the sections that follow: quietly relabelling inconvenient data as "not evidence" so it never has to be weighed at all.

The resolution is not to gatekeep the *word* but to keep two things firmly apart. **What counts as evidence** is generous and authority-independent; **which conclusion the evidence supports** is a separate judgment, settled by the *weight* of the whole body of it — and that verdict is **independent of how we labelled the inputs**. So we adopt the wide definition *and* go on supporting the correct proof — the Earth is round — because the proof is carried by the evidence's weight, not by who was permitted to speak. The two never collide: a generous door does not lower the bar inside the room.

One demand does follow, and it is a demand of plain intellectual honesty: wherever a conclusion is genuinely **in live dispute** — more than one option still open — the honest presentation shows the supporting claims for **every** live conclusion, not only the favoured one, and then lets their weights do the deciding in the open. Hiding one side's evidence, or disqualifying it by *fiat* so it never has to be weighed, is exactly the failure this definition exists to prevent.

Showing every live option is not the same as weighing them equally. The weight a piece of evidence earns is set, first and above all, by the **quality of the reasoning that produced it**: was the inference sound, the method careful, the data handled honestly, *given what was knowable then*? That quality is a **fixed fact** about the moment the reasoning was made — it does not change with *when* we judge it. What *can* change is our **estimate** of it: later evidence bearing on how good the reasoning actually was — a flaw we only now detect, data we only later learn was mishandled — revises what we *thought* the quality was, exactly like any other update to evidence. The one move barred is letting the *outcome* stand in for the quality: that a conclusion later proved true or false is not, by itself, a measure of how well it was reasoned (a sound method can land wrong; a careless one can land right by luck). This quality of reasoning is the **primary** signal. The **overall quality of the source** — its track record across many past judgments — is a real but **secondary** signal: a useful prior, and most useful precisely when the at-the-time reasoning is hidden, garbled, or otherwise impossible to inspect. A trusted expert's bare say-so is weaker than a newcomer's transparently excellent argument; yet when we cannot see the argument at all, the expert's record is the best proxy we have. The two never swap ranks — a strong reputation does not override reasoning we *can* examine and find wanting. (This is the **Authority Axis** doing honest work: authority informs *weight* without ever *defining* what counts as evidence. The full treatment is the framework's Evidence Doctrine, **EVD-1**.)

This is also why genuinely controversial questions — creationism versus evolution, the "flat-Earth" debate — should be *raised* when it is pragmatic to do so (when an audience is actually weighing them), and raised *with* an honest account of each side's reasoning quality, both at the time and overall. A seat at the table always comes with a scorecard. Done this way the payoff is fourfold: every position gets a **seat at the table**, so no one can claim they were silenced; the **audience evaluates for itself** from the open weights, which persuades and inoculates far better than censorship; the **speaker's credibility is maintained**, because fairly airing the other side and then showing why it loses reads as confident, not evasive; and **diplomacy is preserved**, because a fair hearing turns a fight about *who gets to speak* into a calm discussion about *where the weight actually lies*. Two guardrails keep this honest, and they are just the rules above: raise it *when pragmatic* (not as manufactured balance for settled questions), and never grant the seat without the scorecard — a hearing is not a declaration of parity, so flat-Earth claims earn their seat and their near-zero weight in the same breath. (To be honest about the honesty: the first benefit follows straight from the definition, but the social payoffs — better inoculation, kept credibility, calmer disagreement — are well-attested *tendencies*, not guarantees in every case.)

Underneath all four benefits sits one unifying idea the framework calls **expressing Myrion** — hitting, for *this* domain and *this* moment, the right balance between truth (GILE) and what the situation can bear (existence). The speaker's whole job, on this view, is to reconcile three things at once: their own best understanding of reality, what the audience both *wants and needs* to hear, and an honest sense of their own limitations. As the framework puts it, *it is by reconciling all the perspectives and values most pertinent to the situation that a speaker obtains respect, exhibits truth, and properly expresses existence.* Get that balance right and honesty becomes *receivable*; drop your own understanding and you slide into flattery, drop the audience and you lecture into the void, drop your own limitations and you preach with a confidence you have not earned. One guardrail keeps this from sliding into mere people-pleasing: the audience only governs *framing and timing* — what people *want* never reorders the weight of the evidence, only how the truth is delivered. (This balance-target sense of "Myrion" is distinct from "Myrion Resolution," the labelling procedure met in the truth-labels chapter — same root, different job.)

### The reply: success in a domain is not completeness across all domains

The physicalist case quietly performs one extra step that does not follow. From "physical methods work superbly for physical phenomena," it slides to "therefore all phenomena are physical." That is a different, far stronger claim — and it carries its own burden of proof.

Consider the three pillars more carefully:

- **Parsimony is a tie-breaker, not a trump card.** Occam's razor chooses between theories that explain the data *equally well*. It cannot be used to dismiss data a theory fails to explain. Copernicus's early model was *less* parsimonious than Ptolemy's (it still needed epicycles); the evidence carried it anyway. Simplicity decides ties; it does not override observations. And the parsimony is not even as clean as it looks: physicalism itself trades in **abstract** entities — laws, numbers, the very concept "physical" — none of which is itself a physical object, so "one kind of stuff" was never quite one kind of stuff. **(framework-internal reasoning)**
- **Correlation is not identity.** That brain function is *necessary* for normal mental function is well established. That brain states are *sufficient for* — or *identical to* — conscious experience is a separate claim that the correlation alone cannot establish. X reliably accompanying Y does not show X *is* Y. **(framework-internal)**
- **The hard problem remains open.** Even a complete neuroscience would explain which information is processed, integrated, and reported. It would not, on its own terms, explain why any of that processing is *accompanied by felt experience* rather than going on "in the dark." This is David Chalmers's hard problem, and it is a genuine open question, not a settled one. Put bluntly: **no account of neuroscience that leaves consciousness unexplained can call itself complete** — and consciousness is not a peripheral oddity but among the most certain features of reality, the one thing each of us meets first-hand. A physics of everything-*but*-the-observer has quietly left out the observer. **(open / framework-internal)**

There is a sharper structural point. Physicalism advances a *universal negative*: there are no non-physical features of reality, anywhere, ever. Universal negatives are the most expensive claims to defend, because a single genuine counterexample sinks them. So the honest scorecard is symmetry: hold physicalism to the same standard of positive evidence it demands of its rivals. When you do, the most confident reading of physics turns out to be conspicuously incomplete — the measurement problem in quantum mechanics is a century old and unresolved, and dark matter and dark energy together make up roughly 95% of the universe's mass-energy while remaining unexplained by current theory. **(verified that these are open problems; speculative to read them as *support* for any non-physicalist alternative.)**

> **Key insight:** "Physical methods explain physical things" is true and important. "Therefore everything is physical" is a leap — a metaphysical bet wearing the lab coat of a neutral default.

#### Winning the battle, losing the war

Suppose we grant the physicalist the entire statistical battle. Suppose every reported anomaly — every well-documented case of someone seeing, knowing, or experiencing something the physical model says is impossible — is, one by one, ruled *not statistically significant*. Even then a larger question is left standing, and it is the one that actually matters: **why is there so much to dismiss in the first place?** Ruling an observation insignificant is not the same as explaining why the observation was *there to be made*. A theory that has truly won does not merely subtract the anomalies; it **accounts for them** — for their shape, their persistence, their recurrence across cultures and centuries.

On that question physicalism is conspicuously empty, and the gap is not small:

- It does not explain **why the strangeness exists at all** — why reality so reliably *presents* people with experiences that read, from the inside, as impossible.
- It does not explain **why so many people, including careful and highly intelligent ones, disagree** over what should (on the physicalist story) be straightforward evidence.
- It does not explain **why "outrageous" claims about reality recur** so insistently, in every era and population, rather than dying out as one would expect of simple error.

The usual reply is a label: *magical thinking*. But a label is not an account. To *name* a pattern "magical thinking" is to point at the thing-to-be-explained, not to explain it — it restates that the behaviour is widespread and unlicensed by physics, which is precisely what needed explaining. The more honest researchers concede this: the **cognitive science of religion**, for instance, has documented *that* such beliefs are near-universal and *how* they spread, while openly granting it has no settled account of *why the underlying pull is so strong and so uniform*. **(The cognitive-science-of-religion literature is real and is fairly described here as documenting-more-than-explaining; reading its open gap as *support* for any specific non-physical alternative would be speculative — the gap is a debt physicalism owes, not a proof anyone else has paid.)**

So the honest scorecard is split. Physicalism may, with real justification, claim it has **won the battle** — case by case, the statistics rarely force a non-physical conclusion. What it cannot claim by any standard is that it has **won the war**: it has *dismissed* the mountain of anomalous human experience without ever *explaining* why the mountain is there. A framework that aspires to be the whole of reality cannot leave the most insistent feature of human reports filed permanently under "noise."

#### A deeper problem: "physical" has no fixed meaning

There is an objection more basic than any of these, and it cuts at the root: physicalism cannot say, with any precision, what "physical" *means*. The usual gloss is "matter and energy" — but what counts as matter and energy has been a moving target for the whole history of science. The modern atomic model was only formalized in the early twentieth century; quantum theory, antimatter, and the rest of the strange modern menagerie were unknown until decades later. Each time, the inventory of "the physical" was quietly enlarged to absorb the new discovery. So "everything is physical" turns out to mean "everything is whatever physics currently happens to recognize" — a definition that rewrites itself every few generations.

This is not a fringe complaint; it is a well-known argument in the philosophy of mind called **Hempel's Dilemma** (after the philosopher Carl Hempel). It runs like this. Define "physical" by *today's* physics, and physicalism is almost certainly *false* — present physics is admittedly incomplete (recall the unresolved measurement problem and the roughly 95% of the universe we cannot yet account for). Define it instead by some *future, completed* physics, and the thesis goes *empty* — we have no idea what that final theory will contain, so "everything is physical" reduces to "everything is whatever the finished science will include," which is a promissory note, not a claim with content. Either horn defeats the confident version of physicalism. **(Hempel's Dilemma is a real, widely-discussed argument in academic philosophy; it is reported here accurately and is not original to this framework.)**

The pattern hides a revealing double standard. Historically the label "non-physical" has been used to *resist* new phenomena — to keep them out of respectable science — right up until the evidence forced acceptance, at which point the very same phenomenon was quietly reclassified as "physical" all along. Magnetism, electromagnetic fields, and radiation each made roughly that journey. The lesson is uncomfortable for the physicalist: what we call "non-physical" today (a careful person might point to, say, intuition, or contested traditions like the chakra system) could perfectly well be filed under "physical" tomorrow, the moment it is understood and accepted. But notice the price of that flexibility. If *anything* that ever gets confirmed automatically counts as "physical," then physicalism rules nothing out — it predicts nothing, forbids nothing, and can never be shown wrong. A thesis that absorbs every future discovery by definition has stopped being a substantive claim about reality and become a label we agree to keep reapplying. **(The intuition and chakra examples are illustrative only; their status is exactly what is disputed, and nothing here asserts they are real. The point is about the *definition* of "physical," not about any particular phenomenon.)**

There is one boundary where this elasticity meets a genuine wall, and it is the one that matters most. The abstract features of reality — meaning, mathematics, and above all *consciousness* — are not obviously the kind of thing that can be built up out of matter and energy at all. The useful distinction here is between **weak** emergence (a higher-level pattern that is, in principle, fully derivable from its physical parts — a traffic jam from individual cars) and **strong** emergence (a feature *not* derivable from the parts even in principle). Felt experience looks, to many, like the one serious candidate for strong emergence: no amount of detail about neurons firing seems to *entail*, by itself, why there is something it is like to undergo them. Physicalism's standard move is to call consciousness an **epiphenomenon** — a causally-idle by-product thrown off by the machinery. But that is arguably a category mistake. The abstract is not one more item *inside* the physical inventory waiting to be located; it is, prima facie, *its own kind of thing* — met first-hand and a priori, not read off a dial. Demoting it to a side-effect of matter does not explain it so much as change the subject. **(Weak-versus-strong emergence is a standard distinction associated with David Chalmers; the claim that consciousness is *strongly* emergent is a live and contested philosophical position, not a settled result — flagged as the framework's stance, and consistent with TOF-1's separate proposal that the fundamental layer is the indeterminate Tralse Soup rather than matter.)**

Put together, these turn physicalism from a bold thesis into an unstable one: it cannot fix the meaning of its central word, it defends that word by expanding it whenever convenient, and at the one boundary where expansion is hardest — the abstract, and consciousness in particular — it offers relabeling ("epiphenomenon") in place of explanation. None of this *proves* a non-physical reality; what it shows is that physicalism is far less settled, and far less neutral, than its confident presentation suggests. **(framework-internal synthesis; this is candidate principle PDU-1 — Physical-Definitional Instability — not ratified, count unchanged 79; see the corpus ledger.)**

#### The pragmatic cost: a worldview that is hard to live well inside

There is one more count against physicalism, and it is a different *kind* of count — not about what it fails to explain, but about what it costs the person who takes it on whole. Pushed to its logical end, strict physicalism licenses three moves that quietly corrode a human life:

- **It pathologizes disagreement.** If matter is all there is, then people who report otherwise are not interlocutors with a different reading of the evidence — they are malfunctioning, and the language slides easily from "mistaken" to "irrational" to "ill" or "insane." A worldview that, to stay intact, *tends* to classify a large share of humanity as broken should be suspected of a defect of its own.
- **It dissolves objective value.** If only matter and energy are real, then goodness, love, and beauty — the GILE values — are not features of the world but noises a brain makes. "Be kind" drops to the standing of "I like blue." Lived consistently, that is hard to tell apart from nihilism.
- **It denies free will.** If every event is fixed by prior physical states, the felt experience of choosing is an illusion and moral responsibility a convenient fiction — a conclusion many physicalists embrace explicitly.

Here the honesty discipline cuts both ways at once, and both halves have to be said plainly:

- **A belief being demoralizing does not make it false.** Treating "this is bleak" as "this is untrue" is the *appeal to consequences*, a real fallacy, and the framework does not commit it. If physicalism were simply *true*, its bleakness would be a cost to bear, not a refutation.
- **But truth was never the only axis.** The Unified Optimization Principle judges a worldview on *two* things at once: how well it tracks what exists, and how well it lets conscious beings actually live (the existence / HEM pillar). On the second axis physicalism scores badly — and that is a legitimate mark against its *imperial* claim to be the whole picture, even though it says nothing against its narrow physical claims. A map that is accurate about the terrain but gets you killed if you follow it is still a defective map to live by.

Is "bad to live inside" more than an intuition? The empirical record is real but **genuinely mixed, and must be reported that way (#69).** The best-known result — Vohs and Schooler's 2008 finding that experimentally *weakening* people's belief in free will increased cheating — launched a literature tying free-will *disbelief* to worse self-control and less helping. **But** that literature later hit serious replication trouble: large multi-lab attempts recovered the effect weakly or not at all, so the strong "disbelief makes you behave worse" claim is currently *contested*, not settled. What holds up better is the broader, less surprising pattern that a felt sense of *meaning, agency, and mattering* tends to track well-being while its absence tracks with depression and anomie — suggestive of physicalism's pragmatic cost without proving the precise causal story. **(verified that the studies exist and that the replication is contested; the causal claim is preliminary.)**

**The decision-theoretic turn — why this subdues physicalism *in practice*.** Put the two honest halves together and a practical conclusion follows that does *not* wait on the truth question being settled. Recall the chapter's earlier point: non-physical phenomena are not crisply *False*; at most they sit at **Indeterminate**, so the honest probability that some of them are real is not zero. Suppose, only for argument, it is a mere 5% (the figure is illustrative, not measured — pick your own, as long as it is honestly above zero). Now compare two ways to *live*:

- **Adopt strict physicalism as a settled worldview.** If you are right, you gain little you didn't already have from physics-as-method. If you are wrong — even 5%-wrong — you have organized your inner life, and licensed a society, around denying value, agency, and the testimony of most of humanity. That downside lands whether or not the debate is ever settled.
- **Withhold the universal negative (stay at Indeterminate).** You keep every result physics actually delivers, lose nothing in the lab, and simply decline to assert the one claim physics cannot fund. The downside here is small.

That asymmetry is the whole argument, and it is *not* the appeal-to-consequences fallacy flagged above: the question is no longer "is physicalism true?" but "should I adopt it as the frame I live and govern by?" — a decision under uncertainty, which answers to *expected* cost, not to whether the verdict is in. A reasonable rule for adopting any totalizing worldview is therefore strict: do it only if **(1)** the chance it is wrong is near-zero **and (2)** there is a workable way to hold it without damaging the people who hold it. Physicalism, honestly assessed, meets *neither* — its central claim is a universal negative it cannot drive to near-zero doubt, and (per the costs above) it has no clean story for being lived well.

This is a cousin of Pascal's Wager and inherits its known weakness — you cannot run the same move to justify *any* exotic belief you fancy. The reply is that the cheap alternative here is not some rival creed but mere *withholding*: the asymmetry is between asserting a costly universal negative and simply declining to, which is exactly why it does not generalize into licensing arbitrary beliefs. **(decision-theoretic argument; the probability and the cost magnitudes are illustrative, not measured.)**

And the social ledger is not small. If physicalism is wrong even slightly, the standing practice of treating the large share of humanity who report otherwise as deluded or unwell carries **potential** costs — personal, clinical, economic, civic — that scale with population; the order of magnitude alone is a reason for caution. **(qualitative argument, not a costed estimate — no figure is claimed.)**

Net, *for all practical purposes*: physicalism-as-method keeps its results, but physicalism-as-a-worldview-to-live-by is subdued — not because it has been proven false, but because adopting it is a bad bet under honest uncertainty.

> **Key insight:** Physicalism's deepest problem may not be that it is false, but that it is *unlivable* — and once the choice is framed honestly (a small but nonzero chance of being wrong, against severe and asymmetric costs), adopting it as a worldview is a losing bet *for all practical purposes*, even with the truth question left open.

#### But withholding is the floor, not the destination

Declining to assert physicalism is where a *skeptic* stops. It is not where a TI Sigma practitioner stops. A merely-skeptical mind treats *Indeterminate* as a place to sit down; the framework treats it as a place to start digging. Here the framework's own values do real work — and they push past caution into active inquiry.

**Curiosity is a virtue**, not a risk to be managed. So the live *possibility* that some "supernatural" or spiritual claims are real is not a threat to be quarantined but an invitation to *investigate* — openly, and with exactly the evidence-discipline applied to everything else (so this never collapses into "believe whatever you like"). The pull is strongest precisely for someone who takes GILE seriously: if there is any chance that spiritual reality is the **ultimate instantiation of GILE-Truth**, a GILE-seeker has every reason to look and almost none to look away.

### The case for skepticism (steel-manned)

Skepticism, too, has earned its prestige.

- The graveyard of human belief is full of confident nonsense — bloodletting, phrenology, perpetual-motion machines. Demanding evidence is how we stopped paying for those.
- "Extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence" (Sagan, echoing Hume) is good hygiene: the cost of a false positive in medicine, finance, or public policy can be enormous.
- Withholding judgment under genuine uncertainty is honest. The careful doubter who says "I'm not yet convinced" is doing exactly what intellectual responsibility requires.

All true. The framework keeps this. Validation is non-negotiable; the chapters on real work and on objections lean hard on it.

### The reply: selective doubt is a position, not a method

First, separate two claims the skeptic tends to run together. One is **trivial and universally granted**: that questioning, testing, and trying to *falsify* a claim is more rigorous than only hunting for confirmations of it. Nobody disputes this; it is ordinary good practice, and the framework keeps it wholeheartedly. Establishing it wins skepticism no ground, because no opponent was ever standing on the far side of it. The skeptic's **distinctive, contestable** thesis is much stronger: that **doubt and inaction should be the *default* — that withholding belief and withholding action are the safe, rigorous posture until evidence compels otherwise.** *That* is the claim to be judged on its own merit, and it is the claim that fails.

The trouble is not skepticism but its *selective* application — what the framework calls **skepticism-hypocrisy**. A genuine method is applied uniformly. A position is applied where convenient. Watch where the doubt goes and where it mysteriously stops.

The committed skeptic demands evidence for every first-order claim ("this works," "this is true") while exempting their own *second-order* commitments from the same demand:

- "Evidence is the only path to knowledge."
- "Withholding belief is always safer than asserting it."
- "Confidence held before public argument is irrational."

Each of these is itself a substantive claim about how minds and inquiry work — and each is typically held *without* the evidence the skeptic requires of everyone else. The doubter who says "I just follow the evidence" is making a confident assertion about the structure of knowledge that they never submit to their own tribunal.

There is a clean reductio here. If avoiding error were the supreme epistemic virtue and non-belief the safest posture, then the perfect epistemic agent would be **a rock**. A rock has never been wrong, never held an unjustified belief, never overreached. It has achieved total freedom from epistemic risk — by achieving total epistemic emptiness. The lesson: epistemic value lives in the *pursuit of truth*, which necessarily risks error, not in the mere avoidance of being wrong.

> **Key insight:** "Be more skeptical" is not automatically "be more rational." Doubt applied to others' claims and never to one's own foundations is just conformity wearing the costume of rigor.

The framework's positive move is to dissolve the binary that creates the hypocrisy. In ordinary either/or thinking you must *choose*: certainty or doubt, conviction or humility. That forced choice is exactly what traps the skeptic into being *certain about uncertainty*. TI Sigma's tralse epistemology lets both coexist — a claim can be held with working conviction *and* with genuine openness to revision. That is not fence-sitting; it is the difference between a hypothesis you act on and a hypothesis you stop testing. The **Authority Axis (AA)** makes this explicit: how much you *act* on a claim is a separate dial from how *true* you take it to be, applied symmetrically to your own beliefs and to others'.

### The case for Bayesianism (steel-manned)

Bayesianism is the most sophisticated of the three, and the framework concedes the most to it.

- Bayes' theorem is **mathematically valid**. Full stop. **(verified)**
- It cured real diseases of reasoning: it makes the base-rate fallacy precise, it forces you to ask "how likely is this evidence *if I'm wrong*?", and it explains why a positive result on a rare-disease test can still mean you probably don't have the disease.
- As a model of *updating under well-defined uncertainty* — with known reference classes and repeatable trials — it is excellent, and the framework uses it there without apology.

Here too, separate the trivial from the contested. That you should **update your view when new evidence arrives**, and that events can have an **inherent chance of coming out one way or another**, are points essentially everyone already accepts — they are not Bayesianism's distinctive thesis, and proving them wins the position nothing. Bayesianism's **strong** claim is a different animal: that **base rates exist as objective, abstract facts for every question, that Bayes' theorem is the correct default for evaluating *any* frequency and deciphering *any* truth, and therefore that Bayesianism is *complete*** — that *all* rational belief reduces to numbers in [0,1] updated by the rule. The dispute is narrow and specific, and it is with *that* strong claim, not with the truism that one should learn from evidence.

### The reply: the rule cannot supply its own inputs

Three problems, in plain language.

**1. Where do the priors come from?** Bayes' theorem tells you how to *update* a prior, never how to *set* one. For genuinely novel questions there is often no valid base rate at all. What is the prior probability that a specific unprecedented invention succeeds, or that a specific paradigm gets overturned? There is no stable reference class to count. The "subjective Bayesian" answer — "use your personal credence" — is honest, but notice what it concedes: the input is *intuition*, renamed. Bayesianism thus quietly *requires* the very intuitive judgment (the I in GILE) it often claims to replace. **(framework-internal)**

**2. Strong priors can make evidence nearly irrelevant.** When a prior is set very low, even near-perfect evidence barely moves the posterior. Take a claim assigned prior 0.001, then observe evidence with 99% sensitivity and 95% specificity. Bayes returns a posterior of roughly **1.9%** — still "very unlikely." This is the formal engine behind "extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence": set the prior low enough and *no* feasible evidence is ever extraordinary enough. In a research community where training and incentives push the prior on the reigning paradigm toward ~0.999, anomalies get rationally dismissed for decades. That is not a bug in any individual's arithmetic; it is the correct application of strong priors — which is precisely the worry. **(verified as a property of the arithmetic; the historical reading is framework-internal.)**

**3. It cannot represent genuine indeterminacy.** Bayesian probability is built so that P(H) and P(¬H) sum to 1. But some propositions are not "probably true to degree p" — they are *structurally* between, or true under one carving and false under another. "Free will exists" is true under compatibilism and false under hard determinism; that is a conceptual fork, not a frequency. Collapsing it into a single number ("0.6") throws away the information about *why* it is unsettled. The framework's **base-4 labels** (True, False, **Indeterminate**, Meta-Indeterminate) and the **Permissibility Distribution (PD)** keep that information: a genuinely 50/50 proposition is labeled *Indeterminate* — a positive logical state — rather than frozen at a fictitious 0.5. And a malformed proposition is screened out as **Meta-Indeterminate** by Myrion Resolution rather than smuggled in with a made-up prior. **(framework-internal.)**

There is even a self-reference twist. Bayesianism claims that rational agents *should* adopt beliefs by Bayesian updating. But nobody adopted Bayesianism *by* a Bayesian calculation — Bayes, Price, and Laplace reached it by mathematical insight, and modern Bayesians adopt it because they find it compelling. The foundation is not laid by its own method. That does not make the theorem false; it shows the *philosophy* built on it cannot be the whole story of rationality.

> **Key insight:** Use Bayes' theorem wherever you have honest numbers — it is a genuine tool. Just don't mistake the calculator for the whole of reason. The hard parts (which hypotheses to consider, what counts as evidence, when "we don't know yet" beats "probably false") happen *before* the arithmetic starts.

### A note on staying honest while criticizing

It would be easy, having dismantled three giants, to overclaim a victory. The framework does not. Physicalism is the most successful research *method* in history and TI Sigma keeps almost all of its results; the quarrel is not with the method but with the metaphysical thesis built on it — whether matter is *bedrock*, and whether a model that *dismisses* rather than *explains* the anomalies can call itself complete. Skepticism's demand for validation is retained and even strengthened. Bayes' theorem is true and remains in the toolkit. What the chapter rejects is the *imperialism* of each — the move from "this is powerful in its domain" to "this is the whole of reality, rigor, or reason." And the framework's own replacements (TOF-1, the PD, LCC as a coherence measure rather than a credence) are flagged honestly as candidate or framework-internal, not as proven. **(open)**

### In one paragraph

Physicalism, doctrinaire skepticism, and naive Bayesianism each present themselves as the neutral, assumption-free default, and each is in fact a strong, contestable position — and each tries to bank credit for a *distinctive* thesis that really belongs to a basic everyone already grants. Physicalism earns its authority in the physical domain but illicitly extends "physical methods explain physical things" into "everything is physical," a universal-negative claim it cannot fund — and one it defends by *dismissing* anomalous human experience as noise rather than *explaining* why so much of it persists, winning the statistical battle but not the explanatory war; TI Sigma answers with a different monism (TOF-1: the indeterminate Tralse Soup, not matter, is fundamental), not with dualism. Physicalism also carries a steep *pragmatic* cost — pathologizing dissent, dissolving objective value, and denying free will — which on the framework's twin-axis test (truth *and* existence) counts against it as a philosophy to live by, even though a worldview's bleakness is never by itself evidence that it is false; and once you treat adopting-it-as-a-worldview as a *decision under uncertainty* (non-physical phenomena sit at *Indeterminate*, not False, so the odds it is wrong are not zero) the asymmetric stakes make it a losing bet *for all practical purposes* — physicalism-as-method survives, physicalism-as-worldview is subdued; and the practitioner's reply to that subdual is not skeptical fence-sitting but *active, curious exploration* of the evidence for the spiritual, since curiosity is a virtue and spirituality — as the direction toward Myrion (the Truth–Existence optimum the framework calls Supreme) — is plausibly the highest one, so the book urges the reader to seek GILE-Truth and stay open to its possible ultimate instantiation while asserting no spiritual claim to be true. Doctrinaire skepticism is right that validation matters but smuggles in unexamined certainties about doubt itself — selective skepticism is a position, not a method, and taken to its limit makes a rock the ideal knower. Naive Bayesianism rests on a valid theorem but cannot supply its own priors, lets strong priors swamp good evidence, and cannot represent genuine indeterminacy, which the framework's Indeterminate label and Permissibility Distribution preserve. Steel-manned and answered fairly, all three keep their real strengths and lose only their overreach.
