## Chapter 7: Five Fundamental Truth Labels and Meta-Truths

### From two boxes to a working set of labels

Chapter 1 argued that sorting every claim into "true" or "false" is a habit that distorts reality more than it clarifies it. This chapter does the constructive work: it lays out the small, disciplined vocabulary TI Sigma uses instead. The goal is not to multiply labels for their own sake — a logic with a hundred values is as useless as one with two — but to find the *minimal* set that actually covers the kinds of statements people make.

That set turns out to have a clean structure. There are **four base truth labels** that live *on* the truth spectrum, and there is **one operational flag, N/A**, that gets checked *before* anything else because it marks statements that never belonged on the spectrum at all. Four on the spectrum plus the one screen in front of it is why we speak of **five fundamental truth labels**. Sitting on top of all of this is a separate, open-ended catalogue called **Meta-Truths** — labels that describe a verdict rather than replace it.

Before we begin, one reminder from Chapter 2, because nearly every confusion in this area comes from skipping it. **Tralse** (the structured imperfection inside every coherent claim) is a *quality*, not a label. Every statement that survives carries it. So "Tralse" is never one of the boxes below; it is the texture of the boxes themselves.

> **Key insight:** Tralse is the *grain of the wood*; the truth labels are the *shapes you cut from it*. Asking "is it true, false, indeterminate, or tralse?" is a category mistake — like asking whether a chair is wooden, plastic, metal, or *grainy*.

### A formal definition of truth

Before sorting claims into labels, it helps to say — carefully — what the framework even *means* by "truth," because the definition is not the textbook one. In the correspondence picture most of us inherited, a statement is true when it matches the world, and "true" is a switch: on or off. TI Sigma keeps the matching idea and throws away the switch.

Formally, the framework treats truth as a **graded, time-indexed quantity** rather than a binary one. Write **τ(P)** (Greek *tau*) for the *degree of trueness* a proposition P carries — how far its content coheres with what actually is, read at a particular moment. Truth in this sense is not something a sentence simply has or lacks; it is a *reading on a spectrum* — the very dial Chapter 8 will call the Permissibility Distribution. A claim's full truth-state also has a second coordinate: *how* it falls short of being simply-true (its modality), which is why the faithful representation of τ is complex-valued, not a lone number **(framework-internal)**.

Two consequences follow at once, and both matter for the labels below:

- **Truth is indexed to a context.** "It is raining" is not true-or-false in the abstract; it has a τ-value *here, now, for this asker*. The framework defines truth as the actual tralse-state of what exists, indexed to its temporal (and observer) context — which is exactly why **N/A** ("no answer reachable for this mind now") is a real category rather than a dodge.
- **Truth is what resolution *approaches*, not where it starts.** The procedure that takes a messy proposition and improves its coherence until a verdict is earned is **Myrion Resolution** (Chapter 8). On this view truth is the limit resolution drives toward — maximal coherence given the context — and the labels in this chapter are the discrete *verdicts* it hands back.

A one-line version, kept honest: **truth (τ) is the graded, context-indexed degree to which a proposition coheres with what exists, always carrying its irreducible Tralse-quality** — never a bare on/off, and never a perfect 1.0 (Chapter 6's 0.93 cap applies to truth too). The labels that follow are simply the disciplined way of *reporting* that graded reading when you finally have to act on it.

### Truth in one sentence: the six clauses

The framework also offers a compact, citable form of the same idea — its "truth in one sentence" definition **(framework-internal; Brandon Emerick, April 2026)**:

> *Truth is a proper, relational, and fundamentally beautiful depiction of what exists as such, that can be mentally grasped at the present moment.*

It reads like an aphorism, but every word is load-bearing. Six clauses each do a distinct job:

1. **"Proper"** — the depiction must actually *fit* what it depicts; this is adequacy, not just any loose correspondence.
2. **"Relational"** — truth is not a property a statement owns in isolation; it lives in the relationship between the depiction, the thing depicted, and a mind that grasps it.
3. **"Fundamentally beautiful"** — beauty is built *into* truth, not added afterward. This is the framework's most distinctive claim about truth, and it is the same wager as Elegance in Chapter 5: at the deepest level, what is most true tends also to be most beautiful.
4. **"Depiction of what exists as such"** — truth points at *existence*, at the thing's actual mode of being, not merely its surface appearance. (A later refinement widens this to allow faithful depictions of what does *not* exist — a true statement that something is absent — but the core idea is grounding in what is real **(framework-internal/refinement)**.)
5. **"Can be mentally grasped"** — a truth must be receivable by *some* mind in principle. This is deliberately weaker than "already known": it admits truths no one has grasped yet, while refusing "truths" no mind could ever reach.
6. **"At the present moment"** — truth is anchored in time. A truth about today's weather is not a truth about tomorrow's; the reading is taken *now*. This is the same time-indexing that makes τ a moment-by-moment quantity rather than an eternal stamp.

Read together, the six clauses say truth is *fitting, relational, beautiful, existence-grounded, mind-graspable, and time-bound* — a far richer object than the on/off switch most of us were handed, and the reason the labels in this chapter come in five flavors rather than two.

### The five, in order of when you meet them

The labels are not a flat list you choose from at random. They form a screening pipeline — a sequence of gates each statement passes through. The order matters, so here it is up front:

1. **N/A** — the off-spectrum screen. *Is this even a truth-question?* (Checked first.)
2. **Meta-Indeterminate (MI)** — the nonsense screen. *Is this a coherent claim at all?*
3. **True / False / Indeterminate** — the three real verdicts for everything that survives.

Chapter 8 walks a single proposition through these gates in detail. Here we just meet each label and learn to tell it from its neighbors.

### N/A — the question that was never a question

Start with the label that sits **farthest out** on the spectrum. **N/A ("Not Applicable")** flags a real, well-formed question that a given mind simply *lacks the data to settle* — undetermined, at least for that i-cell at that time, and regardless of tense (the answer may lie in the past, present, or future).

- "How many grains of sand were on this stretch of beach at dawn on this exact day in 1600?"
- "Is there a planet circling that particular faint star?" — *asked before anyone has looked.*
- "What did my great-great-grandmother dream the night before her wedding?"

Each of these has a perfectly real fact of the matter; it is just out of reach for this observer, now. That is what makes N/A different from nonsense (the next screen) and from a genuine, *stable* middle (Indeterminate, below). The framework's ruling here, refined from earlier drafts, is precise: **N/A is off the real true/false line, but not off the map.** It sits high on the spectrum's *imaginary* axis — in what the framework calls the **hyperimaginary** region, the same far end where Meta-Indeterminate lives — but unlike a clean point its **value is imprecise**: we know the answer lies *somewhere* far out there, real but out of present reach, without being able to pin where *(framework-internal)*. New data can, in principle, sharpen that imprecise value and pull the question back toward a verdict. In the framework's object-language, an N/A item is **the THAT which is tralse and may (overall) not be tralse** — a real, *object-specific* deficit-in-reality whose value is settled in principle but, for this mind now, may or may not obtain. That is the family resemblance to MI (both concern a single, pinned THAT) and also the difference: N/A's THAT *may* fail to be tralse overall, whereas MI's THAT *both is and is not* tralse at once.

There is a clean analogy from mathematics. Picture the truth spectrum as the complex plane: ordinary true-and-false claims live on the familiar number line, and the more exotic states live far out along the imaginary axis. **N/A sits high on that imaginary (hyperimaginary) end — but at an *imprecise* value**: not a single point you can name so much as a large region you know the answer falls within, like a magnitude you can tell is huge without being able to measure it. **Meta-Indeterminate lives at that same far end**, which is why the two are easy to confuse; the difference is in *kind*, not location — N/A is a real question whose value is merely out of reach, whereas MI is a question whose own terms collapse (the genuinely *undefined* case, "1 ÷ 0"), so no value, precise or imprecise, exists to find. We meet MI next.

> **Key insight:** "I don't have the data to say" and "this isn't a coherent question" are completely different failures. The first is **N/A** (a real answer exists but is out of reach for this observer now); the second is **Meta-Indeterminate** (the question dismantles itself). Both differ again from **Indeterminate** — a claim that is fully answerable yet *stably* rests between the poles. Collapsing these three is the single most common mistake people make about borderline claims.

We flag N/A early for a practical reason: a question you cannot currently settle for lack of data should be held *open, pending data*, not forced to a verdict the evidence cannot support. Marking it N/A keeps it honestly unresolved rather than falsely decided.

### Meta-Indeterminate (MI) — the nonsense screen

A statement clears the N/A screen by being a *real* truth-question. The next gate catches a different kind of failure: claims that are truth-shaped but **structurally incoherent**.

This is **Meta-Indeterminate (MI)**. Its definition is compact and worth stating formally:

> **MI(P) ⟺ τ(P) ∧ ¬τ(P)** — a proposition is Meta-Indeterminate when it both *is* and *is not* Tralse; it asserts and withdraws its own coherence in the same breath. In the framework's object-language: P names a **THAT** — the specific object in question — that **both is and is not tralse, overall.**

In plainer terms: an MI statement contradicts the very feature that would let it be evaluated. The classic ancestors are the self-undermining sentences philosophers have chewed on for centuries — "This sentence is false," and similar knots that explode whichever value you try to give them. A second family belongs here too: **category errors** — *"Is the number seven jealous?", "What colour is Tuesday?", "Is justice heavier than envy?"* — where the subject's very identity leaves no room for the predicate, so the proposition's identity *self-implodes*. (Earlier drafts filed these under N/A; the framework now places them with MI, because the failure is *incoherence*, not *missing data*.) The framework's verdict is unsentimental: **MI statements are discarded as nonsense.** They are not borderline truths, not 50/50 cases, not "deep paradoxes" to be revered. They simply fail to admit truth-evaluation, so they are screened out before any verdict is attempted.

**Why "overall" matters — the two-tralsity, "as-a-whole" mechanism.** MI most often arises not from one atomic claim but from **two or more indeterminate claims taken together**. Each half, on its own, is a legitimate *tralsity* — a real deficit-in-reality attaching to *its own* THAT. The collapse happens only when they are **judged as a whole** and prove incompatible through self-defeat (excessive, nonsensical incoherence). The verdict "**not tralse**" is then passed on the **statement as a whole**, never on either half: we always begin with *two* genuine tralsities, and only their combination earns the not-tralse label. The tell-tale sign is that the *THAT* is **not the same** across the two self-referring propositions — each "that" points at a *different* object, and forcing the single overall statement to own both incompatible referents at once is exactly the identity conflict MI names. Keep this reading in view wherever the framework passes a not-tralse verdict: it is always *two tralsities, then labeled not-tralse as a whole*, and the two THATs never coincide.

And be clear about what that verdict *means*, not just how it is reached. To label the whole statement **not tralse** is to move it into a **different category** — the **unreal** (see Chapter 2). Tralse marks a deficit in a **real** object; *not* tralse marks a deficit in something that **does not exist**. So the not-tralse verdict is not "a milder tralse" or "cleared of deficit" — it says the self-defeating whole has **no real object** for any deficit to belong to. This is exactly why the two labels that carry it are **MI and certain N/A**: MI's object *is and is not* tralse — the "is not" being this unreal pole — while a *certain* N/A is one whose object never existed to be reached. Genuine tralse always presumes its object is **indeterminate but real**; not tralse withdraws the reality itself.

A historical footnote for readers of older TI Sigma material: what earlier drafts called "Double Tralse" or "DT" was renamed corpus-wide to **MI** *(framework-internal)*. If you meet "DT" in an old paper, read "MI."

The crucial discipline here is to keep MI separate from *Indeterminate*, which is the next thing we'll define and which is a perfectly respectable, coherent verdict. MI is a failure; Indeterminate is a result.

### The three real verdicts: True, False, Indeterminate

Everything that survives both screens gets one of three genuine labels.

- **True** — the claim resolves toward true, while still carrying its embedded Tralse-quality. ("Water boils at a lower temperature at high altitude.")
- **False** — the claim resolves toward false, again with Tralse-quality intact. ("The Sun orbits the Earth.")
- **Indeterminate** — the claim is truth-apt and coherent, but it rests stably at a balance point the framework calls the **45-degree door**. This is *not* ignorance and *not* missing data. It is a real, positive state: a claim that genuinely sits between the poles.

The everyday cases that make Chapter 1's point are mostly Indeterminate: *"This is the right career for me," "She loves me," "The treatment is working."* More information sometimes helps — but often the indeterminacy is in the world, not in your files. A relationship can be genuinely, stably both-and; a career can be genuinely a partial fit. Calling these "true" or "false" *forces* a precision the situation does not contain.

Here is the subtle line between Indeterminate and MI, sharpened by a recent refinement:

- If a claim has **leeway** — room left for it to tilt, even slightly — it is **Indeterminate**.
- If that leeway is **annihilated** — the claim is constructed so it must be *fully* one thing *and* *fully* its opposite at once — it tips into **MI**.

> **Key insight:** Indeterminate is an *open door*; Meta-Indeterminate is a *door welded shut against itself*. "It might rain tomorrow" leaves room to resolve — Indeterminate. "It will both fully rain and fully not-rain, here, at the same instant" leaves no room at all — MI, discarded.

An everyday illustration of the whole pipeline: a friend asks, *"Is my novel good?"* First screen — is this truth-apt? Yes, "good" here means something assessable, so it's not N/A. Second screen — is it coherent? Yes, not a self-cancelling paradox, so not MI. Verdict — likely **Indeterminate**: genuinely strong in places, weak in others, with real leeway and no single collapse to True or False. That verdict is *informative*; "it's true that it's good / false that it's good" would not be.

### Telling a deep in-between from mere nonsense: three grounds

We have just drawn the hardest line in this chapter — between a live **Indeterminate**, a real "both-lean" worth holding, and **Meta-Indeterminate (MI)**, the nonsense that gets screened out. In principle the leeway test settles it; in practice a further question presses: faced with a strange, self-tangling claim, how do you actually tell a deep, genuine paradox (keep it) from mere gibberish (discard it)? The framework's answer is a candidate line of work **(framework-internal, candidate)** with three grounds that do genuinely different jobs.

1. **Coherentism → sense.** Load-bearing supporting premises give the in-between a coherent place in the web, so it *makes sense*. A genuine Indeterminate has such a web; a nonsense-claim, pressed, turns out to have none. *(A property of the claim — content-side; coherentism is developed in Chapter 10.)*
2. **Negative Gap Knowledge (NGK) → tolerability.** Some things we grasp precisely by the shape of the gap they leave: we know what we cannot yet directly state by knowing exactly what is missing. NGK makes a hard in-between **holdable in mind** even before it fully makes sense — you can carry it as a live question without collapsing it. *(Also content-side.)*
3. **Epistemic Tralse-Joules (eTJ) → the capacity to hold it.** The first two are properties of the *claim*; this one is a property of the *mind*. eTJ is the framework's name (from its theory of consciousness, Chapter 13) for a person's **capacity to hold a contradiction-in-tension without prematurely snapping it to true or false, or dismissing it as gibberish.** Two people can face the very same well-scaffolded paradox, with the same gap-knowledge available, and the low-eTJ mind still flattens it — "that's just nonsense" — while the high-eTJ mind sits with it and works. *(A property of the knower — agent-side.)*

The guardrail is what keeps this honest, and it is strict: **eTJ governs who can *see* a genuine in-between; it never *creates* one.** A claim earns the Indeterminate label on the content side *first* — coherent scaffolding, passing the nonsense screen above. Only then does eTJ explain why some minds still miss it. The move "you only call my claim nonsense because you lack the eTJ to hold it" is exactly the cheat the framework rules out: no amount of eTJ converts genuine nonsense (MI) into a genuine in-between. The label is objective; eTJ is only about access.

> **Everyday illustration:** "The electron is at once here and not-here." To a student meeting it cold, gibberish — flatten it, pick one. Scaffolded by the quantum formalism (coherentist support), held by knowing exactly which classical picture is *failing* (NGK), and carried by a mind willing to sit in the tension (eTJ), the same sentence becomes one of the most productive true-tralse claims in all of physics. Same words; what changed is the scaffolding available and the capacity brought to it.

> **Key insight:** Telling a deep paradox from mere nonsense takes three things working together — a coherent web that gives it *sense*, gap-knowledge that makes it *holdable*, and the personal capacity to *sit with the tension*. Miss the third and even a fully legitimate in-between will look like gibberish to you — but your inability to hold it never demotes it to gibberish.

### Meta-Truths — labels *about* a verdict

The four base labels (plus the N/A screen) handle *what* a claim is. But sometimes you need to say something about *the verdict itself* — and that is a different job. For this the framework keeps a separate, open-ended catalogue called **Meta-Truths**.

The headline example is **Moot**. A claim is **Moot** when its truth-value is perfectly settle-able but **doesn't matter in the relevant frame**. "Was the coin heads?" — after the bet has already been called off — is still a real question with a real answer, yet the answer has gone weightless. Moot is therefore *not* nonsense (that's MI), *not* off-spectrum (that's N/A), and *not* a fifth base value. It is a **Meta-Truth that composes on top of the base verdict**: "Moot-True," "Moot-False," and "Moot-Indeterminate" are all meaningful — the claim has its ordinary verdict *and* the meta-note that the verdict is dispensable here.

This compositional behaviour is exactly why Moot is filed as a Meta-Truth rather than a base label. Base labels do **not** combine — "True-False" is gibberish — but Meta-Truths *modify* a base label cleanly, the way "approximately" or "thankfully" modify a sentence without becoming a new sentence. The catalogue holds a dozen well-established Meta-Truths (with a larger conjectural extension) covering reversals, scope-shifts, and other higher-order moves; Moot is simply the most useful one to know first *(framework-internal)*.

> **Key insight:** Base labels answer "*what* is the verdict?" Meta-Truths answer "*what should we do with* the verdict?" Keeping these two questions in separate drawers is what stops a tidy four-label system from sprawling into an unusable hundred-label one.

### Hybrid verdicts: when one label honestly isn't enough

Real propositions are sometimes layered, and forcing them into a single box loses information. TI Sigma allows **Hybrid Myrion Resolution (HMR)**: a claim can natively carry **two or more labels at once** when successive evaluations genuinely land in different places *(framework-internal)*.

The discipline that keeps this from becoming a dodge is twofold. **For faithfulness**, you *display* the hybrid — you don't hide that the claim is, say, both Indeterminate and leaning-True. **For present action**, you *privilege the final label* — you still have to decide and act, so one verdict is foregrounded for the here-and-now. The classic shape is the temporal hybrid: a claim that is Indeterminate-now but Indeterminate-True looking forward. This is how the framework dissolves the old "great-individual versus the followers" debate covered elsewhere — the honest answer is **both**, a hybrid, not a forced single winner.

### Why exactly this many

A fair challenge: isn't five-plus-a-catalogue just arbitrary? The framework's answer is that each item earns its place by catching a failure the others cannot:

- Drop **N/A** and genuine data-gaps get forced into false verdicts — questions answered that no one yet has the evidence to answer.
- Drop **MI** and self-cancelling nonsense pollutes your real verdicts.
- Drop **Indeterminate** and you are back in Chapter 1's two-box trap, lying about every genuinely balanced claim.
- Drop the **Meta-Truths** and you cannot say "true but it doesn't matter" without inventing a confused new value.

None is redundant; none can absorb another without loss. That is the test a good vocabulary has to pass.

### In one paragraph

TI Sigma replaces the true/false binary with a disciplined set: four base labels that live on the truth spectrum — **True, False, Indeterminate, and Meta-Indeterminate** — plus an **N/A** flag for real questions a given mind lacks the data to settle — off the true/false line but high on the imaginary (*hyperimaginary*) end, at an *imprecise* value (alongside MI, though differing in kind), not off the map; together these are the five fundamental labels. The pipeline sets aside N/A (real but unanswerable-for-now, held open pending data) and screens out MI (self-cancelling nonsense *and* category errors, formally a claim that both is and isn't Tralse), then resolves the survivors to True, False, or Indeterminate — where Indeterminate is a real, stable balance, not mere ignorance, and is told from MI by whether any leeway survives. On top of the base verdicts sits a separate catalogue of **Meta-Truths**, such as **Moot** ("true, but it doesn't matter here"), which modify a verdict rather than replace it, and the framework permits honest **hybrid** verdicts when a single label would lose information. When a claim sits right at the Indeterminate/MI border, three grounds tell a deep in-between from nonsense — a coherent web (*sense*), gap-knowledge (*tolerability*), and the mind's own capacity to hold the tension (*eTJ*), with a strict rule that the last, agent-side ingredient never relabels real nonsense as depth. Throughout, remember that **Tralse is a quality every coherent claim carries, not a label** — it is the grain of the wood, never one of the shapes cut from it.
