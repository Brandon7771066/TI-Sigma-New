# The "~15 hours/week" hunter-gatherer figure is CONTESTED — cite it carefully

**Never repeat "foragers worked only ~15 hours/week, ~40% of them" as a settled fact.** It is the popular reading of Richard B. Lee's Ju/'hoansi (!Kung) data and Marshall Sahlins's **"original affluent society"** (*Stone Age Economics*, 1972), and it is genuinely disputed.

- Lee's ~15 hours counted **foraging only** (trip out + food brought back). It **excluded** food processing, tool manufacture/repair, firewood/water hauling, childcare, camp maintenance.
- Adding those in raises total working time **substantially** — by some estimates into the **40+ hour** range, i.e. not obviously below a modern week. Critique: **David Kaplan, "The Darker Side of the Original Affluent Society," *J. Anthropological Research*, 2000.**
- On "only ~40% worked": Lee noted the **young and old were largely exempt**, a large fraction of the band supported by the rest. Keep the *direction* of this (many supported by others' labor) but **do not assert a precise %** — it varies by study/definition.

**The defensible claim to make instead:** foragers spent **little time specifically securing food/shelter** and carried a **near-zero maintenance burden** on few possessions — NOT "they barely worked and moderns are dupes."

**Guardrails when using this in the argument:** (1) the comparison is about *labor hours / maintenance burden*, explicitly NOT health, safety, violence, or overall welfare (don't romanticize); (2) ecovillages/cohousing that recover the same efficiency are **self-selected**, may not scale to a whole society.

**Clean companion cites (well-supported, use freely):** Ruth Schwartz Cowan *More Work for Mother* (1983, labor-saving tech didn't cut housework because work was privatized) — the load-bearing one; Liu et al. *Nature* 2003 (smaller households → higher per-capita resource use); McCamant & Durrett *Cohousing* (1988); Mollison & Holmgren *Permaculture One* (1978); Findhorn footprint (Tinsley & George 2006); Milesi et al. 2005 (US turfgrass ~40M acres); Keynes 1930 (predicted 15-hour week that never arrived).
